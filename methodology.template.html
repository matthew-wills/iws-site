Methodology

Findings should be derived, not declared.

IWS provides structure for moving from evidence, to hypotheses, to tested propositions, to findings, while preserving the reasoning in between.

One investigation record: the checklist, the evidence, the interviews, the timeline, the map, the tests and the report, in one file. The method works on that record from the first notification to the last finding.

From evidence to finding

Five steps, and each one is kept

A finding in IWS is the end of a chain the record holds in full. Each link can be read back later, by the investigator or by whoever reviews the work.

Evidence

Each item recorded once, with its source, its significance and everything that rests on it.

Proposition

An event or condition that might have played a part, placed on the map whether it is expected to hold or to fail.

Testing

Three questions asked in order, with the evidence for and against each answer recorded.

Confidence

Every answer carries a probability, and a pass has to reach the investigation's standard of proof.

Finding

What the object becomes follows from its results, not from the investigator's view of it.
The investigation loop

The work runs in rounds, on the same record all the way round

You are notified, you decide an investigation is required, and the checklist starts with whatever is on hand. From there the work runs in rounds: what has come in is recorded, it is analysed, the gaps it exposes become requests for more information, the picture on the map is built out, the evidence behind each object is tested, and round it goes.

The report is drawn off the loop when the picture holds, rather than at the point in a sequence where a method says it is due. Nothing is thrown away when new information arrives: the record takes it, and the objects it bears on are tested again.

The causal map

Every line of enquiry on one page, in time and in organisational depth

The causal map is an AcciMap-style diagram. Each object is an event or a condition that might have played a part, placed left to right in time and bottom to top in organisational depth, with the critical event at the bottom right where every chain arrives.

The critical event goes down first, because everything else on the map exists to explain it. Then every hypothesis goes up, including the competing ones and the ones the investigator expects to fail. Nothing is filtered at that stage: an excluded hypothesis, with the reason it failed, strengthens the record as much as one that holds.

Arrows are claims that one thing led to another. An arrow stays grey and dashed until the object it comes from has passed its tests, so the picture on the map is never more than the current state of the evidence.

The lanes
  • External influences: standards, oversight and guidance from outside the organisation.
  • Organisational influences: the organisation's own systems, such as hazard identification, change management and risk assessment.
  • Risk controls: the defences that should prevent or catch the occurrence.
  • Local conditions: the conditions on the day, such as weather, workload and fatigue.
  • Individual actions: what people did.
  • Occurrence events: the event sequence itself, converging on the critical event.
The three tests

Nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests

Three questions are asked of every object on the map, in order, and a confidence is recorded for each answer. Where the evidence cuts both ways, the test is argued: the evidence for and against is recorded separately and weighed into a confidence.

Existence

Did this event or condition actually exist?

If it did, the next question is asked. If it did not, the hypothesis is excluded: it stays on the map, struck through, with the reason recorded.

PassFailInconclusive
Influence

Did it influence anything else, and if so in what way?

If it did, it is a contributing safety factor and its outgoing arrows become proven causal paths.

PassFailArgued
Importance

If it did not influence anything, is it still important enough to raise?

A factor that played no part in this occurrence may still be a risk worth addressing. If so, it is a finding in its own right, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows.

PassFail

How a finding is derived

  • Existence fails: an excluded hypothesis. It stays on the map, struck through, with the reason on the record.
  • Existence passes, Influence passes: a contributing safety factor, and its arrows become proven causal paths.
  • Existence passes, Influence fails, Importance passes: an other risk factor, raised as a finding with no arrows.
  • A test left inconclusive: the line of enquiry stays open, its arrow stays grey, and the report has to say that it is open.
Standard of proof

The bar a passed test has to clear is set once, for the whole investigation

Before the tests are worth anything, the investigation has to say how sure it needs to be. IWS asks for that once, as the standard of proof, from the balance of probabilities at 50 per cent up to very likely at 90. The application's default is 66 per cent.

Every test then carries its confidence on the probability scale below, and the object's form warns when a pass falls short of the standard. A finding that only just clears the bar is reported with the confidence it actually has, rather than presented as settled.

Probability and confidence

Ten fixed bands, so confidence means the same thing in every investigation

A confidence word in a report is only useful if every reader takes it the same way. IWS records every confidence and every finding as one of ten bands of a fixed probability scale, so two investigations can be compared, and a reviewer can see exactly how strongly each finding was held.

The prose does not have to repeat the same ten words. Each band accepts a short list of phrases that state that band and nothing else, and the checks read any of them back as the band it belongs to.

A Findings section is held to seven of the ten bands: narrower wording for the sentences that become the investigation's own conclusions. The wording lists can be edited in Settings.

Evidence and Argument Tables

The trail from proposition to finding, written out

The map is the picture. The tables are the working behind it, exported from the same record, one table per object, in the order the map reads.

What each table holds
  • The proposition: the object as it sits on the map.
  • Each test's result and confidence.
  • The evidence recorded for it, and against it where it was argued.
  • The investigator's own reasoning.
  • What the object became: the finding, or the reason it is not one.

None of it is typed twice. It is the same record the map draws from and the report is drafted from, so the tables preserve the evidence, tests and reasoning behind each finding and the investigation can be reviewed later, step by step.
