Overview

The Investigation Workflow Suite maps a safety occurrence as a causal diagram. Every suspected event or condition becomes an object on the canvas, is tested against evidence with the E/I/I framework (Existence, Influence, Importance), and is connected to its consequences with causal arrows.

A finished map reads in two directions at once. Left to right is time: the sequence that led to the occurrence. Bottom to top is organisational depth: from what individuals did, up through the conditions and controls that shaped their actions, to the organisational influences behind those controls. The causal chains converge on the critical event at the bottom right.

The discipline of the method is that nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests. Objects carry their test state visibly, so anyone reading the map can distinguish a proven contributing factor from an untested idea at a glance.

The canvas

The six horizontal lanes are organisational levels. The faint italic text inside each lane suggests the kinds of factors that belong there.

  • External Influences: Influences from outside the organisation: regulators, industry standards, surveillance and guidance.
  • Organisational Influences: The organisation's own systems: hazard identification, change management, risk assessment, training needs analysis, communication, auditing.
  • Risk Controls: The defences that should prevent or catch the occurrence: procedures, warning systems, training, supervision, rosters, infrastructure.
  • Local Conditions: The conditions on the day: weather, workload, fatigue, distractions, health, knowledge and experience.
  • Individual Actions: What people did: handling, planning, inspecting, communicating, documenting, monitoring.
  • Occurrence Events: The event sequence itself, converging on the critical event.

The heavier line above Local Conditions separates the organisational levels (above) from the individual and situational levels (below). Drag any lane boundary to resize it; the lanes and objects below move together, so nothing falls out of its lane.

Objects

An object's colour, shape, and border show exactly where it stands in the testing workflow. The full reference, with the path that leads to each category, is on the Object key tab. The states you will see most:

Two other signals: a dashed border means Existence is not yet proven, and the small corner dot tracks completion - amber on every object still outstanding, green once it is finalised - so an unfinished investigation is obvious at a glance.

The evidence form carries a second panel, Analysis, beside Evidence. Left, read-only: the object's record exactly as the Record tab would render it, so the paragraph on the right is always written against what a reader actually sees if it is left blank. Right, the paragraph itself - on the critical event, labelled Outcome and consequences - with a status badge (not written yet, draft, finalised, or out of date against a record that has since changed), Finalise once you are happy with it, Revert to drafted to undo your edits, and Clear to remove it. Below that, a Safety actions list records what was done, what is proposed, or a recommendation against this object - most relevant to a flagged safety issue, but open on every object. Both save quietly as you work, the same as the suite tabs, and each save is written to disk at once rather than waiting for the autosave timer, because neither is covered by canvas undo: an unrelated Ctrl+Z on the map can jump back past a paragraph or safety action written since, without ever touching it directly.

Running an investigation

The order below is the method, not a suggested reading order. Several steps are only correct if they happen before another one: gather before evidence perishes, build the timeline before the map, settle the interview record before drafting. These are the same phases as the Checklist tab, so the tool and this page describe one method rather than two.

1Notification and triage

Decide whether there is an investigation, and start the record the moment there is.

  • Create the investigation from File, New investigation, and name it for the occurrence rather than the reportFile menuThe name reaches every export and every draft. An occurrence keeps its name; a report is one of several things you will produce about it.
  • Work down the triage questions and answer each Yes, No, or N/AChecklistAn unanswered question is not the same as N/A, and the header count tells you which is which at a glance.
  • Record the decision to investigate, and the reasoning behind it, as your first log entryLogThe log is contemporaneous. An entry written now is evidence of when you knew something; the same entry written in a fortnight is a recollection.
2Initiation and administration

Set the scope and the clock before the work spreads.

  • Write the scope into the notebook: the questions the investigation must answer, and what it will not examineAcciMap notebookScope creep in a safety investigation is usually invisible until the report will not converge, and the half that gets left out is the half nobody wrote down.
  • Set the standard of proof the investigation works to, and the organisation's acronym glossarySettingsThe evidence form warns when a passed test sits below the standard you set, and the glossary is what lets a draft expand an abbreviation instead of guessing at it.
  • List what has to happen and by when, dragging the list into the order you will work itChecklist, to-do list
3Containment

Deal with continued safe operation before investigating why it happened.

  • Decide whether anything must change now to keep operating safely, and log the decision either wayLogA decision that nothing was needed is a finding, and needs the same record as a decision to act.
  • Refer any risk outside the investigation's scope rather than absorbing itChecklistAbsorbing it is how an investigation stops converging.
4Evidence gathering

Get what perishes first, then build the sequence everything else will be argued against.

  • Work the evidence-gathering list and mark each source obtained, N/A, or still outstandingChecklistCCTV is overwritten, recorded data is overwritten, and testing for impairment has a window that closes. This is the only part of an investigation where a day's delay destroys evidence rather than costing time.
  • File each source as an evidence item with a caption, why it matters, and a referenceEvidenceThe reason you kept something is what you forget, and it is the half a draft most needs. A caption says what a thing is; significance says why it is in the investigation.
  • Mark every text extract as verbatim or as your own descriptionEvidenceThe application holds no recordings, so CCTV and audio arrive as text you typed. A verbatim record may be quoted; your description may not, and marking it is what stops a draft putting your words in someone's mouth.
  • Build the sequence of events, giving each one its source and its time basisTimelineDo this before the map. The map is an argument about what led to what, and you cannot argue about a sequence you have not established. Times in mixed bases with no correction recorded are the usual reason a sequence will not hold together.
5Interviews

Get each person's own account, early, and turn it into something the investigation can use.

  • Review the recorded data and the timeline before the interview, and draft your topics as open questionsTimeline, InterviewsAn interview is the one source you cannot re-run cleanly. Asking what the data already answers spends the time you needed for what only this person knows.
  • Work the preparation checklist before each interview: notice, fitness to attend, a support person, somewhere private, and enough timeInterviewsAn account given by someone fatigued, rushed or blindsided is worth less, and interviewing them again does not undo the first attempt.
  • Read the opening protocol at the start, and confirm the interviewee has understood it before you beginInterviewsWhat an interviewee believes the interview is for shapes what they tell you, so leaving it unsaid does not leave it unanswered.
  • Settle the record while it is fresh - the transcript where it was recorded, your notes where it was not - correct it against what was said, and finalise itEvidence, InterviewsReport drafting is told whether an interview's account is settled or still a working draft, and weighs it accordingly. Leave it unfinalised and the drafting reads it as shorthand, which is why an event overview can read thin.
  • File what the interview added as evidence, and add any new events to the timelineEvidence, TimelineAn interview that stays in the Interviews tab argues nothing on the map.
6Analysis

Test hypotheses rather than select them.

  • Place the critical event, then add an object for every event or condition that might have played a partAcciMapDo not filter at this stage. The method works by testing, and a hypothesis you later exclude still strengthens the record.
  • Test each object for Existence, then Influence, then Importance, recording the confidence behind each resultAcciMapThe category comes out of the testing. Choosing the category first and finding evidence for it is the failure this whole method exists to prevent.
  • Where the evidence genuinely cuts both ways, switch the test to Argument mode and record both sidesAcciMapBoth sides then reach the report, so it can resolve the contrary evidence instead of quietly dropping it.
  • Mark a test Inconclusive when it was examined and could not be settled, and say why in the commentAcciMapInconclusive is not the same as untested, and it is not a finding. It must still be discussed in the report, and your comment is what the draft discusses.
  • Connect the causal chains back from the critical eventAcciMapEvery arrow is a claim that one thing led to another, and the map is only as strong as those claims.
  • Finalise each object once its evidence is settledAcciMapThe green dot says the evidence would withstand review. Exports warn about anything left unfinalised.
7Reporting and review

Draft from the investigation, not from memory.

  • Check what each section will be drafted from before generating anythingHelp, What feeds whatA section receives what its style declares and nothing else. If a section is coming out thin, the usual cause is that what it needs has not been recorded yet, not that the model is weak.
  • Generate section by section, read each against the source material, correct it, then finalise itDraft reportA generated section is a proposal. Finalising is where you take ownership of the words, and it is the only signature the tool records.
  • Regenerate any section marked stale rather than keeping itDraft reportA section drafted from material that has since changed is quietly out of date, which is worse than being obviously wrong because it still looks finished.
  • Export the full set and file it with the investigationExportThe map image, the E/I/I tables and the collation together are the working record behind the report, and they are what a reviewer will ask for.
  • Assign findings to owners with timeframes, and close the investigation on the checklistChecklistA finding with no owner is an observation.

Building the map

The Analysis phase above, in detail. This is the part of an investigation the map exists for.

  1. 1Place the critical event

    Use + Critical event and put it in Occurrence Events, conventionally at the bottom right. Open it and record what happened and the proof of its significance. Everything else on the map exists to explain this object.

  2. 2Brainstorm hypotheses

    Add an object (+ Object) for every event or condition that might have played a part, placed in the lane where it belongs and roughly where it sits in time. Do not filter at this stage: the method works by testing, not by intuition, and a hypothesis that is later excluded still strengthens the investigation record.

  3. 3Gather evidence and test Existence

    Double-click an object, record the evidence, and answer the first question: did this actually exist or occur? A pass turns the border solid. A fail excludes the hypothesis, struck through but kept on the map.

  4. 4Answer the risk questions

    For objects that exist, decide whether the event or condition increased risk or decreased risk. The two are mutually exclusive. Increased-risk objects take sharp corners; decreased-risk objects become green positive events.

  5. 5Test Influence

    Did this factor actually influence the occurrence? A pass makes it a blue contributing safety factor and turns its outgoing arrows solid green: a proven causal path. A fail leads to one final question.

  6. 6Test Importance

    A factor that did not influence this occurrence may still be worth addressing. A pass makes it an orange other risk factor, a finding in its own right. Failing both Influence and Importance excludes the object.

  7. 7Connect the causal chains

    Hover an object, drag from one of the ports on its edge, and drop on the object it led to. Build the chains back from the critical event: every arrow is a claim that one thing led to another, and the map is only as strong as those claims.

  8. 8Attach the supporting material

    The evidence form's right-hand panel holds working notes and the material behind them: click the capture zone and paste a screenshot or a manual extract, or drag a file from Explorer and drop it on the zone (PDFs included; the zone lights up as the file comes over it). The Evidence tab's own capture box takes a drop the same way. Each capture takes a short note on why it matters. The app is a tool, not a repository: everything captured is a copy embedded in the .sfm (files to 2 MB, images to 5 MB), so nothing breaks if originals move - and whole documents stay in your organisation's investigation folder, which remains the system of record. Capture the relevant page or passage, not the manual. The form opens at 90 per cent of the window, with that right-hand column taking about a third of it; drag the divider between the two to give whichever side you are working on the room, resize the form from its corner, and both are remembered for the next time you open one. Every field grows as you type. The Captured material sub-tab shows everything held across the investigation in one place, grouped by where it lives, by lane, or by kind, with a jump to wherever each item lives; drag the tabs themselves into whatever order suits how you work.

  9. 9Finalise and review

    When an object's evidence is settled, tick Finalised in its evidence form; the corner dot turns green. Then stand back: does every contributing factor have a proven path to the critical event? Are the excluded hypotheses genuinely closed? Exports will warn about anything left unfinalised.

The workspaces

An open investigation shows a row of tabs, each one workspace of the suite. Everything they hold travels in the one .sfm file. Drag the tabs into whatever order suits how you work - the order is remembered on this machine.

Checklist

A sequential question flow for the whole investigation, answered Yes, No, or N/A (click an answer again to clear it). Guidance notes sit under the questions where a step needs prompting - including an evidence-gathering list (CCTV, recorded data, audio, records of recent change, impairment and fatigue assessment and so on) to help you think of sources before they are overwritten or lost. The header counts how many items have been addressed, and each section collapses to its header (click the title or chevron) so completed sections tuck away. The checklist seeds from the template in Settings when the investigation is created.

Beside it runs the to-do list, which is the other half of the same idea. The checklist asks what an investigation of this kind should cover and is the same every time; a to-do is whatever this one needs next, in your own words, and no template could anticipate most of it: ring someone back on a number, chase the CCTV before it is overwritten, ask the roster office for the prior fortnight. Items stay in the order you added them. Ticking an item writes a line to the Log as a record of what was done and moves the item out of the list above into a collapsed Completed section beneath it, which you can expand to check or untick; unticking moves it straight back. Dropping an item with the cross removes it without logging anything, because it was never done. The list is yours as a working tool; nothing on it reaches a report.

A to-do can also be raised from inside an object's form on the acci-map, just under its notepad, for whatever you need to chase up before you can finish that object. It is the same list as this one, not a separate one: it shows here too, with a from: <object> line you can click to jump straight back to the form that raised it, so what still needs doing is visible at any point, whether you are working the to-do list or the map, and it is easy to hand a job to another investigator.

Below it sits Distribution: who this investigation's outputs go to, as a Title and Name table. Add, remove, and reorder rows freely - it is appended automatically to the foot of every exported report, so it is never left off by hand. Copy as table puts the same rows on the clipboard as a table for pasting into a document the app did not produce, falling back to tab-separated text where a rich clipboard paste is not available. A new investigation starts with whatever rows the edition ships as defaults, if any; existing investigations are never changed.

Log

The running sheet: a timestamped record of investigation activity, displayed as message bubbles grouped by day. Type a line, press Enter, and it is on the record. Entries cannot be edited afterwards (one added in error can be removed), which is what makes the log worth citing. The logbook is included in exports and in the drafting collation as the contemporaneous record.

The application writes to the same sheet as you work. A checklist question answered, a to-do added or ticked, an object created, renamed or removed, a test result recorded, evidence captured or finalised, an interview finalised, a draft landed, an audit run, a report exported, a shared-file conflict resolved: each one appears as its own entry, marked "auto" and shown in a muted bubble. Typing in a text box is never logged, only the change it settles. Use the All / Manual / Automatic filter at the top of the tab to read one kind at a time. An automatic entry cannot be edited or removed, which is the point of it, and both kinds go into the drafting collation and the report's investigation activity appendix, so a reader can follow the investigation's own timeline.

Evidence

Everything the investigation has gathered, under one tab with three sub-tabs: Safety reports, Interviews, and Captured material.

Safety reports

Reports submitted by the people involved: air safety reports, written statements, self-reports. Paste each one in as it was written, with the submitter's role, the date, and the report reference so it stays traceable. Do not tidy the wording; how someone described what happened is itself evidence. These are first-person accounts written close to the event, often before anyone has been interviewed, which makes them some of the best narrative material an investigation has, and they feed report drafting the same way interviews do. They need no writing-up pass of their own: a submitted report is already prose, in the submitter's own words.

Each report is marked as submitted under a confidential or just-culture scheme by default. That flag travels with it into drafting, where the model is told it may use the account to understand what happened and attribute it by role, but must not quote it verbatim or reproduce identifying detail from it. Those protections are the reason people report at all, and a drafted report is a document that gets distributed. Untick it only for a statement given without them.

Interviews

One record per interview. Each starts with a preparation checklist and an opening protocol seeded from the templates in Settings - answered Yes, No, or N/A and collapsible once complete, the same way as the investigator's checklist - then background fields and the boxes you write the interview up in.

An interview here is a record, and the app asks one question about it first: was it recorded?

Recorded - the transcript is the record. Import the Teams .docx or .vtt download, or paste it in. It is what was actually said rather than what you caught and remembered. Correct it where the transcription is wrong, because Teams mishears names, readbacks and technical words, and a record nobody may fix is worse than one they may. Your own notes sit alongside it as your observations.

Not recorded - your notes are the record. Write them up while the interview is fresh. If it turns out there was a recording after all, importing a transcript switches the interview to Recorded.

You write the interview up in three windows. Free recall is what the interviewee told you in their own words, before you asked anything, in the order they told it. Additional questions is what you asked once the free recall was finished, and what they answered. Follow-up questions is what is still open: what to put to this person again, or to check with someone else. Under them is your own notes box, for what you observed rather than what was said. Where the interview was recorded the transcript is the record and the three windows wait behind a button, for your own additions to it.

Finalise interview when the record says what was said. The boxes give way to the finished record, read as one document - who was interviewed and when, then the transcript or the three sections in order, then your notes, with anything you left empty simply not there - and report drafting is told the account is settled rather than still moving. Reopen puts the boxes back, which is the only way to correct a finalised record: the lock is there so an account cannot drift underneath a draft that already quotes it.

Give every speaker in a transcript a role. The names stay in that box: the record and the report see only the roles you set. A speaker you leave unmapped still reaches the record, but as an unidentified speaker rather than by name - dropping their turns would lose evidence, and keeping the name would put it in the report. The mapping is deliberately yours to make: a model that guesses which speaker was the captain can attribute a remark to the wrong person, and nothing downstream would catch it.

Where the interview was run by another investigator, add their notes with Add notes from another investigator rather than pasting them into your own box, so whose account is whose stays clear in the record and in drafting.

Captured material

Evidence pasted into an object's form, or captured from the timeline or directly, is held once: it shows here too, in one grid, grouped by where it lives, by lane, or by kind, so you see what rests on what without leaving the map. The grid runs newest capture first, within each group, so the file you just dropped in is the one you see. Each item is a card to read, not a row of boxes to type in: the capture, its caption, why it matters, its category and reference, and what rests on it, with a jump to its owner. A text capture renders as formatted text rather than raw markdown, so a pasted quote reads as an indented extract and bold wording reads as bold. The cards themselves are read-only: Edit opens the item's own form, at the same 90 per cent of the window as an object's, where the caption and significance have room to be written in and the capture stays in view beside them. The capture is never editable, because it is what was captured. A file is not shown in the app at all: clicking it gives its name and size and an Open button, which writes a copy into the investigation folder's evidence subfolder, beside exports, and opens it in whatever this computer uses for that kind of file. Your own PDF reader beats anything the app could show you. Finalise settles an item and the card stops offering Edit; Reopen is the only way back in. Unlink, in the form, takes the item off the object you opened it from and leaves it here, in this view. Captured material also files by category, shown as sub-tabs. Only categories something is filed under appear; "Show all" reveals the rest greyed out, where they work as a prompt - nothing under CCTV yet, on an occurrence that happened in view of a camera.

The category list itself is edited in Settings - Evidence - Categories: add, rename, reorder, or remove one, or reset back to the built-in list. An item always keeps its category as its own text, so renaming or removing one here only changes what the picker offers for the next item filed, never material already captured, and typing a category that is not on the list still works - it is kept with that item and shown for it.

An image can also go into the report. Its form carries one tick, Include in the report as a figure, off until you set it, and the card then shows a Figure marker so you can see at a glance which captures are headed for the document. Only images get the tick: there is nothing to put on a page for a text extract or a file. A ticked image is numbered in the order the report argues the objects the images belong to, so Figure 1 is the first picture a reader meets, and its caption and reference are what print under it. Write the caption as you want it read, because that is the wording that goes into the report.

The application holds no recordings. Audio, CCTV and recorded data arrive as text somebody typed, so a CCTV item is a time and what happened at it, with a screenshot if it helps. That makes one distinction worth stating on every text extract, and each one carries it: Verbatim for the source's own words - a transcript, a quotation from a manual, the text of a message - and My description for your account of what you watched or heard. A report may quote the first. The second is already your interpretation, so a draft will summarise it and never put it in quotation marks or attribute it as anyone's speech. Anything you have not marked is treated as a description, because putting your words in someone else's mouth is the mistake you cannot defend and a reader cannot detect.

Timeline

An interactive sequence of events, worked in UTC throughout. Add one with + Event for the full form, with the quick-add line (21:43:52 Aircraft departs the sealed surface then Enter, with an optional leading date like 28/08 06:10 …), or by editing the table, which works like a spreadsheet. The time is the truth: correcting one re-sorts the sequence, while dragging a bubble only changes its layout - the stalk keeps pointing at the true tick on the axis. Sources rarely agree, so the event card asks what the recorded time is expressed in: UTC (the default - no correction needed), Local, or Other for a source clock or device time. Local and Other prompt for a signed correction to UTC, like -8:00 for a zone conversion or -0:02:14 for a clock error, and the card shows the calculated UTC time as you work. The corrected effective time drives ordering and every output; corrected events are starred on the axis and chipped in the table. Each event also takes a source (tower log, CCTV camera, FDR, witness statement) so every entry on the timeline is traceable - sources ride into the reports and the drafting collation. Timelines can span days, weeks, or months - day changes are marked on the axis with dated dividers. Create entities (Aircraft, ATC, Tug…) with + Entity; each takes a colour, its events' bubbles match, and a legend explains them - click any legend entry to rename it, recolour it, or delete it (deleting warns how many events use it and leaves those events in place with no entity). Bubbles resize from their corner handle when a title needs the room, and every timeline edit is undoable. An investigation confined to one timezone can work in local time rather than UTC using the clock toggle, which changes the labelling everywhere including the exports; per-event corrections work either way. The spacing toggle switches between Sequence (even spacing, readable, not to scale) and Proportional (true time gaps). The canvas pans and zooms like the map - drag empty canvas to pan, Ctrl+wheel to zoom at the cursor, wheel to travel along the axis, and Fit to frame everything - which is how a proportional view with tight clusters or long quiet gaps stays workable. Double-click a bubble, or press Open in the table, for the event card: detail text - what happened, where the time came from, how firm it is - and the same paste/drop supporting material as any map object. Most events are a point in time, but where an event lasted, enter a duration ("45 s", "2m30s", "1:05:00") or the end time, whichever the source gives you; the card converts between them. It is stored as a length rather than an end time, so correcting the start or its UTC correction moves both ends together, which is what you want when start and end came from the same source. In Proportional spacing a span is drawn as a bar beside the axis, and concurrent spans stack into rows, which is how an overlap becomes visible - the point of recording a duration is usually to show that two things were happening at once. A span far shorter than the timeline's range is drawn at a minimum width so it still registers; zoom in to see its true extent. In Sequence spacing the length appears as text on the tick instead, because there the position is the event's order, not its time, so a bar would imply a scale the layout does not have. A chronology already held in a spreadsheet comes in through Import…: choose a .csv or .xlsx, confirm what each column holds (the app guesses from the headings), and see exactly what will land - including any row it cannot read - before committing. Only the rows are taken; the file itself is not kept. Bubbles with material show a paperclip. Exports include the timeline as an image and as a chronology in the report and the drafting collation, with recorded times and shifts noted so the correction trail survives into the record.

AcciMap

The safety factor map itself, labelled AcciMap in this edition - the canvas, objects, lanes, and arrows described in the sections above.

Record

The investigation as recorded: every section assembled straight from what has been entered, in the order and under the headings of the style you choose. It is not a drafted report, which is a different document written on the Draft report tab. Read it here, check it, and export it. Change the style and the page re-titles and re-orders itself into that style's layout; the line under the header names the layout in force and lists the sections it produces, in order, so the effect of the picker is visible without scrolling the document. Every section renders straight from what is already recorded on the objects it is about: a factor's accepted analysis paragraph, an event's narrative sentence, the derived summary and findings. Where nothing is written yet, the section shows the outline block instead - the evidence, tests, and causal links a paragraph would have to argue from - marked plainly as not yet written, with a link that opens the object and takes you straight to where you write it. A summary at the top counts what is outstanding across the whole report, and it recomputes the moment anything changes. Beside the outline, Record checks lists what the record itself can already tell you is wrong or incomplete - a missing outcome, a factor with no path to the critical event, a recommendation with no safety action, an Existence note worded as a measured fact while its own grading sits below virtually certain, an arrow that points backwards in time (read from the times recorded against the objects themselves where there are any, and only from where they sit on the canvas when there are not), prose that has fallen out of date - grouped by severity, each one a link to the object it names. Nothing here needs a model: this tier is a complete product on its own, and it is also what a whole-report draft in the Draft report tab will start from. Export the record as Wordwrites the outline as it stands to Word and markdown, gaps included, so nothing about the state of the investigation is silently dropped.

Where the page discusses the objects from the map one after another, each object is a card with its title, and its classification, above the card rather than inside it, so it is plain where one object's discussion ends and the next begins. That is how this tab renders the document; the document itself, and everything exported from it, is unchanged.

Also exported, below the record, previews everything else that leaves with it. The E/I/I test tables are there in full, one collapsible table per object under its lane, with Export E/I/I tables as Word beside them: the Existence, Influence and Importance results with the text recorded for each, which is the working paper behind every finding. The AcciMap and timeline images are named there as a placeholder, with no preview and no button yet.

Assembled from the record and Drafted sections, beside the style picker, switch between the two ways of filling this view: the derived outline above, or the Draft report tab's own finalised sections assembled in the style's order, with a marker for any section not yet drafted, not finalised, or out of date. Flip between them to compare the record's own account against what has been drafted from it; Export the record as Word exports whichever is on screen. Check the record runs the same mechanical record check as the Draft report tab, over everything currently drafted for this style, and needs no model.

Groups, beside the style picker, is where the analysis section's cross-factor argument lives - the linking paragraph that says how a set of factors relate to each other and lead towards the critical event, which no single factor's own paragraph can carry. With no groups defined, the analysis falls back to one group per lane, shown in the editor read-only with no linking prose; Propose by lane and Propose by mechanism turn that into real groups you can rename, reorder, and move factors between (a factor left in no group renders last, under "Other safety factors"), each with the same Draft, Finalise, Revert and Clear controls as a factor's own paragraph. Clear groups returns to the lane fallback. Context, and any style section written as prose with no objects behind it, gets the same treatment: click its "Not yet written" gap to open a narrative editor drafted from the section's own guidance and the collation it declares.

Draft report

The report is built here, one section at a time, and it lives in the investigation file rather than only in an exported document. The page runs in the order the work does: Style at the top, then Sections, then Check and export at the foot. Pick a style, then work through its sections: Draft this section, read it, correct it, and Finalise it. The strip in the Sections band is the flow: the style's sections as connected steps, in the order they are written rather than the order they print, each showing where it stands (not drafted, drafted, audited, finalised, or out of date because the record has changed), its word target, and one sentence saying what that section is for. The step marked Next is the first one still to draft and carries the button to start it; clicking any step opens it in the editor below.

The model's draft is a first version built from the record, not the report: the investigator works from that head start, reads it against the record, corrects what is wrong or overstated, and finalises it before anything is published under their own name. The Audit action below is an extra check on top of that reading, not a replacement for it.

What this style produces, an expandable line under the picker, open until the first section is drafted, is the longer version: every section of the chosen style with an explanation of what goes in it and which parts of the record it is drafted from. Both it and the strip are read out of the style itself, so an organisation's own style describes itself here the same way the shipped ones do.

Under the picker itself is the short version: what the chosen style is for, in the style's own words, and a line counting its layout, the number of sections and what they add up to in words, followed by the section names in printed order. Where the app ships a worked example drafted in that style, an Example (PDF) link sits on the same line and opens it in your PDF reader. A style with no example simply has no link.

The style decides the sections, their order, and what each one may be drafted from. These are the styles this edition ships with:

  • Standard investigation. The full investigation report, and the longest of these styles: executive summary, event overview, PEEPO analysis, findings summary and appendices. Written for the safety department and the people who have to act on it.
  • Preliminary investigation update. About three pages for executives while the investigation is still running: what is known, what is still being looked at, and what is already being done. Carries no findings.
  • ATSB-style final report. A full-length public report in the ATSB shape: investigation summary, the occurrence, context, safety analysis, findings, then safety issues and the action taken against them. Written for an industry readership. Modelled on published ATSB reports (CC BY 4.0).
  • NTSB-style probable cause report. A full-length public report in the NTSB shape: factual information, analysis, then conclusions carrying the findings and a single probable cause statement with its contributing factors, followed by recommendations. Modelled on published NTSB reports (US government work, public domain).
  • AAIB-style causal and contributory factors report. A full-length public report in the AAIB shape: synopsis, factual information, analysis, then conclusions listing the findings and splitting causal from contributory factors, followed by safety action and recommendations. Modelled on published AAIB reports (Crown copyright, Open Government Licence v3.0).
  • Executive brief. Three to four pages of plain language for the people who decide: what happened, why it happened, what was found, and what is being done. No factual detail beyond what carries the argument.

Appendices on the export, in the Check and export band beside the export it changes, decides what goes out behind the report itself: an image of the accimap, an image of the timeline, and the E/I/I test tables. Each ticked one is appended after the report body under its own heading, in the Word document and therefore in any PDF made from it. The two images each take a landscape page of their own. The ticks are yours rather than the investigation's, so they stay put from one investigation to the next, and they apply to every report export, this tab's and the Record tab's alike, which is why the same ticks appear on both pages. An appendix with nothing to show, a timeline with no events on it, is left out rather than printed empty, and so is one the style already prints itself: where a style's own body carries the E/I/I tables, that tick is disabled and says "already in this style", rather than the report going out with the same tables twice under two lettering schemes.

Sections are drafted individually for two reasons. You can redraft one section without rerunning the whole report, which on a local model is the difference between a minute and twenty. And nothing regenerates over the top of your edits: a section you have approved is never rewritten. Instead, each section is given the sections already finalised before it, so it follows on from your approved text rather than repeating it. Working in the style's own order therefore gives the best result.

The actions on the whole report sit where they belong in the order of work. Draft whole report heads the section list, with the length target and where drafting starts from on the same line. Everything done to the report once it is drafted is in the Check and export band at the foot: Check, Repair once an audit has run, and Export. Each carries a line saying what it does, and the one action that is next is the only one drawn as the primary button, wherever on the page it sits, so the page always points at the same step the strip marks Next.

With an endpoint that has the context for the whole investigation, Draft whole report, at the head of the section list, does the opposite: the entire record goes to the model in one run (an outline, then every section against the full collation, a cohesion edit, and an adversarial grounding audit that checks every claim in the draft against the source material), so the report is written as one argument rather than assembled from sections drafted apart, fitted to the chosen length target without dropping established facts. The finished report lands in the Whole report area, and each of its sections also lands in the matching editor above as a draft, so you still read, correct and finalise it one section at a time. A section you have already finalised is never overwritten; the run log names any it kept. The audit appears in the audit panel and the record check runs as it does for a section draft, and any claim the audit marks unsupported or contradicted is the model's invention and must be fixed or cut.

Sections are stored as markdown, because that is what the model writes, what the next section receives as context, and what the exporter reads. Edit it directly with the toolbar for headings, bold, italic, and lists, or press Preview to read it rendered as a document. Any bracketed marker, such as [NOT ESTABLISHED], is highlighted in the preview, since those are what must be resolved before a draft goes anywhere.

An argued section such as Safety analysis drafts a few factors at a time rather than as one request - by your approved analysis groups when you have set any, by lane otherwise - and the note under Draft this section says how many passes that is. What Draft and Audit actually do follows the AI access setting in Settings: off, they are hidden and you assemble the record into a document on the Record tab instead; Chat copies a prompt (one per pass, for an argued section) and takes a pasted reply back; Endpoint drafts straight into the section, in the same passes.

Length, beside Draft whole report, is a compact choice between no target, Concise, Standard, Extended and a custom word target, with the words and pages of the chosen one shown beneath it. It applies wherever a length target is asked for, and it is remembered on this machine rather than in the investigation.

Drafting starts from the assembled record: your finalised paragraphs and narratives marked to keep exactly as written, and the record's own outline blocks where nothing is written yet, built the same way the Record tab assembles it. That is the behaviour, not something to turn on. Under Advanced you can start from the collated evidence alone instead, the way drafting worked before the record view existed, which is worth a look only when comparing the two. A drafted section gets a mechanical Record check for free - each factor's category and confidence word checked against what its own test result actually says, and, for a Findings section, its categories checked against the derived classification. It also reads the prose itself: more than a couple of semicolons per thousand words is flagged as a sign the text has not been edited into full sentences, and a Findings bullet that negates its subject ("No assessment was likely recorded") is flagged too, because the term then qualifies what did not happen rather than the absence itself and the finding reads as its opposite - it belongs in front of the whole statement instead ("it is likely that no assessment was recorded"). It runs automatically once an endpoint draft lands (never on a pasted chat reply, which is yours to read before it lands), and Check the record, in the Check group, runs it on demand over everything currently drafted. It is mechanical, not a model opinion, and sits separately from the model audit above it.

A Findings section whose list contains a finding graded at a band that may be stated with no hedge at all (by default, virtually certain) opens with one sentence saying so: "A finding stated without a likelihood term was established as fact." It is there for the reader, so a plain finding among hedged ones reads as a deliberate statement of fact rather than a missing word, and the Record tab, the Word export and the drafted report all carry it in the same place. It is fixed wording: a drafting pass is told to reproduce it exactly, and the record check and the model audit both leave it alone rather than reading it as one of the findings. Where every finding carries a term, the sentence is left out, since there is nothing to explain.

Audit against the record, beside it in the Check group, is the model pass over the assembled sections rather than one of them: it checks the report against the whole investigation, which is the only way to catch two sections that are each defensible on their own and together say different things. On Endpoint it runs straight away; on Chat it copies the prompt and takes the verdicts back. What the audit flags is what Repair against the audit acts on, described under LLM drafting below.

Export report as Word assembles the sections in the style's order into a Word document and a markdown copy. A section that is missing, unapproved, or out of date is still included, but carries a marker saying so, and the cover page records that the report is incomplete. Silently dropping unapproved work, or letting it leave looking finished, would both be worse than a marker you have to delete.

Figures travel with the report. An image you ticked on the Evidence tab goes in after the paragraph that first says "Figure 3", and where the prose never mentions it, at the end of the section that argues the object it belongs to; a capture no object claims closes the document in a Figures appendix. It prints at the text width inside a thin black border, with "Figure 3: your caption" under it. The drafting model is told the figure exists and what it shows, so it can refer to it by number, but whether the argument needs it is left to the draft and to you. In Word the number is a real caption field and every "Figure 3" in the text is a cross reference to it, so Insert Caption, Insert Cross-reference and a table of figures all work on what the app wrote, and adding a figure by hand renumbers the rest. The Record tab's assembled view and the Draft tab's preview show the figures in the same places, so what you read on screen is what the Word file carries.

A drafted section says "Figure 3" and nothing more, and what Figure 3 is gets settled again when the report is exported. So if you tick another image onto a factor the report argues earlier, every number below it shifts and sentences written about one picture now point at another. The record checks say so: a section drafted against a different numbering is reported as out of step, naming what each number now points at, and you redraft the section or correct the numbers yourself. Nothing is silently rewritten, because only you can tell which the sentence meant.

Whole report (smoothed), below the sections, edits your finalised sections together into one document: shared terms and tense, transitions between sections, repetition removed. It never adds, removes, or reweighs a finding, and it never replaces the section texts above it - the same Draft, Finalise, Revert and Clear controls apply, and it exports as its own file so it is never confused with the section-by-section report. It goes out of date the moment a finalised section changes underneath it, the same way any other drafted text does here.

Causal arrows

Arrows carry the causal argument, and their appearance reflects its strength. An arrow stays grey and dashed while its source object's Influence test is unresolved; the moment Influence passes, every arrow leaving that object turns solid green. A map whose chains are green from organisational influences down to the critical event is a completed causal argument. These arrows also decide reading order: wherever a report or the Record lists factors - Analysis, Findings, Issues - it lists each cause before the effects it feeds, following the arrows rather than where a factor first happened to appear on an earlier chain.

Arrows are claims. If you cannot say in one sentence how the source led to the target, the arrow is not ready to draw.

Evidence discipline

Three habits keep an investigation defensible. First, keep excluded hypotheses on the map: what was ruled out, and on what evidence, is as much a part of the record as what was confirmed. Second, record positive events: the defences and decisions that limited the outcome deserve reinforcement, and identifying them is part of the investigation's value. Third, finalise deliberately: the green dot is a statement that the evidence for that object is settled and would withstand review.

Each test also takes a confidence slider, with the percentage translated to a verbal probability ("likely", "virtually certain") so reports use calibrated language rather than bare gut feel. Beside the slider the form states what the position you have chosen reads as: the term, the whole band of percentages that term covers, and whether the value passes the standard of proof, fails it or settles nothing either way. The wording comes from the same table Settings edits, so a band edge or a term changed there shows here too. The standard of proof the investigation works to is set in Settings, in steps of 5 from 50 to 95 per cent: 50 is the civil balance of probabilities, 60 the figure some just-culture frameworks set, and 70, the default, the standard transport safety investigators commonly work to. Each step is named by the standard it is, and says which band of the probability scale it opens or sits inside, read off the scale as it stands, so the standard and the language of the report cannot drift apart even if you move a band edge. The form warns when a passed test sits below it. The threshold works both ways, so it also settles what counts as a fail, at its mirror, and leaves the band between the two inconclusive rather than forcing a choice the evidence does not support. Where the evidence genuinely cuts both ways, switch a test from Conclusive to Argument mode: it then records evidence for and evidence against side by side, and both appear in the outputs, so the report can present and resolve the contrary evidence instead of hiding it.

What is never sent

  • The to-do list. It is your working tool. What is outstanding on it says nothing about whether a line of enquiry is settled, and half of it is logistics.
  • An interview synopsis you have not finalised. Finalising is where you take ownership of the model's words. Before that they are unchecked, and an unchecked generation feeding another generation is how an error arrives in a report wearing the confidence of a fact.
  • A finalised synopsis whose interview record has since changed. It is quietly out of date, which is worse than being obviously wrong, because it still looks finalised. Regenerate and finalise it again.
  • Deleted map objects in the recycle bin. Deciding something is not part of the investigation should remove it from the reasoning, not leave it where a model can still see it.

Where you take ownership

Material does not flow onward on its own. At each of these points you decide, and until you do the application falls back to something weaker and says that it has.

  • Finalising an interview synopsis. Only a finalised, current synopsis is used when a report is drafted. Anything else falls back to your raw notes, labelled as raw notes.
  • Marking an extract verbatim or a description. A verbatim extract may be quoted. A description is your interpretation, so a draft summarises it and never quotes it or attributes it as speech. Unmarked material counts as a description.
  • Testing an object. Categories come from the E/I/I results, never from assumption, and the confidence you recorded travels with each result so a draft cannot state a matter more firmly than you did.
  • Declaring what a report section is drafted from. A section's Needs line names the parts it receives, and it receives nothing else. A section that declares nothing receives everything, so the declaration is the control.
  • Finalising a report section. A generated section is a draft until you finalise it, and a section whose collation has since changed is marked stale rather than silently kept.

LLM drafting

AI access, in Settings, decides how every Draft action in the app reaches a model - the same setting whether the object is a factor's analysis paragraph or the whole report.No AI writes from the outline with nothing leaving the machine at all. Chat copies a complete prompt (the record, the guard rails, the instruction to return only the paragraph) to the clipboard and opens a box to paste the reply back into; a pasted reply lands as written, with no automatic check, because the investigator reads and signs whatever they bring back. Endpoint sends the same prompt straight to the configured model and drafts in place, with cancel. A factor's Analysis panel shows the matching Draft control above its paragraph; the whole-report pipelines below use the same setting.

Each factor's Analysis panel also has an Audit action: it checks the paragraph against that factor's own record - never the whole investigation - and reports verdicts in the same Checked / Supported / Unsupported / Contradicted / Distorted format as the whole-report audit. On Endpoint it runs on the audit model, set in Settings as an endpoint's "fast model" (ideally a different, smaller model from the one that drafted, since a checker from the same family tends to wave through its own confident mistakes); on Chat it copies an audit prompt and takes the verdicts back the same way a draft does. The result is shown inline and is never saved with the investigation - it is a second opinion, checked again by hand, not a certificate.

A paragraph drafted on Endpoint is also swept by a set of mechanical checks the moment it lands: a factor whose Influence test did not pass being written as having contributed, a probability word other than the one recorded against the test, the investigation's own E/I/I notation leaking into report prose, and American spelling. These run with no model at all, so they are instant and always available; a Chat paste skips them, on the view that the investigator reads and signs whatever they bring back themselves. The warnings sit above the paragraph and clear the moment you edit the text or finalise it - they never block Finalise.

An Endpoint is usually a local LLM server (LM Studio, Ollama, or anything else speaking the OpenAI-compatible protocol) configured in Settings, in which case nothing leaves your network. The server needs CORS enabled and a capable model loaded with generous context - 32k tokens at minimum, more for a large investigation. It can run on this machine or on another one on the same private network - Settings has an "Add" preset for LM Studio or Ollama either way. Reaching LM Studio on another machine needs "Serve on local network" and CORS turned on in its Developer tab; reaching Ollama on another machine needs OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0 and OLLAMA_ORIGINS=* set on that machine. Once either is reachable, Settings fetches its model list itself and shows which model is loaded and at what context, so there is nothing to type from memory.

An endpoint can also be hosted: OpenAI's or Anthropic's public API, or an organisation's own gateway in front of one of them. Settings has an Add button for each, and for an Azure or gateway endpoint, so the protocol and address are filled in for you; all you supply is the key. You create the account and generate the key on the provider's own site and pay them directly for what you use. Settings takes the protocol, the header the key travels in, and the key itself - stored on this machine only, the same as the rest of an endpoint's settings, never written into the .sfm and never carried into an export or a run log. The difference that matters is where the prompt goes: whatever an endpoint drafts or audits, the record it is built from - the factor, the section, the whole investigation - is sent to that provider over the internet, the same way it would be typed into a chat window. The Settings pane says so in those words above the buttons. Point AI access at a hosted endpoint only once your organisation has approved investigation content leaving the machine; a local server keeps that content on your network regardless.

Draft whole report runs the same drafting and audit pipeline over the whole investigation in one pass, described under Draft report in The workspaces above.

Once there is an audit, Repair against the audit sends back only the sections something was flagged in, with only the flagged claims to correct, re-audits each one, and shows the result beside the text it would replace. A Findings or summary bullet is judged against the narrower seven-term scale those two sections are held to, not the full ten, so a bullet correctly narrowed to one of those terms is no longer flagged as unsupported, and a summary may also state a finding plainly, with no probability term at all, since the Findings section already carries it. Nothing is written until you land it, section by section: the model proposes and you accept, the same as everywhere else here. A section the pass could not improve is shown too, with the reason, because "the re-audit was no better" is worth reading.

Every drafted text carries where it came from and whether it still matches the record. A badge reads "Drafted by" and the model's name, or "Pasted from chat", and a document brought in from elsewhere says that instead. When the record underneath it changes - a test result, a factor's paragraph, the interview it rested on - the text is marked Out of date and its Finalise button reads "Finalise against the current record" until you have read it again. Only a finalised, current text feeds a later drafting pass, so neither an unreviewed generation nor one the record has moved past can quietly become the input to the next.

Drafting retries automatically on transient server errors, and the dialog shows a live log of each pass. Interviews have no drafting pass of their own: the record is yours to write and finalise, and drafting reads it as it stands.

Files and safety net

The app opens on a start screen: New investigation…, Open existing investigation…, Reopen last where there is one, and underneath them or explore the worked example investigation. The example is a complete investigation - a rejected take-off and runway overrun at Tarlton Springs - with its map tested, a timeline, interviews, evidence and a drafted report, and it is the quickest way to see what a finished one looks like. Everything in it is invented: the operator, the aerodrome, the aircraft and the people were written for teaching and none of it describes a real occurrence. The documents it produced ship with the app too. With the example open, File, Open exports folder shows the standard report, the preliminary update, the executive summary, the E/I/I tables and the collation, in Word and PDF, and anything you export from the example lands there beside them.

Start your own with File, New investigation: name it and choose where it lives, and the app creates a folder there holding the investigation file and an exports subfolder that exports and report drafts write into. The whole investigation - map, checklist, timeline, interviews, evidence captures, logbook - lives in the single .sfm file, compatible in both directions with the original application, so the folder can sit beside your other investigation material (OneDrive included).

Save with Ctrl+S; a dot beside the project name marks unsaved changes, and autosave writes every five minutes once the file has a home. If a save ever fails the app says so plainly - a red notice, not silence. Every edit is undoable with Ctrl+Z, deleted objects are recoverable from the recycle bin, and File, Reopen last restores the previous investigation at the start of the next session; in a browser this takes one permission click.

Investigations on a shared folder

A .sfm on OneDrive or a network drive can be open on two machines at once, and every save writes the whole file, so the last writer would otherwise win without either person noticing. Two guards sit under that. The app notes the file's size and modification time each time it reads or writes it, and if the copy on disk has changed since, the save is held and you choose what happens: take theirs and reload, save yours alongside as a copy, or write over theirs. There is no merge, because a whole investigation file cannot be merged safely, so the choice is deliberate and it is yours. Beside the file the app also keeps a <name>.sfm.lock while the investigation is open, refreshed about every minute and removed on close, so whoever opens it next is told you have it. The lock only warns: a synced folder can delay it by minutes, and a stale one from a crash or a sleeping laptop is ignored, so the size-and-time check is the guard that actually holds.

Templates and settings

Settings holds the default templates as plain, editable text: the investigator's checklist, the interview preparation checklist, the interview opening protocol, the interview and the background fields. Each uses a simple line format - # Section starts a checklist section, - item adds a question or checkbox, > text adds a guidance note or a statement to read aloud, and two leading spaces nest an item; background fields are one entry per line.

Templates seed new content only. Creating an investigation copies the checklist template into its file; adding an interview copies the preparation, opening, and background templates into that interview. From then on the copy belongs to the investigation - it travels in the .sfm, is edited in place, and is never rewritten by later template changes. Template overrides are stored on the machine, not in the investigation, and Reset to built-in returns any template to the edition's default. Settings also holds the analysis standard of proof, the evidence-form guidance toggle, AI access and the LLM endpoints for report drafting, the Licence pane, and a Word template: point it at your organisation's report template (.docx) and every generated document adopts its heading and body styles, so output drops naturally into your document workflow.

Finding terms, under Report drafting, holds the words a Findings section uses: the three group headings, the safety issue tag, and the lane at which a safety factor becomes a safety issue. The pane opens with the rule those words are attached to, so an organisation can see what it is customising. An object that fails Existence, or passes it and then fails both Influence and Importance, is an excluded hypothesis and is reported under the other-findings heading. One that passes Existence and Influence and increased risk is a contributing safety factor. One that passes Existence, fails Influence and passes Importance is an other factor that increased risk. Everything else - a factor that reduced risk, a neutral event or condition, a factor whose Influence test was examined and left unsettled - goes under other findings. The lane then decides the tag: either kind of safety factor sitting in Risk Controls, or in a lane above it, is also a safety issue. It keeps its own heading, carries the tag, and is listed again in the report's Safety issues section; below that lane it is a safety factor and nothing more. The threshold lane is a setting like the words are, so an organisation using its own lane names can move it. Three layers decide each word, and the nearest one wins. The app has a built-in; a report style can declare its own instead, which most do, so different bodies' formats read as they should; and anything you type here overrides both, for every style on this machine. Leave a field empty to let the style decide it, and Reset hands it back. The word you set is used by the findings scaffold, by the report check that reads a drafted Findings section, and in the instruction a drafting model is given, so the three can never disagree.

Probability wording, beside it, holds the words a report may use for each term on the probability scale. The ten terms themselves are fixed, so findings stay comparable between investigations; what you can change is how each one may be said. "Almost certainly" means the same band as "extremely likely", "probably" the same band as "likely", and a drafting model may reach for any phrase you list. Each row starts at the wording in force, so you edit the list in front of you rather than retyping it, and clearing a row hands it back to the built-in. A phrase already accepted under another term is refused, since a phrase meaning two bands would make every check that reads it ambiguous. The report checks read every phrase in use back as its term, so a report can read like English without a single claim changing strength. The confidence slider on an object's tests stops at 5 and 95 per cent as well as the round tens, so "very unlikely" and "extremely likely" can be recorded exactly rather than only reached by typing.

The pane is a table, one row per band. The first column is the lowest percentage the band covers, and you can change it: the app ships with the ATSB's own table from AR-2007-053, and an organisation working to different numbers edits them here. An edge is shared with the band below, so moving one number moves one boundary and the bands can never gap, overlap or swap order; a number outside what its neighbours allow is pulled back to the nearest value that works. The second column reads the band back to you and names the slider steps that can record it, since two bands at the bottom of the scale, 1 to 4 per cent and under 1, have no slider step at all. Reset every band puts the standard table back. A moved edge follows through everywhere at once: the word the object form shows beside a slider, the standard of proof list, the findings scaffold and every check that reads a term from a percentage.

Each phrase in a row has a tick box. Unticking one takes it out of use without losing it, so an organisation that does not want "ruled out" read as a probability can switch it off and switch it back on later; a phrase out of use is offered to no drafting pass and read back by no check. The term's own word has no tick box, because a band with no word for itself leaves a check nothing to read. Type in the field itself to add a phrase or take one off the list altogether.

Each term also has a tick box for a plain statement with no hedge at all. It is on for "virtually certain" only, and should stay that way: where the investigation recorded 100 per cent, writing "the pump was unserviceable" says exactly what the record says, while the same flat sentence against a recorded "likely" claims more than the evidence supports. A drafted paragraph that drops the hedge that way is flagged after the draft lands, alongside the check for a term from the wrong band.

Abbreviations holds your organisation's glossary. Paste it straight in, one term per line. A term on the list may be written out at first use in a drafted report, using exactly the meaning you gave it; anything not on the list is reproduced as the source wrote it and never guessed at, so an unfamiliar code stays as the investigator recorded it rather than becoming a confident mistake. Every drafted report ends with an abbreviations table covering the terms it actually used, built by matching the finished text rather than asked of the model, so it cannot list a term the document never used or invent a meaning. Codes the glossary does not cover are named at the end of the grounding audit, for you to add or write out.

Exporting

Export in the header writes a timestamped folder containing whichever outputs you tick, plus a snapshot of the .sfm file for the record. When the investigation was created with File, New investigation, exports land in its exports subfolder automatically; otherwise the app asks where (or downloads the files in a browser). The outputs come in three groups:

  • Word documents - the investigation report and a summary with the map image embedded, ready for distribution or further editing. The E/I/I tables are also available as Word tables: the test results and supporting text for every object, grouped by lane.
  • Markdown and text - the same report content as plain files, and the report-drafting collation: everything the investigation holds (tested map, evidence, notes, interviews, captured material) structured for a local LLM to draft the report from.
  • Images - the full map as a PNG, rendered with every object in frame regardless of the current zoom, and the timeline as arranged on its tab, with entity colours and legend.

Binary supporting material - object attachments, timeline captures, evidence files - is unpacked into an attachments subfolder of the export, so every file reference in the reports resolves to a real file beside them. Exports warn when objects are not finalised, and every report marks untested material clearly, so a draft sent early cannot be mistaken for a finding. Report drafting with a local LLM is covered in its own section above.

A drafted report comes out as a document meant to be read on paper: a cover page, a contents page after it, and every major section starting on a new page. Word builds the contents itself from the headings, which is why it asks once, as the document opens, whether to update the fields in it. Say yes and the entries and page numbers fill in. That question is Word doing its job, not a fault in the file.

The example reports and PDFs that ship with the app came out of this same path, run against the worked example investigation, so what you see there is what the app writes rather than something tidied up by hand afterwards.

Licence and tiers

What a licence decides is the tier, and the tier is how far AI drafting may go. Standard is the whole application with no AI: the map, evidence, the timeline, the checklist, the to-dos, the E/I/I tables and the assembled report. Chat adds Draft actions that copy a prompt out for you to paste into a chat and take the reply back. Endpoint adds a configured model endpoint, whole-report drafting and the audit passes.

With no licence, this Generic edition build runs at the Standard tier and every output - reports, images, tables - carries a "EVALUATION COPY" watermark. Nothing is ever locked: the app does not stop working, and your investigation files are not touched by any of this. A .sfm written unlicensed opens in any build.

A free 30-day trial is the full application, Endpoint tier and all. Email the copyright holder with your name and email address and you will be sent one; say if you are working through a transport safety investigation course, since a licence can be issued to last it out. There is no trial the app starts by itself, so there is nothing to reset by reinstalling.

A licence arrives as a block of text beginning IWS1.. Open Settings, choose Licence, paste the whole thing into the box - line breaks and all, they do no harm - and press Apply. The pane then shows the tier in force, who the licence names, and when it runs out. It is checked on this machine against a key built into the app; nothing is sent anywhere, and it works with no network at all. If a paste is refused, the pane says which of the problems it is: not a licence, not issued for this application, issued for a different edition, or expired. The app header carries a chip reading either "Standard tier" or "Licensed" with the days left on it; hover it for who the licence names and its expiry date, and click it to open this pane.

Two things can cap AI access, and the lower of the two wins: your licence, and the edition itself. Settings, AI access greys out the choices above the cap and says which of the two is holding it there. When a licence lapses the app drops back to Standard with the watermark; renewing restores it. One thing to know about the clock: if the machine's date is set earlier than the last date the app saw, the licence is treated as expired until the date is put right.

Getting the app

On your own machine, use the installed desktop application. On a machine without administrator rights (a corporate laptop), try these in order:

  1. Standalone exe. Copy investigation-workflow-suite.exe anywhere and run it - nothing is installed. It needs only the WebView2 runtime, standard on Windows 10 and 11. Full native file dialogs and in-place saving.
  2. Per-user installer. The ...x64-setup.exe installer puts the app in your user profile with no admin prompt and adds a Start-menu entry.
  3. Portable bundle. Copy the portable folder to the machine and double-click Start IWS.bat. Python's built-in web server hosts the app and your browser opens it - nothing is installed and the bundle contains no executable, so application-control policies have nothing to block. Open and Save work on real .sfm files in Edge or Chrome; exports arrive as downloads.
The exe options are unsigned, so expect a SmartScreen warning ("More info", then "Run anyway") where policy allows them at all. The portable bundle works wherever Python exists - the same prerequisite the original Tkinter application relied on.
