What it is

One place to run a safety investigation from notification to report

Most investigations are run across a dozen tools: notes in one application, evidence in a folder, interviews in another folder, a diagram drawn by hand, and a report written from memory of all of it. The record and the report drift apart, and the reasoning that connects a piece of evidence to a finding lives only in the investigator's head.

The Investigation Workflow Suite is a single application, and a single file, for the whole of that work. The record is the source of truth: every piece of evidence is filed once and linked to whatever rests on it, every interview is a finalised account, every event sits on a timeline, and every hypothesis is an object on a causal map that must pass its tests before it can be called a finding. The report is a view of the record. Findings are derived from the tests, not asserted, and a drafted report is checked against the evidence before it leaves.

The method is a systemic one. The map uses the AcciMap layout, with organisational levels as lanes, and each factor is tested for Existence, Influence and Importance, with a recorded confidence, so a reader can tell a proven contributing factor from an untested idea at a glance.

It is built for safety investigators and investigation teams in aviation and other high-consequence industries, and for the people learning the discipline. It runs on the investigator's own computer. Nothing is uploaded, no account is needed, and the investigation file can be kept on a secure shared drive while the work runs and archived when it is closed.

How it works

Worked through on a case study

The Tarlton Springs case study, the worked example behind this site, is a fictional rejected take-off and runway overrun at a regional aerodrome. Scroll through it here from the first checklist item to the finished report. Click any picture to see it at full size.

Stage 1: the checklist and the evidence

Start from a checklist that follows your process

The checklist runs from notification and triage through evidence gathering, interviews, analysis and reporting. Each item is answered yes, no or not applicable, and the tab shows how much is still open.

It is a template, not a fixed list. An investigator edits it in Settings to suit how they work; an organisation's own edition ships with its own checklist, so every investigation starts from the same standard.

One to-do list for the whole investigation

Anything that needs chasing goes on a single list. A job raised while filling in an object's form appears here with a link back to that object, so the list can be read in one place and handed to a colleague.

File every piece of material once

Screenshots, extracts and documents go into one register with a caption, its significance, a category and a reference. Each item is linked to whatever rests on it, so when a source turns out to be unreliable it is clear what has to be revisited.

Interviews become records, with people identified by role

Import the Teams transcript, as the .docx or .vtt download, or paste it; correct what the transcription got wrong; add your notes; finalise. From then on the account is settled and report drafting treats it that way.

De-identification is built in. Each speaker in a transcript is mapped to a role, and it is the role, never the name, that reaches the record, the collation, or any drafting model.

Build the sequence of events

Each event carries its source and its time basis, so the sequence can be defended. The timeline exports as an image beside the report.

Stage 2: the causal map

Place the critical event

The occurrence goes in the bottom lane, with what happened and the proof of its significance recorded. Everything else on the map exists to explain this one object.

Put every hypothesis on the map

An object for every event or condition that might have played a part, in the lane it belongs to and roughly where it sits in time. Nothing is filtered at this point: the method works by testing, not by intuition, and a hypothesis that is later excluded still strengthens the record.

Adjust the map live as the evidence comes in

An investigation does not arrive in order. A transcript changes what a factor means, a record arrives that closes a line of enquiry, a new hypothesis appears in week three. Objects are moved, tested, connected and reconnected as that happens, and the map is never more than the current state of the evidence.

Every arrow is a claim that one thing led to another. It stays grey until the object it comes from has passed its tests, and turns green when it has, so the picture builds itself as the work is done.

Stage 3: testing an object

Record the evidence and answer three questions

  • Did this event or condition actually exist?
  • Did it influence anything else, and if so in what way?
  • If it did not influence anything, is it still important enough to raise in the report?

Each answer carries a confidence, recorded as a percentage and read back in the calibrated verbal probability terms the report will use. The standard of proof a factor must reach before it counts as established is set for the investigation, from the balance of probabilities upwards, and the form warns when a pass falls short of it.

Argue the contested ones

When the evidence cuts both ways, record it for and against and let the confidence say how far the argument reaches. The report will carry that confidence word, not a certainty the record does not support.

Stage 4: the finished map and the report

Read the whole investigation in one image

Left to right is time. Bottom to top is organisational depth. Green arrows are the proven chains; a grey dashed arrow is a claim still under test; struck-through objects are the closed lines of enquiry. A green dot marks an object whose investigation is complete, an amber dot one still being investigated.

Assemble or draft the report from the record

Findings and the factor lists come straight from the tests. Choose a style, assemble the report from the record by hand, or draft it with a model and have it checked against the record before it leaves. See AI drafting and Reports below.

The tests

Nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests

Three questions are asked of every object on the map, in order, and a confidence is recorded for each answer. What the object becomes on the map follows from the answers.

Existence

Did this event or condition actually exist?

If it did, the border turns solid and the next question is asked. If it did not, the hypothesis is excluded: it stays on the map, struck through, with the reason recorded.

PassFailInconclusive
Influence

Did it influence anything else, and if so in what way?

If it did, it is a contributing safety factor and its outgoing arrows become proven causal paths. Where the evidence cuts both ways, the test is argued with evidence for and against.

PassFailArgued
Importance

If it did not influence anything, is it still important enough to raise in the report?

A factor that played no part in this occurrence may still be a risk worth addressing. If so, it is a finding in its own right, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows.

PassFail
  • Blue, solid border: a contributing safety factor. It existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange: an other risk factor. It existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through, dashed: an excluded hypothesis. It did not exist, and the reason is on the record.
  • Green, rounded: a positive event, something that reduced the risk or limited the outcome.
  • Red, rounded: the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White, dashed: not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • Green arrow: a proven causal path. The object it comes from has passed Influence.
  • Grey dashed arrow: a claim still under test.
  • Green dot: the object's investigation is complete and its evidence finalised.
  • Amber dot: still being investigated.

AI for drafting the report

Useful, and only under control

A capable language model writes fluent, well-structured prose from a large body of material in minutes, and it will read a record more consistently than a tired investigator at the end of a long week. For a report that is mostly the record retold under headings, that is real value.

The same model, left to itself, will also do things a safety investigation cannot tolerate. It fills gaps in what it was given from its own general knowledge. It rounds "likely" up to "certain" because that reads better. It turns a factor that failed its Influence test into one that "contributed", because that is the shape of the sentence it has seen most often. It invents a recommendation because reports usually have one. None of that is visible in the prose, which is the problem: the errors read exactly like the truths.

So the question is not whether to use a model, but how to use one so that every sentence it writes can be traced to the record and checked against it.

What goes wrong without control
  • 1Facts and figures invented to fill a gap, in the same confident voice as the real ones.
  • 2Confidence overstated: "very likely" becomes "certain", and an argued test becomes a settled fact.
  • 3Evidence reweighed: a factor the investigator excluded is quietly written back in as contributing.
  • 4Recommendations the investigation never made, because reports usually end with some.
  • 5A protected report quoted as if it were an ordinary statement.
  • 6People named where a role was all the report needed.
  • 7Investigation material sent to a service that keeps it, trains on it, or is outside the organisation's control.
  • 8No record of which model, which prompt, or which version of the evidence produced the text.
A chat window

Fluent and unaccountable

  • The investigator pastes what they remember to paste. The model sees a fragment of the record and fills the rest from its own knowledge.
  • The reply lands in the document as written. Every one of the eight failures above is left for the investigator to catch by hand, on every draft.
  • Nothing records what the model saw or which version of the evidence it worked from, so the text cannot be audited later.
  • The material has gone to whichever service was open in the browser, on whatever terms it was offered.
This application

Grounded, checked, and on the record

  • The model is sent the whole record, structured: every object with its tests and confidences, the timeline, the finalised interviews, the evidence and why each item matters. Never a fragment.
  • Grounding rules travel with every prompt: nothing that is not in the record, no invented figures, no recommendation the record does not carry, the confidence word the test actually produced, roles rather than names, protected reports handled as protected.
  • Every draft is audited against the record by a second pass that reports each claim it cannot find, then checked mechanically: each factor's category and confidence word against its test, the findings against the derived classification.
  • Contradictions the model notices in the record itself are reported separately for the investigator to resolve, never smoothed over in the prose.
  • Every draft records which model wrote it and which version of the record it came from. It goes stale visibly when the record changes, and nothing is final until the investigator finalises it.

The drafting pipeline

The application's own pipeline, run the same way whichever model is behind it. Each stage exists to catch one of the failures above.

Connected through an endpoint, on your terms

The application speaks the standard model APIs. Point it at a model running on your own machine, at a hosted API with your own key, or at your organisation's own gateway. Nothing is sent anywhere until an endpoint is chosen, and the Settings pane says so plainly.

Drafted a section at a time, or the whole report

Each section is drafted from the record's own outline for that section, with the sections already approved as context, so the report follows on from what the investigator accepted. A whole-report run is available for models with the context for it.

Audited, then checked

A second pass reads the draft against the record and reports every claim it cannot find. A mechanical check then compares each factor's category and confidence word, and the findings, against what the tests actually say.

Provenance and staleness

Every draft records the model and the version of the record it came from. When the record changes, the drafts built on it are marked out of date rather than quietly kept.

Local

A model running on the investigator's own machine (LM Studio). Nothing leaves the computer.

Hosted, your key

A public API such as Anthropic's or OpenAI's, with a key the investigator holds. The pane states what will be sent before it is sent.

Organisation gateway

An organisation's own secured endpoint, such as a private model deployment, so the material never leaves its control.

Settings

The templates, the proof threshold, and how a model is reached

Settings is where an investigator or an organisation makes the application their own. The templates behind the checklist and the interview protocols are editable. The standard of proof is chosen to suit the framework the investigation answers to: the balance of probabilities at 50 per cent, the 60 per cent some Just Culture frameworks set, "likely" at 66 per cent, "clearly likely" at 75, or "very likely" at 90. And AI access is a choice between none, a copied prompt, or a configured endpoint.
