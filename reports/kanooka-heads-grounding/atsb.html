Investigation summary

What happened

On 07/07/2026, MV Tarrakin Star, a 24.4 m aluminium catamaran passenger ferry certificated for 96 passengers, was inbound on service TF18 through the Merrandi Channel at Kanooka Heads, in darkness on a falling tide, with 38 passengers, a master, a deckhand and a kiosk attendant on board. Times are local time (UTC+10). At 18:07:06 the chart plotter display froze, the heading eased from 195 to 200 degrees without a decision to turn, and the vessel continued at about 8 kt. It crossed the western edge of the dredged channel at about 18:09:00 and made contact with Boolarong Bank at 6.8 kt over the ground at 18:12:04, 16 seconds after the shallow alarm sounded. The vessel came to rest 74 m west of the charted centreline and 54 m outside the dredged channel. There were no serious injuries. The starboard hull bottom plating split in way of the No. 2 void. The vessel refloated at 00:20 on 08/07/2026, and the channel was closed to commercial traffic for 19 hours.

What the investigation found

The defect procedure did not require a fault that had cleared itself to be recorded, so earlier freezes, including two that morning, were never written down and the firmware fix was never applied. It is likely that this contributed to the freeze. The freeze was very likely a contributing factor in the vessel leaving the channel, because the master then had no position fix of any kind.

Service TF18 had been moved into darkness without reopening the route risk assessment that restricted the channel to daylight transits. It was likely that this contributed to there being no night passage plan and no hold point for loss of the primary fix. That absence very likely contributed to the transit continuing at about 8 kt for 114 seconds before the channel edge.

With the plotter frozen, the No. 4 beacon was the only reference left. Mangroves screened it from the channel between 0.84 nm and 0.34 nm north of it, and it was likely that this allowed the heading change to go unnoticed. The deferral of trimming at three successive works rounds, because the islet lay outside the 50 m vegetation scope of the services agreement, very likely led to the screening. The shallow alarm, set to 0.9 m since 2019, gave 16 seconds against a 92 m crash stop distance, and it very likely contributed to the grounding.

Other conditions increased risk without contributing: the aids code's lack of a notice or availability duty for beacons off declared channels, the solar conversion that halved the beacon's nominal range without notice, a roster that put the darkness transit within 20 minutes of the spread limit, and a plotter fault passed on verbally without a defect book entry. The forward saloon lifejacket locker is still being examined. Whether the boat ramp floodlights delayed the sighting of the beacon, which is also still being examined, and whether the master was fatigued could not be determined because the evidence on each is evenly balanced. A steering or propulsion failure and a tide below prediction were very unlikely. The decision not to refloat very likely reduced risk.

What has been done as a result

Work order WO-176 was funded and the canopy trimmed to 2 m on 24/07/2026. Local notice LNM-2026-14 was issued on 22/07/2026, and Tarrakin Ferries reissued the passage plan appendix as issue 3. Two recommendations remain open. [NOT ESTABLISHED: regulator's response and any action on the operator's other two 24 m vessels]

Safety message

A procedure written for one set of conditions needs reopening when the operation moves outside them. Operators should give masters a written rule for losing the primary means of position fixing, and should record faults that clear themselves.

The occurrence

Before the voyage

The outgoing master worked the morning shift on MV Tarrakin Star on 07/07/2026 and handed over to the relieving master at the town wharf at about 13:05. The plotter's internal event log recorded two freezes of the chart plotter display that morning, at 10:42 and 11:17, each with cause code 0x51, and the unit recovered itself on both occasions. The outgoing master told the relieving master about both freezes at the handover, and the relieving master replied that the plotter had done the same to them the month before. Neither entered the fault in the wheelhouse defect book, and no entry of any kind was made for 07/07/2026 before the occurrence.

The outgoing master's account of the freezes differed from the log in timing. In interview the outgoing master placed the first on the 10:20 departure from the town wharf, about twenty minutes in, and the second on the 11:05 return, both in open water outside the Merrandi Channel. Both accounts agree that the display stopped responding and recovered by itself.

The voyage

MV Tarrakin Star is a 24.4 m aluminium catamaran passenger ferry certificated for 96 passengers. At 17:35:02 it departed Muntari Island as service TF18, the last service of the day, and the master reported the departure to Port Control on VHF channel 12 with 38 passengers embarked. A deckhand and a kiosk attendant were also on board with the master. TF18 had been made in darkness on every day it ran since the winter timetable began on 01/06/2026, and this was the thirty-first such transit. Sunset was at 17:02 and civil twilight ended at 17:29. The moon had risen at 06:12 and was 2% illuminated. The wind was from 305 to 310 degrees at 7 to 9 kt, visibility was more than 10 km, and the sea in the channel was slight with wind ripple only. The tide was falling, with a predicted low water of 21:34.

The channel transit

The vessel entered the Merrandi Channel from the north abeam the No. 1 beacon at 18:03:58, 2 m east of the charted centreline, at 8.4 kt on a course of 195 degrees. At 18:04:14 the deckhand, who had offered to stay on the bridge, was told by the master to go and collect fares. This was the normal practice on the route, and no lookout remained on the bridge. At 18:05:30 the vessel was abeam the No. 2 beacon, 1 m west of the centreline and 0.90 nm north of the No. 4 beacon, and the master said "Two ... good."

At 18:07:06 the plotter's event log recorded the display process not responding, this time with no reset. The vessel was 3 m west of the centreline and 0.68 nm north of the No. 4 beacon. The master's account was that the chart picture stayed on the screen with the vessel symbol stationary and the display not accepting touch input. The bridge audio recorded "Oh, come on" at 18:07:11, sounds consistent with repeated screen taps at 18:07:26, and "(unintelligible) frozen again" at 18:07:44. The recorded heading began to increase in the same second as the freeze, from 195 to 200 degrees over about a minute, and was then held. The recorded rate of turn peaked at 6 degrees per minute at 18:07:40 and returned to zero by 18:08:50. Rudder angle followed helm demand within 1.5 degrees throughout. In interview and in the safety report, the master said that the vessel had been eased to starboard by about five degrees while attending to the plotter and then searching for the beacon, and that this was not a decision the master made. The master did not consider stopping, and went to the visual transit because the master regarded it as the backup. Speed was 8.1 kt at 18:08:00, with no throttle movement recorded.

At 18:08:31 the master said "Right. Where's four", with the vessel 12 m west of the centreline and 0.47 nm north of the beacon. At about 18:09:00 the vessel was 20 m west of the centreline, at the western edge of the dredged channel, 0.40 nm north of the beacon, with heading 199 degrees and speed 8.0 kt. At 18:09:40 the audio recorded a sound consistent with the wheelhouse window being slid open, which the master said was done in case the glass was the problem.

At 18:10:02 the master said "There's four. Bring her back." The vessel was then 39 m west of the centreline and 0.25 nm north of the beacon, heading 200 degrees at 7.9 kt, outside the dredged channel. The master said the beacon was dead ahead rather than on the starboard bow where it belonged, and that the master knew immediately that the vessel was off the line to the west. The master said that the vessel was thought to be just outside the edge, by about a boat width, and that stopping on the edge of a bank that could not be seen felt worse than easing back in. The master was in fact nearly 40 m out. The course over ground then eased from 200 to 197 degrees, a turn of about three degrees to port that slowed the westing but did not stop it, and the course remained west of the channel's 195 degrees until contact. Between 18:07 and 18:11 the speed fell from 8.4 kt to 7.6 kt with no throttle movement recorded. At 18:11:00 the vessel was 58 m west of the centreline and over Boolarong Bank.

The grounding

At 18:11:48 the echo sounder's shallow alarm sounded, with the vessel 70 m west of the centreline at 7.1 kt. The alarm was set at 0.9 m under the keel, the value entered at commissioning in 2019. The master said "No no no" at 18:11:50, and both throttles were moved to astern together at 18:11:52. At 18:12:04 the vessel made contact with Boolarong Bank at 6.8 kt over the ground, 74 m west of the charted centreline. The audio recorded a heavy impact followed by grinding and vibration for approximately 9 seconds. The vessel came to rest within 11 m, heeled 4 degrees to starboard, on a falling tide. Furrows in the bed consistent with the starboard hull and skeg ran 11 m from the point of first contact on a heading of 197 degrees. Both engines were stopped at 18:12:31 and were not restarted.

The response

At 18:12:40 the master addressed the passengers, and at 18:13:04 broadcast a pan pan on VHF channel 16. The master reported the vessel aground on Boolarong Bank abeam the No. 4 beacon, 38 passengers and 3 crew, no injuries reported, no pollution, the vessel stable and upright, and "I am not attempting to refloat." Port Control acknowledged at 18:13:29 and called the port workboat. At 18:14:52 the master addressed the passengers again and directed that lifejackets be worn.

At 18:16:20 the deckhand reported water in the No. 2 void of two or three inches on the sounding rod, and the master directed that it be sounded every ten minutes. At 18:17:05 the master reported to Port Control about 75 mm of slow ingress into the No. 2 starboard void, with no ingress elsewhere. At 18:22:40 the deckhand reported that everyone was in a lifejacket, and that two passengers in the forward saloon had been reached by going round the servery because the locker was behind the counter with people standing at it. In the safety report the deckhand described asking three people at the counter to move and passing behind it through a gap to hand jackets out over the counter, which took a couple of minutes. The master's reasons for not refloating, given in interview, were the falling tide, the unknown bottom, 38 people on board, and the nine seconds of grinding.

The workboat came alongside the port quarter at 18:41, and the last of the 38 passengers was ashore at 19:26:08, with the crew of three remaining on board. One passenger reported a grazed shin and two were treated at the wharf for distress. There were no serious injuries. [NOT ESTABLISHED: Port Control's or the operator's record of the injured passengers' details beyond the grazed shin and two cases of distress.] The vessel refloated on the tide at 00:20 on 08/07/2026 and was towed to the town wharf, and it was then withdrawn from service. The Merrandi Channel was closed to commercial traffic for 19 hours.

Damage and post-occurrence examination

The survey of 09/07/2026 to 14/07/2026 found the starboard hull bottom plating set up over 4.1 m with a 180 mm split in way of the No. 2 void. The sounding rod gave approximately 75 mm of ingress at 18:16 and approximately 260 mm when the vessel refloated. All three starboard propeller blades were bent aft and the shaft was bent 1.4 mm. The starboard rudder stock was distorted 3 degrees and the starboard skeg was torn away and recovered from the bank on 08/07/2026. The port hull, propulsion and steering were undamaged. The steering gear was removed and function tested on 12/07/2026, and all of it was serviceable.

The grounding position was surveyed on 08/07/2026 at 74 m west of the charted centreline, 54 m west of the channel's western edge and 34 m west of the No. 4 beacon. The bed at the contact point was 0.50 m below chart datum, rising to 0.15 m below datum 30 m further west, and the bank gradient across the contact section was about 1 in 70.

Context

Vessel

The vessel's beam was 8.2 m. It completed its annual survey on 11/03/2026 with no outstanding conditions, and its last slipping was in 03/2025. Its static draught was 1.35 m, the calculated squat at 6.8 kt was 0.18 m, and the vessel's own trials figure for a crash stop from 8 kt was 92 m. Crew

The master held a master's certificate for sheltered-waters passenger vessels, held since 2012. The master had 14 years as a master, 11 of them on the Muntari Island route and with Tarrakin Ferries, and had made approximately 4,100 transits of the Merrandi Channel. The last route check was on 14/02/2026 and was satisfactory, and the master had no prior occurrence history. Of the 30 darkness transits of the channel before the occurrence, the master had made 26.

The deckhand had been with the operator for 7 months and held a basic marine safety certificate and a coxswain's certificate. This was the deckhand's first winter season on the route.

The outgoing master, morning shift, had been with Tarrakin Ferries for 6 years, 4 of them on the route, and had made approximately 1,400 transits of the channel. The outgoing master had made the other 4 of the 30 darkness transits before the occurrence.

Duty and rest

The route was worked on a cycle of 12 days on and 2 days off, and 07/07/2026 was the master's ninth consecutive day. The duty was split, from 05:40 to 08:40 on another of the operator's vessels and from 13:00 to 19:20 on MV Tarrakin Star. That gave 9 hours 20 minutes of duty across a spread of 13 hours 40 minutes, against the operator's limits of 10 hours of duty and 14 hours of spread. The master recorded 6 hours 15 minutes of sleep on the night of 06/07 in the fatigue diary that the operator asks masters to keep but does not collect.

Five masters worked the route on the same cycle, and no relief master was rostered. A master who reported unfit was covered by extending another master's tour.

The Merrandi Channel

The Merrandi Channel was a dredged channel 40 m wide with a minimum depth of 3.2 m. It was not a declared shipping channel. It lay on a waterway managed by Kanooka Shire Council through the Kanooka Heads Port Authority, and it had six beacons. Boolarong Bank lay to the west of the channel, dried in places, and shoaled at about 1 in 70 across the section where the vessel grounded. The route risk assessment recorded a single unlit reach between the No. 2 and No. 4 beacons and a tidal range of 1.4 m.

Tide and weather

Predicted high water was at 15:20 (1.66 m above chart datum) and predicted low water at 21:34 (0.30 m). The Port Authority gauge read 0.91 m at 18:12 against a predicted 0.94 m. At 18:00 the wind was from 305 degrees at 8 kt with visibility over 10 km, and no rain had fallen since 09:00. The port workboat reported a slight sea state in the channel at 18:45, with wind ripple only. The moon was a waning crescent.

The No. 4 beacon

The No. 4 beacon showed Fl G 2.5s from a lantern 4.2 m above chart datum. It was converted from a mains supply to a solar lantern in 11/2025. The replacement lantern was rated at a nominal range of 3 nm against 6 nm for the lantern it replaced, and a measurement on 18/07/2026 gave a luminous range of 2.6 nm.

Foreshore lighting

The Kanooka Heads foreshore redevelopment was commissioned on 22/05/2026. It included 14 LED floodlights on 12 m columns lighting the new boat ramp and car park, 300 m east of the town wharf. A survey on 18/07/2026 measured each floodlight at about 4,800 candela toward the channel, with none shielded to seaward. The survey described a condition and did not measure any effect on the detection of the beacon.

Passage planning and operating documents

Operations Manual OPS-3 revision 6 (02/2023) required a written passage plan for each scheduled route, and the master was to conduct the passage in accordance with it. Section 3.4 required the Merrandi Channel transit to be made "on the charted centreline, monitored on the chart plotter, with the No. 2 and No. 4 beacons identified visually before the vessel passes the No. 1 beacon". Section 3.6 required a reduction of speed and consideration of anchoring or returning to open water in reduced visibility.

Appendix A, issue 2 (02/2023), was the current Merrandi Channel passage plan. It gave a transit speed of 8 knots and a course of 195 degrees true inbound. The primary means of position fixing was the chart plotter, and the secondary means was the visual transit of the No. 4 beacon and the town wharf leading lights. It recorded the No. 4 beacon at 6 M and noted that the plan was "written for the daylight transit" and that "Night transits are not scheduled on this route."

Operations Manual OPS-9 revision 4 (08/2024) required, at 9.1, immediate reporting of a defect that rendered the vessel unseaworthy or affected a statutory item of equipment. Section 9.2 required all other defects to be entered in the vessel defect book at the end of the shift in which they were found, for weekly review by the Operations Manager. Section 9.3 required the outgoing master to brief the relieving master on the state of the vessel and any defect. The chart plotter was not a statutory item on this route, although it was the primary means of position fixing named in the passage plan.

Route Risk Assessment RRA-07 issue 2 (03/2023) carried the operating restriction "daylight transits only" and required the General Manager Operations' approval and a specific passage plan for a night transit. Change record CHG-2026-014, approved by the Operations Manager on 22/04/2026, moved service TF18 from 16:15 to 17:35 from 01/06/2026. TF18 did not run on Sundays or public holidays, and the occurrence transit was its thirty-first in darkness.

Aids to navigation requirements

Section 4.1 of the Waterways Aids to Navigation Code WAN-2 required the responsible authority to maintain an aid so that it performed the function for which it was established, and it had no channel threshold. Section 4.2 set an availability target of 99.0 per cent, a duty to record availability and a duty to notify changes of character, position or range by notice to mariners, but only for aids on a declared shipping channel. Section 4.3 applied to other waterways and required only that the responsible authority could demonstrate inspection at intervals it had determined and recorded.

Schedule 2 of the services agreement between the Council and the Port Authority provided for a quarterly daylight inspection and a monthly confirmation of each lit aid's operation from the town wharf (S2.1). It treated vegetation management within 50 m of an aid as a works item funded from the general waterways maintenance vote and placed vegetation beyond 50 m outside its scope (S2.4). It contained no requirement to notify waterway users of a defect, a deferral or a change to an aid (S2.7).

Recorded data

Four sources of recorded data were available: the shore AIS receiver log AIS-2026-0093-R1 with the vessel's engine monitoring recorder and steering data, the bridge camera and VHF audio AUD-2026-0093, the chart plotter event log, and the echo sounder alarm log. The plotter was a 12-inch multifunction display fed by a separate GPS antenna unit, running chart software revision 4.11 on firmware issued 03/2024.

Safety analysis

The analysis credits what worked first and then deals with two hypotheses that were tested and not supported. It then follows the occurrence in the order it developed: the loss of the chart plotter and how it was handled, the No. 4 beacon, the organisational and external conditions behind both, and last the vessel's final defence.

What worked

Several things worked on the night, and the most significant was the response after the grounding. Both engines were stopped 27 seconds after contact and were not restarted. The master addressed the passengers within 36 seconds of the impact and again at 18:14:52. The pan pan at 18:13:04 stated "I am not attempting to refloat" as a decision already made. The master directed the No. 2 starboard void to be sounded and reported about 75 mm of ingress to Port Control at 18:17:05. All 38 passengers were in lifejackets by about 18:22 and ashore by 19:26.

The survey later confirmed a 180 mm split in the starboard hull plating in way of that void. A vessel holed in the compartment it was sitting on, backed off into 3.2 m of water on a falling tide with the ingress unassessed, would have carried a flooding compartment into deep water with the passengers still aboard. The master gave the same reasoning in interview: falling tide, unknown bottom, 38 people aboard, and a vessel that cannot sink while it is on the bank. The decision not to refloat very likely reduced the risk to those on board.

Two other controls worked as they were designed. Helm work held the vessel on the charted centreline to within 3 m for the first two minutes in the channel. The shallow alarm sounded at the setting it carried, and both throttles were moved astern 4 seconds later.

Hypotheses considered and not supported

A steering or propulsion failure before the vessel left the centreline was very unlikely. A five degree heading change followed by a steady set out of the channel can look like a jammed rudder or a lost engine, but the recorded data did not support that. The engines ran at 1,780 and 1,784 rpm, within 12 rpm of each other, from 18:03 to 18:11:52 with no alarm and no throttle movement. Steering gear hydraulic pressure held at 96 to 99 bar against a normal band of 90 to 105 bar, and rudder angle followed helm demand within 1.5 degrees throughout. The rate of turn peaked at 6 degrees per minute and returned to zero, which is a heading applied and held rather than a sheer. The steering system was also removed and function tested on 12/07/2026, and every component was serviceable with no sign of a pre-existing defect. The starboard shaft, propeller and rudder damage dated from the contact. The bridge audio contained no steering or engine remark at any point. The hypothesis was considered and not found to be contributing.

A tide set down below prediction was also very unlikely. The Port Authority gauge read 0.91 m at 18:12 against a predicted 0.94 m, a difference of 0.03 m, and the gauge's largest departure from the table in the twelve hours to 20:00 was 0.07 m. With the bed at the contact point 0.50 m below chart datum there was 1.41 m of water over it, against a static draught of 1.35 m and a calculated squat of 0.18 m at 6.8 kt. At the predicted height there would have been 1.44 m against an effective draught of 1.53 m, so the vessel would have touched at the predicted tide as well. The vessel touched because it was 54 m outside a channel dredged to 3.2 m, not because the water was lower than predicted. This hypothesis was likewise considered and not found to be contributing.

The loss of the plotter and what followed

The plotter freeze removed the primary means of position fixing. It led in the same second to an unplanned heading change, and, because no written control told the master what to do, to a transit that continued at speed. Together these took the vessel out of the channel.

The plotter's internal event log recorded the display process not responding at 18:07:06 with no reset. The bridge audio matched to the second, with "Oh, come on" at 18:07:11, repeated screen taps at 18:07:26 and "(unintelligible) frozen again" at 18:07:44. The master described the chart picture remaining on the screen with the vessel symbol stationary and the display not accepting touch input. The same cause code, 0x51, had been logged at 10:42 and 11:17 that morning, when the unit recovered itself. The unit was bench tested for 46 hours on 21/07/2026 with the same antenna and cabling, and the fault did not recur and no defect was found. The contractor identified the code as known to the manufacturer and addressed by firmware issued in 11/2024, which had never been applied to this unit. What caused the freeze, beyond that association with the unapplied firmware, could not be determined because the fault could not be reproduced. The vessel held the centreline while the plotter worked, and the master's search for the beacon began 85 seconds after the freeze with no position fix of any kind in between. It is extremely likely that the freeze contributed to the vessel leaving the channel, because everything the master did afterwards was done without the means of fixing the vessel's position that the passage plan named as primary.

From 18:07:06 the heading increased steadily from 195 to 200 degrees over about a minute and was then held. The recorded rate of turn peaked at 6 degrees per minute at 18:07:40, while the master was at the frozen screen, and was back to zero by 18:08:50. Rudder angle followed helm demand within 1.5 degrees, so the turn was steered. The wind was offshore at 8 kt and there was no cross-set in the channel at that state of tide, so no current or wind effect accounts for it. In interview the master said, "I eased her to starboard while I was fiddling with the plotter and then looking for four. Five degrees. I never made that decision." Nothing on the bridge showed the new heading, because the plotter's vessel symbol was frozen and the beacon was screened. Held at 200 degrees against the channel's 195, the heading moved the vessel west at about 20 m a minute, and it reached the channel's western edge by about 18:09:00. The heading change was the action that took the vessel out of the channel, and it was virtually certain that it caused the westing.

The vessel then continued at speed. The AIS track recorded 8.4 kt at the freeze and 8.1 kt at 18:08:00. The fall to 7.1 kt by 18:11:48 is accounted for by shoaling drag, and the engine recorder showed no throttle movement until 18:11:52. The master's attention moved to the search for the beacon at 18:08:31, and the audio contains no consideration of stopping. At 18:10:02 the master saw the beacon dead ahead and knew the vessel was off the line to the west. The master brought the vessel back to port to regain the channel rather than stopping, believing it to be just outside the edge when it was 39 m west of the centreline. The course over ground came back from 200 to 197 degrees, which was still west of the channel's 195. There was no other traffic in the channel, the water was flat, and the vessel could have been stopped in 92 m. The vessel crossed the western edge 114 seconds after the freeze, so a crash stop begun at any point before about 18:11:40 would have stopped it short of the contact point. That included the 98 seconds after the master knew the vessel was off the line.

The master's account in interview and in the safety report was that stopping was not considered when the plotter froze, that the visual transit was taken to be the backup, and that after the sighting, stopping on the edge of a bank that could not be seen felt worse than easing back. The master also said that stopping in that channel on a flat night with no other traffic was reasonable. The outgoing master, asked what would have been done had the plotter frozen inside the channel, said, "I'd have taken the way off her and sat there until it came back or until I was happy on the marks. There's no traffic. There's nothing to hit if you're stopped." The outgoing master was not aware of any rule requiring that. OPS-3 3.6 required a reduction of speed and consideration of anchoring only "in reduced visibility", which this was not. No lookout was on the bridge after the deckhand was sent to collect fares at 18:04:14, which was the normal practice on the route and which OPS-3 does not address either way. Continuing at eight knots with no means of fixing the vessel's position is extremely likely to have been what turned the loss of the plotter into a grounding, because stopping was available for nearly the whole period and cost nothing.

The No. 4 beacon

With the plotter frozen, the beacon was the only reference left, and it was screened over the stretch that mattered. That kept the westing from being seen while the new heading was held, and so it allowed the vessel to leave the channel.

The range and visibility check of 18/07/2026, made from the port workboat on the inbound track at the same time of night, found that the No. 4 beacon was not visible from the channel between 0.84 nm and 0.34 nm north of it. The sightline over that stretch passes through the mangrove canopy on Merrandi Islet, about 240 m north of the beacon and about 10 m across. The canopy measured 5.8 m against a lantern height of 4.2 m. The master began looking for the beacon at 18:08:31 when the vessel was 0.47 nm north of it, crossed the channel's western edge at about 0.40 nm, inside the screened arc, and called the beacon at 18:10:02 at 0.25 nm, 91 seconds after beginning. Seen on the starboard bow where it belongs, the light would likely have shown the vessel drawing west while it was still in the channel.

Two points weigh against this. The heading change began at 18:07:06, before the search started, so the screening cannot have caused the turn, only its going unnoticed. The light was also clear of the islet for at least the last 37 seconds of the search, from about 18:09:25, and for longer from the vessel's own track west of the inbound one, so for part of the search there was something to see and it was not seen. Neither point removes the screening of the first part of the search, when the vessel crossed the channel edge. The screening of the beacon was likely a condition that allowed the heading change to go unnoticed.

Floodlights behind the beacon: an unresolved matter

Whether the boat ramp floodlights delayed the sighting in the last part of the search is still being examined, and it could not be settled on the evidence. The survey of 18/07/2026 found 14 unshielded LED floodlights on 12 m columns at the new boat ramp, each presenting about 4,800 candela toward the channel, commissioned on 22/05/2026. From 0.5 nm north of the beacon the array lies behind it within 4 degrees of the same bearing and within 1.5 degrees of the same elevation. It is continuously lit while the beacon flashes green for 0.4 seconds every 2.5 seconds. The Port Authority's aids to navigation officer was not consulted and no assessment against the channel's aids was required or made. These facts are not in dispute.

On whether the floodlights delayed the sighting, the accounts point one way. The master described looking "into a wall of light" and called the beacon "a nothing" against the town and the car park, and the outgoing master independently described it as "washed out altogether from up the top". For at least the last 37 seconds before the master called the beacon it was clear of the islet and not seen, which is the stretch in which the floodlights, rather than the canopy, stood between the master and the light.

The evidence on the other side is as strong. Over most of the search the canopy screened the beacon, which would not have been visible against a black background either. The heading change began before the search, so the floodlights cannot have caused the turn. No measurement exists of what could be seen on the night, because the survey was made eleven days later, and nothing establishes a detection threshold for a 0.4 second green flash against that background. The master also said twice that they could not tell whether the lights were why the beacon could not be seen. The matter could not be resolved because there is no measurement from the night and no detection threshold, and the evidence is evenly balanced on whether the floodlights contributed.

Fatigue of the master: an unresolved matter

Whether the master was fatigued during the transit could not be determined either way, and no detailed fatigue assessment was made. The master was on day nine of a twelve-day tour, on a split duty of 05:40 to 08:40 and 13:00 to 19:20, with the channel transit in the last hour and a quarter of a 13 hour 40 minute spread. The fatigue diary recorded 6 hours 15 minutes of sleep the night before. In interview the master said, "I was tired. I'd be lying if I said otherwise." After seeing the beacon dead ahead, the master took the vessel to be just outside the edge when it was nearly 40 m west of the centreline, which is the kind of misjudgement fatigue is expected to produce.

Against that, in the same answer the master said, "I wasn't fighting to stay awake and I wasn't slow," and the recorded performance agrees. Helm work held the centreline to within 3 m for the first two minutes. The response to the shallow alarm was 4 seconds. The radio work after the grounding was complete and correct at the first attempt, and the decision not to refloat was made in about thirty seconds. Six and a quarter hours of sleep is what the master said is normal on a split, and nothing else bears on the master's state. Even if fatigue were established, every decision that mattered on the night is either answered by another factor, such as the absence of a night plan or hold point, or contradicted by the recorded data, so it would not change the standing of the other factors. The matter remains unresolved.

Why there was no plan for losing the plotter

The absence of a night passage plan and of any hold point was what left the loss of the plotter to the master's own judgement. The decision to move the service into darkness without reopening the assessment explains why that plan did not exist.

OPS-3 3.4 required the transit to be made on the charted centreline, monitored on the chart plotter, with the No. 2 and No. 4 beacons identified before passing No. 1. Appendix A, issue 2 and current, named the plotter as the primary means of position fixing and the visual transit of the No. 4 beacon with the town wharf leading lights as the secondary. It closed with the note that the plan was written for the daylight transit and that night transits were not scheduled. There was no night passage plan, no guidance on what to do if the plotter was lost during the transit, and no abort or hold point. The leading lights bore 195 degrees but lay about 1.9 nm beyond the No. 6 beacon and did not resolve as two lights until the vessel was inside the channel's southern third. At the point in the transit where the plotter was lost, the beacon was therefore the whole of the secondary means. OPS-3 3.6 applied only in reduced visibility. Both masters interviewed said stopping was the right answer and that nothing asks for it, and the master said "There's nothing that tells you to stop. There's nothing that tells you anything." A hold point would have applied throughout the 114 seconds before the channel edge, on flat water with no other traffic. A master who did not think of stopping unprompted might not have applied a written rule under the same pressure. Even so, the absence of a night plan and hold point very likely contributed to the transit continuing at eight knots after the plotter froze.

RRA-07 issue 2 (03/2023) carried the operating restriction "daylight transits only" and stated that a night transit required the General Manager Operations' approval and a specific passage plan. CHG-2026-014, approved by the Operations Manager on 22/04/2026, moved service TF18 from 16:15 to 17:35 from 01/06/2026. It assessed the change as "timetable change only", recorded "No new hazard identified" and answered "No" to route risk assessment review required. The daylight restriction was not referenced in the change record, RRA-07 was not reopened and no issue 3 exists. No night passage plan was written, and the General Manager Operations' approval was neither sought nor given. The change was approved one level below the person whose approval the restriction names. TF18 ran 31 times in darkness between 01/06/2026 and the occurrence, which was the thirty-first. The master said that the timetable change brought a new roster sheet and "No brief, no plan, no conversation."

A review of RRA-07 against a night operation would have reached the unlit reach between the No. 2 and No. 4 beacons, which the assessment already named as a hazard, and could not have closed without a night passage plan. A night plan written for this channel is also the document most likely to say what to do when the primary fix is lost. However, a reopened assessment might have produced a plan that said no more than the daylight one, with no hold point in it. Why the change was assessed as a timetable change only is [NOT ESTABLISHED: why the change was assessed as a timetable change only]. It is likely that moving the last service into darkness without reopening the assessment contributed to the absence of a night plan and hold point, because the restriction named the plan and a review that read it could not have ended without one.

Why the plotter fault had not been dealt with

The defect procedure allowed a fault that cleared itself to go unrecorded, so the plotter fault that froze the display at 18:07 had not been put in front of anyone who could act on it.

OPS-9 9.1 required immediate reporting only of a defect that rendered the vessel unseaworthy or affected a statutory item. The chart plotter was not a statutory item on this vessel on this route, although it was the primary means of position fixing named in the passage plan. Paragraph 9.2 put all other defects in the defect book "at the end of the shift in which they are found", which for a fault that had come and gone by then provided no trigger, and the manual did not say whether such a fault was a defect. Paragraph 9.3 required the outgoing master to brief the relieving master on any defect, but nothing required that brief to be written down. Both masters described the same reading independently, in the master's words that "The book is for things that are broken when you hand over. If it's broken you write it, if it came good you say it." The book's eleven entries in the 90 days before the occurrence were all faults present when they were written, and none concerned the plotter.

On the outgoing master's account the plotter froze three or four times on this vessel in the year before the occurrence, it froze on the relieving master a month earlier, and it froze twice on the morning of 07/07/2026. None was written down. The maintenance system held no work order, fault report or correspondence about the plotter in 2026, and the operator held no subscription to the manufacturer's bulletins. Recorded freezes would have reached the Operations Manager's weekly review and the maintenance system, and from there the contractor, who identified cause code 0x51 against the 11/2024 firmware in one bench test. The chain from a written entry to an applied fix, however, ran through somebody ashore finding the manufacturer's bulletin. A recorded freeze might have been reviewed at the end of the week, noted and left, and nothing establishes how many recorded freezes it would have taken for the unit to be sent to the contractor, or how quickly. Why the firmware bulletin was not obtained, beyond the absence of a subscription, is [NOT ESTABLISHED: why the firmware bulletin was not obtained, beyond the absence of a subscription]. A year of freezes on the vessel's primary means of position fixing is unlikely to have gone to nobody if the procedure had required them to be recorded, so the defect procedure likely contributed to the plotter fault remaining unaddressed.

The handover at about 13:05 was an instance of this gap and not a separate cause. The outgoing master told the relieving master about both morning freezes, and the relieving master replied that it had done the same the month before. Neither made an entry, and both volunteered the omission before being asked. An entry at 13:05 would have been reviewed by the Operations Manager at the end of the week, and nothing written that afternoon could have applied firmware to the unit or taken the vessel off TF18 by 18:07. The omission at the handover was therefore unlikely to have contributed to the occurrence. It nonetheless increased risk, because it showed the practice and not a lapse by two individuals: both masters made the same omission and described the same rule for it, so the next fault passed on by word of mouth may be one that an entry on the day would catch.

Why the beacon was screened

The deferral of the work to clear the beacon's sightline was what allowed the screening to exist on the night. The Authority's own records showed that the screening was known, and the funding arrangements show why it was not acted on.

Work order WO-118, raised in 09/2023, recorded mangrove regrowth on Merrandi Islet screening the No. 4 beacon from the northern approach, recommended trimming to 2 m on a three-yearly cycle, and estimated $18,500. It was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 works rounds, each entry reading "defer, unfunded, refer next round". WO-176, raised in 03/2026, recorded the same sightline as "now fully closed on the northern approach". The Authority's file note against WO-176 recorded that the islet was 240 m from the beacon and so fell outside the 50 m scope of schedule 2.4 of the services agreement. The work therefore competed for the general waterways vote rather than the aids to navigation vote. The canopy grew from 2.1 m in 2019 to 5.8 m on 18/07/2026, about half a metre a year. A trim funded in 2023 would have left it near 3.5 m on the night, under the 4.2 m lantern, and the beacon open to the channel over the stretch where it was screened. The 2023 figure rests on two measurements and the work order's description, so growth may not have been even across the seven years. It is very likely that the deferral led to the screening.

Nothing in the Authority's checking would have shown the screening. The monthly confirmation under S2.1 was made from the town wharf, 2.1 nm south of the beacon, from which the islet was not in the line of sight. The beacon was recorded as "observed, operating" in every month from 01/2024 to 07/2026, and no defect has ever been recorded against it. Nothing in the services agreement required an aid to be observed from the approach a vessel uses. The master said the beacon had been getting harder to see for two years and that this was said among the masters but never written down. The outgoing master gave the same account and asked, "Who to? It's the council's light. There's no form." The operator's reporting system has a "navigation hazard" category that was not used on this route in three years, and no master interviewed knew it was there. The Authority holds no record of any operator asking about the channel's aids, and the register had never been provided to the operator.

Conditions that increased risk without contributing to this occurrence

Four further conditions were examined and each was found unlikely to have contributed, although each increased risk.

The aids code applied its availability, recording and notice duties in WAN-2 section 4.2 only to aids on a declared shipping channel. The Merrandi Channel was not one, so section 4.3 applied and required only that the responsible authority could demonstrate inspections at intervals it had set itself. Had 4.2 applied, the range change of 11/2025 would have gone out as a notice to mariners and the Authority would have recorded availability. Neither reaches this occurrence. A notice of the range change would not have made a screened light visible, and an availability figure measures whether a light is lit, which this one was throughout. Section 4.1 applies to every aid whatever the channel's status, the register had recorded the screening since 09/2023, and what kept WO-118 unfunded was the 50 m scope of S2.4. The code was therefore unlikely to have contributed. It remains likely to have increased risk, because on a channel carrying a 96-passenger ferry in darkness it gave the operator no right to be told when a beacon's range, character or position changed.

The solar conversion in 11/2025 halved the beacon's nominal range from 6 nm to 3 nm, and the character did not change. A notification was lodged with the hydrographic service on 04/02/2026 and the chart correction was still pending on 07/07/2026. No local notice was issued, and the operator's passage plan appendix still recorded the beacon at 6 M. The investigator measured a luminous range of 2.6 nm on 18/07/2026. The master called the beacon "a nothing" and said "It used to be a proper light," but the master already knew it was weaker, so a notice would have told the master nothing new. A screened light is not made visible by being brighter, and the search ran from 0.47 nm to 0.25 nm, well inside even the measured 2.6 nm. Nothing shows the plan's 6 M figure was used for anything on the night. The conversion was unlikely to have contributed, and it is likely to have increased risk because a master working to the plan is looking for something twice as strong as what is there.

The roster put the master through a darkness transit at the end of a split duty, 9 hours 20 minutes of duty across a 13 hour 40 minute spread against the operator's limits of 10 hours and 14 hours, so the transit fell within 20 minutes of the spread limit. The same split is worked on every day of the tour, no relief master is rostered, and a master who reports unfit is covered by extending another master's tour. The operator asks masters to keep a fatigue diary and does not collect it. The roster could reach this occurrence only through the master's fatigue, which could not be established, and the recorded performance gave no sign of it. The roster was therefore unlikely to have contributed, and it is likely to have increased risk, because the exposure recurs on every darkness transit of the timetable.

The forward saloon lifejacket locker is a matter still being examined. The locker held 40 adult jackets behind the kiosk servery counter. The survey of 16/07/2026 found that the locker face could not be reached from 22 of the 34 forward saloon seats without passing behind the counter through a 0.6 m gap, and that with people at the counter the gap was the only route. The muster placard directed passengers to "the locker forward on the port side", which was not visible from the seats. The arrangement approved at build in 2011 had an open bench in that position. The counter was fitted in a 2019 interior refit under a minor modification notice that did not trigger a re-survey of the safety equipment arrangement. The deckhand had to ask three passengers to move and pass behind the counter to hand jackets out. The vessel grounded with the locker where it was and would have grounded with it anywhere, and all 38 passengers were in lifejackets within about eight minutes and ashore within 74, so the locker was unlikely to have contributed to the grounding or its outcome. It very likely increased risk, because in a fire, a capsize risk or an evacuation into the water the passengers could not equip themselves without crew help. The operator says the same counter is fitted to its two other 24 m vessels, but whether the same arrangement exists across the fleet is [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether the same arrangement is fleet-wide, awaiting the operator's and the regulator's answers].

The final defence

The shallow alarm was the last control the vessel had, and it was too late to prevent the grounding. The alarm was set to 0.9 m under the keel, the value written into the setup at commissioning in 2019 and never changed. It sounded once, at 18:11:48, and both throttles were moved astern together 4 seconds later. Contact followed at 18:12:04, 16 seconds after the alarm. At 7.1 kt the vessel covered 58 m in that time, against the vessel's trials figure of 92 m for a crash stop from 8 kt. Astern thrust was developing but had not reached full at the moment of contact. On the surveyed bank gradient of about 1 in 70, a setting of about 1.6 m under the keel would have sounded about 107 m short of the contact, enough for a crash stop with four seconds to react. Such a setting would still not sound in the dredged channel at low water, whereas a setting that gave forty seconds would, which is why the setting has to be chosen for the channel and not simply raised. The equipment did exactly what it was set to do, and the setting and the speed together are extremely likely what left too little warning to stop the vessel, so the 2019 setting extremely likely contributed to the grounding.

How the factors related

The plotter freeze, in a transit with no night plan or hold point, led to the transit continuing at eight knots. The heading change, which began in the same second as the freeze, was held and unnoticed, because the one remaining reference was screened by canopy that the Authority's own deferrals had left to grow. The resulting westing took the vessel out of the dredged channel, and the alarm setting then left too little time to stop on the bank. The organisational conditions behind this were a risk assessment that was not reopened when the service moved into darkness, a defect procedure that did not require a self-clearing fault to be recorded, and a funding scope that kept the screening unaddressed for three works rounds.

Findings

From the evidence available, the following findings are made with respect to the grounding of MV Tarrakin Star on Boolarong Bank. These findings should not be read as apportioning blame or liability to any particular organisation or individual.

The investigation applied the standard of proof "Likely or probable": a proposition is established at a probability of 65% or more and not established at 35% or less, and anything between is inconclusive.

Contributing factors

  • The Port Authority deferred work order WO-118, which recommended trimming the mangrove canopy on Merrandi Islet that screened the No. 4 beacon, at three successive works rounds in 2023, 2024 and 2025, each time as unfunded. The scope of schedule 2.4 of the services agreement extended only 50 m from an aid, and the islet is 240 m from the beacon, so the work competed in the general waterways vote.   • The No. 4 beacon was screened from the channel by the mangrove canopy on Merrandi Islet between 0.84 nm and 0.34 nm north of the beacon.
