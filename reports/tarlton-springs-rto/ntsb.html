Executive summary

What happened

On 18/06/2026 Saltbush Air flight SB512, a 50-seat twin-turboprop registered VH-SBQ, began its take-off roll on runway 09 at Tarlton Springs Aerodrome at 17:30 with 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew on board. Times are local time (UTC+10). The roll began 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight, with no moon. The pilot in command called V1 (112 kt) at 17:30:29, and the first officer called two kangaroos on the runway at 17:30:31. The pilot in command rejected the take-off at 17:30:32 at 118 kt, 6 kt and 3 seconds past V1, with 695 m of sealed surface remaining.

The aircraft struck both animals at 17:30:33 at 114 kt, and the No. 2 propeller and right main landing gear took the impact. It left the sealed surface at about 21 kt at 17:30:51 and stopped at 17:30:57, 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area, upright and on the extended centreline. The aircraft sustained substantial damage. There were nil serious injuries, and one cabin crew member reported bruising during the disembarkation. Two kangaroos were killed. There was no fire and no evacuation, and passengers disembarked by the forward airstair. The aerodrome was closed for 4 hours 20 minutes.

What the investigation found

The aircraft overran the runway because the take-off was rejected above V1 after two kangaroos reached the sealed surface, and the strike then left the stop with too little deceleration. Neither alone would have carried the aircraft off the end. The scheduled accelerate-stop distance of 1,780 m against 1,847 m available left a 67 m margin, and a stop beginning 6 kt and 3 seconds late used it up. The strike disabled the No. 2 propeller, so the mean deceleration was 2.6 m/s², approximately 15% below the certified value.

The kangaroos probably reached the runway because the northern boundary fence, recorded as failed in 04/2023, had been deferred unfunded at three budget rounds. It is very likely that the departure fell in the period of logged kangaroo movement, and no runway inspection was requested before the departure after last light. The darkness probably hid the animals until they were on the lit surface.

The operator's inspection requirement applied only to aerodromes listed after a reported strike. It is very likely that this criterion contributed to the absence of a request, because only the outcome the inspection exists to prevent could meet it. The evening service was approved in 05/2025 as a schedule change without reopening the daylight-only route risk assessment, which probably led to the roll in darkness. The AS-139 threshold left the aerodrome with no documented wildlife plan, although the aerodrome was not in breach, and this probably left the fence a deferrable works item. No training or guidance covered a runway obstruction during the take-off roll, which probably contributed to the reject above V1.

Maximum braking was established 2 seconds after the decision, against a fleet mean of 3.2 seconds, and brake energy reached 84% of the certified maximum without fire. This very likely decreased risk, because the aircraft stopped within the prepared safety area.

Two further matters are not settled findings. The floor escape path lighting is checked only at a 24-month functional test, and on VH-SBQ two floor segments were found dark. Four northern edge lights measured above nominal intensity. Whether the crew were fatigued could not be determined either way.

What can be done

Crews, operators and regulators should not rely on a control that can only be triggered by the event it exists to prevent. Operators should list aerodromes for runway inspection on wildlife exposure, review route risk assessments when a service first operates after last light, and give crews guidance and training for an obstruction on the runway at and after V1.

The evening service was suspended on 19/06/2026. Temporary revision TR-2026-04 listed Tarlton Springs in Appendix 8A on 26/06/2026. The aerodrome operator funded the fence replacement for 09/2026 and installed a temporary electric wire on 25/06/2026. Two recommendations to the operator are open, with responses due by 30/09/2026. Factual information

History of the flight

Saltbush Air flight SB512 was the last scheduled sector of 18/06/2026, from Tarlton Springs Aerodrome to Port Carrick. The aircraft was VH-SBQ, a 50-seat twin-turboprop, carrying 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew. It was the fifth sector of the crew's duty, and the first officer was pilot flying.

The only runway inspection that day was the daily movement area inspection at 07:35, before the first scheduled arrival. The aerodrome's daily inspection log gives the result as "movement area clear". No inspection was carried out or requested afterwards.

The doors closed at 17:16 and SB512 was scheduled off blocks at 17:20. At 17:21:50 the CTAF recording holds three transmitter keyings from SB512, which switched on the pilot-activated runway lighting. At 17:21:56 the pilot in command said: "Lights are on. That's thirty minutes on the timer." At 17:23:15 the pilot in command broadcast on the CTAF that SB512 was taxiing for runway 09. Tarlton Springs has no control tower, so the crew made their own broadcasts.

At 17:28:51 the first officer reported the AWIS "still zero nine zero at six". At 17:29:10 the pilot in command broadcast that SB512 was lining up on runway 09. At 17:29:22 the pilot in command handed the take-off to the first officer with the words "Your aeroplane, your take-off." No runway inspection was requested by the crew or offered by the aerodrome. Wildlife was not mentioned on the CTAF or on the flight deck before departure.

The take-off roll began at 17:30:02 with both engines at 98.4% and 98.5% torque. The 80 kt check was made at 17:30:15 and the pilot in command called "V one" at 17:30:29, at an indicated airspeed of 112 kt. At 17:30:31 the first officer called: "Roos - roos, roos, on the left, they're on the -". The first officer saw two kangaroos moving onto the runway from the northern side, crossing to the south, at an estimated range of around 200 m at first sight. According to the occurrence notification, the animals were past the 1,100 m point. VR, 116 kt, was reached in the same second, and no rotate call was made.

At 17:30:32 the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control", and the first officer replied "You have control." The power levers reached flight idle within 0.6 seconds, at 118 kt, 6 kt and 3 seconds after V1. The aircraft had 695 m of sealed runway ahead of it (Figure 5).

The aircraft struck both animals at 17:30:33 at 114 kt. The right main landing gear and the No. 2 propeller took the impact. The flight data recorder (FDR) registered a lateral acceleration spike of 0.31 g right and a vertical spike of 1.48 g. Maximum braking was established at 17:30:34. At 17:30:36 No. 1 reverse was selected, and the pilot in command said: "Reverse - number two's not coming. Max brakes, max brakes." At 17:30:47 the pilot in command said: "We're not stopping on it." The first officer called "End's coming up" at 17:30:49.

The aircraft left the sealed surface at about 21 kt at 17:30:51. Deceleration fell from 2.6 m/s² on the seal to 1.9 m/s² in graded gravel. It came to rest at 17:30:57, 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area, upright and on the extended centreline, with the nose wheel in graded gravel. There was no fire.

At 17:31:04 the pilot in command reported no fire indications and nothing on the warning board, and said "I'm not evacuating". Both engines were shut down at 17:31:26. At 17:31:54 the pilot in command told the aerodrome reporting officer on the CTAF that the aircraft had rejected and gone off the end of runway 09, that there were no injuries reported and no fire, and asked for the aerodrome emergency plan to be activated. The reporting officer activated it at 17:32:10 and dispatched a vehicle. At 17:32:24 the reporting officer broadcast that runway 09/27 was obstructed and the aerodrome was closed. The aerodrome remained closed for 4 hours 20 minutes.

The forward airstair was operating at 17:34:40. No evacuation was commanded and no slides were deployed. The main cabin lights stayed on throughout. The occurrence notification and the cabin crew statement give the disembarkation as 4 minutes 8 seconds after the aircraft stopped. The timeline entry for the disembarkation runs from 17:34:40 to 17:38:48, which is also 4 minutes 8 seconds. The aircraft had stopped at 17:30:57. Damage to the aircraft

The aircraft sustained substantial damage. The engineering inspection of 19/06/2026 to 21/06/2026, the structures report ESR-2026-0417 and the propeller teardown PTR-2026-0417-02 recorded the following.

  • Three No. 2 propeller blades were damaged beyond repairable limits and one blade tip separated.
  • The No. 2 propeller pitch change actuator dome was cracked through, the oil transfer tube was fractured and the pitch change oil was lost.
  • The No. 2 engine required a shock-load inspection. A borescope of both engines found no pre-existing damage.
  • The right inboard flap, at the take-off setting at impact, had its lower skin crushed and torn over 0.9 m of span.
  • The wing lower surface aft of the No. 2 nacelle had two punctures, of 30 mm and 55 mm, consistent with propeller debris. There was no main spar damage and no fuel leak.
  • The right main gear doors were distorted. The forward door was bowed 85 mm outboard at its trailing edge against a running clearance to the nacelle of 22 mm. Gear structure was within limits on detailed inspection.
  • Both right main tyres deflated through the fuse plugs.
  • The lower right fuselage skin had abrasion and two punctures consistent with propeller debris.
  • Overrun damage was confined to the nose gear, its doors and the lower forward fuselage. The structures survey separated strike damage from overrun damage by animal tissue and hair, the direction of deformation and the absence of gravel.

Recorded data

The FDR and cockpit voice recorder (CVR) were removed by the operator's engineering representative at 22:40 on 18/06/2026 and couriered to the operator's flight data section. The FDR readout report FDA-2026-0417-R1 was issued on 24/06/2026. The CVR was transcribed on 25/06/2026 and the transcript was verified by a second investigator.

The FDR readout showed the following.

  • Both engines were at take-off torque, within 0.1% of each other, from power set at 17:30:02 until the power levers were retarded at 17:30:32. Fuel flow, ITT and propeller RPM on both engines were within normal scatter, and no caution, warning or exceedance was recorded before the reject.
  • The reject began 3 seconds after V1 at 118 kt. The power levers reached flight idle within 0.6 seconds and maximum braking was established 2 seconds after the decision. The operator's recorded fleet mean from decision to maximum braking for rejected take-offs is 3.2 seconds.
  • Torque on the two engines fell to 12% and 9% by the time of impact at 17:30:33. No. 2 propeller RPM decayed from that moment. No. 2 reverse was not selected and the No. 2 reverse interlock did not make. The No. 2 blade angle stayed near the flight idle value for the rest of the stop and never reached the ground idle range.
  • Distance reconstruction from recorded groundspeed put the aircraft 1,152 m along runway 09 at the reject, leaving 695 m of sealed surface. The stopping distance actually achieved from 118 kt was 757 m.
  • The mean deceleration from 17:30:34 to 17:30:44 was 2.6 m/s². The FDR readout gave this as approximately 15% below the value used in the certified accelerate-stop data for this weight and runway condition.

Aerodrome and site information

Tarlton Springs is a certified aerodrome with no control tower. Crews use the CTAF, and the runway lighting is pilot-activated. Keyed from the CTAF, the lighting runs for 30 minutes at a single intensity set at the aerodrome's lighting controller. Crews cannot vary it. The runway end safety area is 90 m long and graded, with ungraded terrain beyond. The aircraft stopped 28 m short of the end of the prepared area.

The aerodrome's heaviest scheduled aeroplane is the 22,900 kg twin-turboprop used on the Saltbush Air service. It handled 61,400 passenger movements in 2025. Aerodrome staff go off duty at 18:30. The reporting officer was on duty at the terminal during the turnaround and confirmed that an inspection would have been carried out if one had been requested. Aerodrome Manual 6.3 provides for inspections outside the scheduled time on the request of the reporting officer or a pilot in command.

  • Two adult kangaroos were recovered from runway 09 at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the threshold, on and either side of the centreline.
  • The nose wheel rut continued from the runway end to the stopping point 62 m into the runway end safety area, on the extended centreline.

Organisational and management information

Aerodrome standard and aerodrome measures

Aerodrome Standard AS-139 section 10.4 provides:

  • "10.4.1 The operator of a certified aerodrome must take reasonable measures to reduce the risk to aircraft operations presented by wildlife on or in the vicinity of the aerodrome." (AS-139 10.4.1)
  • "10.4.2 The operator of a certified aerodrome must prepare, implement and maintain a documented wildlife hazard management plan where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes having a maximum take-off weight exceeding 27,000 kg, or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements in a calendar year." (AS-139 10.4.2)
  • "10.4.3 Where subsection 10.4.2 does not apply, the measures taken under 10.4.1 need not be documented, but the aerodrome operator must be able to demonstrate what measures are in place." (AS-139 10.4.3)

Neither limb of 10.4.2 applied to Tarlton Springs. The aerodrome held no wildlife hazard management plan, no hazard assessment, no dispersal programme and no record of measures beyond the single pre-first-arrival inspection under Aerodrome Manual 6.3 and a sighting log. It was not in breach of the standard.

The Tarlton Shire Council aerodrome works register item WR-221, raised in 04/2023, described the northern boundary perimeter fence between the 700 m and 1,300 m runway markers as having four panels displaced and wire strained, with animals observed passing through. It recommended replacement at an estimated $41,000. The item was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 budget rounds, each time with the entry "defer, unfunded, review next cycle". Item WR-263, raised in 02/2026, described the same section as "deteriorating further".

The investigator's site inspection on 23/06/2026 found a continuous 140 m section of the northern fence with the bottom two wires separated from the posts. There was a clear animal pad through the fence at the 1,050 m mark, 138 m before the point at which the first carcass was recovered (Figure 2).

The aerodrome's wildlife sighting log for 01/01/2025 to 18/06/2026 holds 46 entries. Of these, 41 record kangaroos on or adjacent to the movement area. Of those 41, 38 are timed between one hour before and two hours after last light, and eleven record animals on the sealed surface of runway 09/27. On 18/06/2026 last light was 17:25, so that window ran from 16:25 to 19:25. The log is a paper register held in the aerodrome office. It is not reported to any operator, and the reporting officer confirmed that no airline has ever asked for it.

Operator procedures

  • "8.14.2 At an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, the pilot in command shall request a runway inspection from the aerodrome operator prior to the first departure after last light, and shall not commence the take-off roll until the result of that inspection has been passed." (Part A 8.14.2)

Training

The recurrent training syllabus TRG-SYL-07, cycle 2025 to 2026, module 4 gives its objective as: "the crew will demonstrate a correctly executed rejected take-off for an engine failure, an engine fire and a configuration warning, each initiated below V1, and will demonstrate the decision to continue for a failure recognised at or after V1." The module has four scripted simulator scenarios: engine failure at 90 kt, engine fire at 100 kt, configuration warning at 85 kt, and engine failure at V1 plus 5 kt, continued. Every scenario is an aircraft system event. None presents an obstruction, an animal or any external hazard on the runway at any speed. A total of 214 crew completed module 4 in the 2025 to 2026 cycle. No crew has been presented with an external obstruction scenario in the simulator in the five cycles held on file.

Floor escape path lighting

The first officer noticed sections of the aisle floor strip unlit while walking back through the cabin during the disembarkation, and a functional check of the cabin emergency lighting was raised on that account. On 24/06/2026, with the system armed and the aircraft on battery power, two of the six floor proximity escape path segments on the left side aft of row 16 did not illuminate. The fault was traced to a failed ballast common to both segments.

The pre-flight emergency lighting test on this type illuminates the ceiling emergency lights and the exit signs. It does not illuminate or monitor the floor proximity segments. Those segments are checked by a dedicated functional test at the maintenance programme's 24-month interval, and the last such test was 11 months before the occurrence. The disembarkation was conducted with the main cabin lights on, by the forward airstair, without urgency. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether the same ballast part is fitted across the fleet]

Tests and research

Saltbush Air Performance Engineering issued assessment PEA-2026-0417 on 01/07/2026, using the type certificate holder's drag data for the damage in the structures report. It reconstructs a take-off continued from 17:30:32. It is a reconstruction of something that did not happen, and the assessment says so.

The assessment found that VR was reached at 17:30:31 and that a continued take-off would have rotated from about 17:30:31.5 at the operator's normal rate of about 3 degrees a second. From the reject point at 1,152 m the aircraft, still accelerating at about 2 kt a second, would have reached the impact point of about 1,210 m within a few hundredths of a second of the time the aircraft actually did. It would have been at about 120 kt and 4 to 5 degrees nose-up, with the main wheels still on the runway, and liftoff would have followed at about 17:30:34, about 1,300 m along the runway. The No. 2 propeller tip would have been about 1.3 m above the runway, against a measured standing height of 1.6 m for the larger animal. The assessment judged a strike on the No. 2 propeller and the right main gear close to certain, at take-off power rather than flight idle.

For the undamaged aircraft at 20,800 kg, flap 10, 412 ft and +8 °C, the one-engine-inoperative second-segment gradient was 4.1% gross and 3.3% net, against the 2.4% minimum. The net flight path would have cleared the mast by about 200 ft, and the take-off was limited by accelerate-stop distance, not by climb. The assessment then applied gross gradient penalties for departures from the appendix 4C basis. These were 2.2% (range 1.6% to 2.8%) for the No. 2 propeller unfeathered and windmilling at the pitch-lock angle with the engine shut down, and 0.4% (0.3% to 0.5%) for the right main gear retracted with the doors unable to close. They were 1.6% (1.4% to 1.8%) for the right main gear not retracted, in place of the previous line, and 0.5% (0.3% to 0.8%) for right inboard flap damage with the roll control needed to hold the wings level.

In every case in which the No. 2 propeller does not feather, the gradient was below the 2.4% minimum and the net flight path did not clear the mast by 35 ft (Figure 6). The one damaged case above the minimum, case B, needs a feather that the propeller teardown said was not possible.

The assessment did not assess controllability at V2 with the right flap damaged and the right propeller windmilling, or the effect of the propeller's imbalance. The teardown report did not assess what sustained imbalance would have done to the engine mounts or the nacelle. The assessment also rests on damage done at flight idle and 114 kt. The teardown report notes that at take-off power and a higher speed the propeller would have absorbed more energy at impact, and that it could not say whether the pitch change mechanism would have failed in the same way.

The structures report could not establish whether the right inboard flap would have retracted past the torn fairing, and recorded that the flap asymmetry protection would have stopped both flaps if it had not. It assessed gear retraction as possible but not assured, because the forward door is linked to the gear leg and would have met the nacelle structure on retraction, and it assessed door closure as not possible.

The assessment noted that RRA-041 records no alternate within 200 nm at night. After a continued take-off the aircraft would therefore have had to return to Tarlton Springs, at night and over unlit country, to a runway with the two carcasses on it at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the runway 09 threshold.

Analysis

The analysis follows how the animals reached the runway, why the take-off was rejected above V1, and why the stop did not finish on the sealed surface.

General

The qualifications and currency of the flight crew were not in question. The pilot in command held 8,410 hours, 2,140 of them on type, with a satisfactory simulator check on 11/03/2026 and line check on 02/12/2025. The first officer held 1,655 hours, 405 of them on type, with a satisfactory simulator check on 28/04/2026. The pilot in command's duty and rest in the previous 7 days were within limits with no variations applied. Both crew returned negative drug and alcohol tests.

The aircraft was serviceable. A borescope of both engines found no pre-existing damage, the anti-skid system tested serviceable after the occurrence, and the brakes absorbed 84% of certified maximum energy without fire.

Weather was not a factor. The automatic observations gave visibility of 9999 and no recorded rainfall, and the runway was dry.

How the kangaroos reached the sealed surface

Two adult kangaroos were recovered from runway 09 at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the threshold. The first officer called them at 17:30:31 and the impact followed at 17:30:33. The animals were on the sealed surface, and the strike and the reject both followed from that. The route, the timing and the absence of any check on the runway are examined below.

The northern fence

Works register item WR-221 recorded the northern boundary fence between the 700 m and 1,300 m markers as failed in 04/2023. It noted four displaced panels, strained wire and animals observed passing through, and estimated replacement at $41,000. The item was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 budget rounds, each time as unfunded for review the next cycle. Item WR-263 in 02/2026 recorded the same section as deteriorating further.

The site inspection on 23/06/2026 found a continuous 140 m section with the bottom two wires separated from the posts and a clear animal pad through it at the 1,050 m mark (Figure 2). That is 138 m before the first carcass and on the same side of the runway as both carcasses (Figure 3).

WR-221 recommended replacement without stating a specification, and nothing shows that a replacement fence would exclude kangaroos, which can clear a stock fence. The sighting log also does not say where its animals came onto the aerodrome.

That evidence did not carry the argument far. The pad shows the route these two animals used, and the argument against concerns what an intact fence would have stopped. A fence repaired in 2023 would have been intact in 06/2026 and this route closed. The failed fence probably contributed to the animals reaching the runway.

The aerodrome operator funded WR-221 from the 2026 contingency, programmed the 140 m replacement for 09/2026, and installed a temporary electric wire on 25/06/2026.

The standard's threshold

AS-139 10.4.2 requires a documented wildlife hazard management plan where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes above 27,000 kg maximum take-off weight, or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements in a calendar year. Tarlton Springs' heaviest scheduled aeroplane was 22,900 kg and it handled 61,400 passenger movements in 2025. Neither limb applied, so under 10.4.3 its measures need not be documented. The aerodrome was not in breach.

The aerodrome did carry out a daily inspection before the first arrival and kept a sighting log. It held no plan, no hazard assessment and no dispersal programme. The log was not reviewed or passed to any operator. With no plan, the fence stayed an ordinary works item competing for council funds, and each deferral was made with no hazard assessment before the council.

Section 10.4.1 still required reasonable measures, and a plan is not the only way to repair a fence. The deferrals were budget decisions, and nothing shows a plan would have been funded better. The weight of that argument held this factor below the others. The standard's threshold probably contributed, by leaving the fence a deferrable item instead of a recorded wildlife control.

The kangaroo peak

The aerodrome's log for 01/2025 to 06/2026 held 46 entries, 41 of them kangaroos on or beside the movement area. Of those 41, 38 were timed from one hour before to two hours after last light, and eleven recorded animals on the sealed surface. On 18/06/2026 last light was 17:25, so the window ran from 16:25 to 19:25 and the roll began at 17:30. The pilot in command told the investigation they had seen kangaroos on the strip at Tarlton Springs four or five times, always in the evening and never in daytime.

The log records sightings and not presence. Aerodrome staff go off duty at 18:30, part way through the window, so the pattern may partly reflect when someone was there to see. The log is also a paper register whose completeness cannot be verified. The pilot in command's account and the log nonetheless agree. It is extremely likely that the departure fell in the period of peak kangaroo movement, and the departure in that period probably contributed to animals being on the runway.

No runway inspection requested

The aerodrome's only inspection on 18/06/2026 was at 07:35, before the first arrival, and it found the movement area clear. Neither the CVR nor the CTAF recording contains any request for an inspection, any offer of one or any mention of wildlife before departure. The pilot in command said wildlife was not discussed with anyone during the turnaround.

Aerodrome Manual 6.3 provides for an inspection on the request of the reporting officer or a pilot in command. The reporting officer was at the terminal during the turnaround and confirmed one would have been carried out if asked. Both crew said they did not consider requesting one. The pilot in command had expected Tarlton Springs to be listed in Appendix 8A and was surprised to find it was not.

An inspection would have covered the 1,050 m to 1,250 m section, where the fence pad and both carcasses were found, and aerodrome staff disperse animals when they find them. An inspection some minutes before the roll cannot guarantee the runway stays clear. That did not outweigh the opportunity lost, so the absence of an inspection probably contributed to the animals being on the runway.

The Appendix 8A listing criterion

Part A 8.14.2 required the pilot in command to request a runway inspection before the first departure after last light at an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, and not to start the roll until the result was passed (Figure 1). Section 8.14.3 required no specific action at other aerodromes. Appendix 8A listed an aerodrome only where a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft had been reported there in the preceding 24 months. Nine aerodromes were listed and Tarlton Springs never had been. The list was last reviewed on 03/03/2025, four months before the evening service began.

The inspection requirement itself would have worked. The criterion could be met only by a reported strike, the outcome the inspection exists to prevent. Nothing in Part A asked a crew to report a sighting, so the eleven sightings on the sealed surface could not list the aerodrome. Because the aerodrome was not listed, the requirement did not apply to SB512 on 18/06/2026.

An inspection does not guarantee a clear runway at the roll, but the control would have caused one to be requested and passed before the roll. It is very likely that the listing criterion contributed to the absence of an inspection request.

The operator amended Appendix 8A by temporary revision TR-2026-04 on 26/06/2026 to list Tarlton Springs. The investigation recommended that the operator replace the criterion with one based on wildlife exposure, and add a route risk assessment trigger for any aerodrome first operated into after last light.

Darkness and the evening service

The roll in darkness

The roll began at 17:30, 32 minutes after sunset and 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight. Moonrise was not until 22:41 and the moon was 14% illuminated. The only lighting was the runway edge lighting the crew had keyed on at 17:21:50. On the investigator's site visit at the same time of day, the unlit strip either side of the sealed surface could not be seen from the centreline.

On the CVR, the first officer said the animals "came out of the dark on the left of the strip, crossed the centreline." The first officer said they had the animals in view for about two seconds, perhaps less. In daylight, animals crossing the graded strip from the northern fence would have been in view before they reached the seal, earlier in the roll.

The first officer did see them at around 200 m once they were on the seal. Animals that came onto the strip only seconds before reaching the seal would have been seen late in daylight too. The pilot in command was looking at the engine page after V1, as procedure directs, so in daylight the sighting would still have rested on the pilot flying. The darkness probably contributed to the late sighting and so to the reject.

The evening service approval

RRA-041 issue 3 (11/2022) is the current route risk assessment for Tarlton Springs. Among its assessed hazards were a single runway with no lighting redundancy and no alternate within 200 nm at night. It carried the restriction "daylight operations only pending review of night lighting and diversion fuel policy." The Head of Flying Operations approved CMR-2025-118 on 22/05/2025, adding an evening rotation from 14/07/2025, the first scheduled company operation into Tarlton Springs after last light.

The change was assessed as "schedule change only" with "No new hazard identified". The entry for route risk assessment review required read "No." The restriction is not referenced in CMR-2025-118, RRA-041 was not reopened and no issue 4 exists. The Safety Department's involvement was limited to receipt of the approved record.

The approval asked whether the change was new and not whether the assessment still held. SB512 was scheduled off blocks at 17:20, the doors closed at 17:16 and it departed on schedule. The roll after last light therefore followed from the service, not from a delay.

The restriction named night lighting and diversion fuel and not wildlife. A review might have approved the evening service with lighting and fuel conditions and left the departure after last light as it was. The approver has left the company and cannot be asked what was in front of them, so what a review would have concluded is inference. A review would have had to assess a night operation at a single-runway aerodrome with no alternate within 200 nm at night, and that weighed more heavily. The approval of the evening service without reopening the daylight-only assessment likely contributed to the roll in darkness.

The Head of Flying Operations suspended the evening Tarlton Springs service on 19/06/2026.

The reject above V1

Part B 4.6.4 states: "At or above V1 the take-off shall be continued." (Figure 4). The section contains no guidance on an obstruction seen on the runway at any speed and no amplification of 4.6.4 for a case where continuing is itself hazardous.

V1 was called at 112 kt at 17:30:29. The first officer called the animals at 17:30:31, and the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control." at 17:30:32 (Figure 5). The power levers reached flight idle at 118 kt, 3 seconds and 6 kt past V1.

The reject began 1,152 m along the runway, with 695 m of sealed surface remaining. The scheduled accelerate-stop distance of 1,780 m against an ASDA of 1,847 m gave a 67 m margin, and that margin assumes the stop begins at or before V1. It is extremely likely that the reject above V1 contributed to the overrun. A start 3 seconds and 6 kt later used up the margin before the strike took its toll.

The pilot in command told the investigation that they judged continuing would leave the aircraft airborne at night with damage they could not assess, over country with no usable option. They said the decision was not in line with the procedure.

The alternative

The performance assessment reconstructed a continued take-off. VR, 116 kt, was reached at 17:30:31. A continued aircraft would have reached the animals at about the same place and within a few hundredths of a second of the actual strike. It would have been at about 120 kt and 4 to 5 degrees nose-up, with the main wheels on the runway and the engines at take-off power. A strike on the No. 2 propeller and right main gear was assessed as close to certain.

The teardown found the pitch change actuator cracked through and its oil lost, so the propeller could not have been feathered after the impact. With the propeller windmilling, the right main gear doors unable to close and the right inboard flap damaged, the central case gave a one-engine climb of 1.0% gross against the 2.4% minimum (Figure 6). The net path would have been short of the telecommunications mast. Its top is 184 ft, 3,400 m beyond the runway end and 260 m north of the extended centreline. RRA-041 records no alternate within 200 nm at night, so the aircraft would have had to return to a runway with the two carcasses on it.

Rejecting the take-off was probably the safest course of action available, and no higher than probably. The structural damage was done at flight idle and 114 kt, and the teardown could not say whether the pitch change mechanism would have failed the same way at take-off power. A propeller that feathered would have left about 3.2%, and a gradient below the minimum is not itself a crash. Controllability at V2 and the effect of the propeller's imbalance were not assessed.

This does not lower the reject's contribution to the overrun and does not make the decision compliant with 4.6.4. It also does not show that the pilot in command could have known it in the second available.

Training and guidance

The four scripted scenarios in training syllabus module 4 are engine failure at 90 kt, engine fire at 100 kt, configuration warning at 85 kt, and engine failure at V1 plus 5 kt, continued. All are aircraft system events. None presents an obstruction, an animal or any external hazard on the runway. No crew has met an external obstruction scenario in the five cycles on file, which included 214 crew in the current cycle alone. Part B 4.6 has no obstruction guidance. Appendix 4C states the assumptions behind the go case (an engine failure at V1 on an otherwise undamaged aeroplane with the propeller feathered), but 4.6 does not refer to them. Both crew said independently that every stop or go scenario they had been given was an engine.

The pilot in command said they knew the rule and departed from it deliberately, so training might not have changed what they knew. An unrehearsed decision made cold in about a second is not the same as one made against a briefed and practised frame. The performance assessment adds that guidance could only have told the crew which of two outcomes outside the certified data to take, and it might well have told them to stop. The likelihood assigned therefore rests on the decision being unrehearsed and not on what the guidance would have said.

It is extremely likely that the absence of training and guidance existed. It is likely that it contributed to the reject above V1. The investigation recommended that the operator, with the type certificate holder, develop guidance and simulator training for an obstruction during the take-off roll, including at and after V1.

The stop

The strike at 17:30:33, at 114 kt, one second after the reject began, took out the No. 2 propeller. Three blades were damaged beyond limits, animal tissue was found in two blade roots and the pitch change actuator was cracked through with its oil lost. The No. 2 blade angle stayed near flight idle and never reached the ground idle range, and the reverse interlock did not make. Reverse was selected on No. 1 only and held straight with rudder.

The achieved mean deceleration of 2.6 m/s² was about 15% below the certified value. The stop from 118 kt needed 757 m against 695 m of sealed surface. At the certified deceleration from the same point, the reconstruction puts the stop at roughly 660 m, inside the 695 m available. The strike and the resulting deceleration shortfall both contributed to the overrun, and the evidence is conclusive on each. Each was needed to reach the end, and neither alone did. The aircraft left the seal at about 21 kt, where deceleration fell to 1.9 m/s² in gravel, and stopped 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area, 28 m short of the end of the prepared area.

The braking that did occur reduced the consequences. Maximum braking was established at 17:30:34, 2 seconds after the decision, against a fleet mean of 3.2 seconds. Anti-skid was active on all four wheels and brake energy reached 84% of certified maximum without fire. A stop begun at the fleet mean response would have carried the aircraft an estimated further 30 to 40 m, close to the far edge of the runway end safety area. The prompt braking very likely decreased the risk.

Other factors that increased risk

The evening rotation roster

The evening rotation operated on 289 days between 14/07/2025 and 18/06/2026. Each time it was rostered as the fourth and fifth sectors of a Port Carrick-based duty, signing on between 06:00 and 07:00. The planned duty was between 12 hours 45 minutes and 13 hours 15 minutes, against a 13 hour 30 minute maximum. On six of those days a member of the rostered crew reported unfit and the rotation went to a crew already on duty, extending that crew's day from three sectors to five. 18/06/2026 was the sixth, with sign-on at 06:30 and the take-off 11 hours in.

The FDR shows 0.6 seconds from decision to the power levers at flight idle and 2 seconds to maximum braking, at the fast end of the fleet distribution. There was no error of omission on the roll. The one decision a long duty could plausibly have affected, requesting an inspection, was never prompted by any document. It is unlikely that the roster contributed to this occurrence.

The roster remains a risk. Every operation of the rotation closed a five-sector duty planned to within 45 minutes of the maximum, at an aerodrome with no alternate within 200 nm at night and limited engineering support. It is likely that this exposure increased risk on the operations of the rotation.

Floor escape path lighting

The pre-flight emergency lighting test on this type illuminates the ceiling emergency lights and the exit signs. It does not illuminate or monitor the floor proximity escape path segments, whose only check is a dedicated functional test at the 24-month interval. The first officer noticed sections of the aisle floor strip unlit while walking back through the cabin during the disembarkation, which led to the functional check on 24/06/2026. That check found two of the six floor proximity segments on the left side aft of row 16 dark on battery power, traced to a failed common ballast. The last dedicated functional test had been 11 months before the occurrence.

No evacuation was commanded and the main cabin lights were on throughout. The disembarkation took 4 minutes 8 seconds by the forward airstair without incident. It is unlikely that the defect or the monitoring gap influenced the overrun or its outcome.

A defect of this kind can stay undetected for the remainder of the interval, because nothing a crew or line engineer can run would reveal it. In a fire, smoke or night evacuation over the wing, two dark segments in the aft cabin could separate an orderly exit from a blocked one. It is very likely that this gap increased risk.

Considered and not found contributing

A tailwind was considered because a stop that runs 62 m past the end invites a question about the wind. The automatic observations gave a headwind component of 5 to 7 kt on runway 09, and the FDR gave 4 to 6 kt throughout, with no tailwind at any point. A tailwind component was very unlikely.

An engine power loss before the reject was considered because a reject at 118 kt is most often an engine event. Both engines were within 0.1% torque from power set until the levers were retarded, and no caution or exceedance was recorded. The CVR has no engine-related call, and the borescope found no prior damage. The No. 2 decay began at 17:30:33, after the reject began and at the moment of impact. A pre-reject power loss was very unlikely.

The edge lighting intensity was also considered and is discussed under the unresolved matters below. It was not found to be contributing.

Unresolved: flight crew fatigue

It is about as likely as not that the flight crew were fatigued at the time of the take-off, and the matter could not be settled. The take-off was 11 hours into the duty on the fifth sector, after a 06:30 sign-on. The day had been extended that morning from three sectors to five, and both crew described themselves in interview as tired.

Both crew described the tiredness as ordinary and not impairing, and the first officer said they had had a proper break in Port Carrick during the duty. The planned duty of 13 hours was inside the 13 hour 30 minute maximum, and the 7-day duty and rest were within limits. The recorded performance shows none of the usual signs, with fast power lever and braking responses, callouts on time and no error of omission.

"Tired" after 11 hours is not the same as fatigued to a degree that degrades performance, and no other evidence reaches it. Settling it would need a detailed fatigue assessment with sleep history and the duty pattern before 18/06. That was judged not required, because every mechanism fatigue would have worked through here is contradicted by recorded data. The matter remains unresolved and nothing from it is carried into the conclusions.

Unresolved: edge lights above nominal intensity

A photometric check on 25/06/2026, seven days after the occurrence, measured four northern edge lights between the 900 m and 1,200 m markers at 118% to 131% of nominal intensity for stage 3. This is the section the animals crossed. The runway lighting is pilot-activated and runs for 30 minutes at one fixed intensity, found at stage 3, the commissioning setting. Crews cannot vary it. No work on the lighting or its controller was recorded between the occurrence and the check, and the aerodrome's maintenance programme checks lamp serviceability but does not measure intensity. It is likely that the four lights were at the measured intensity on the night.

Whether that intensity delayed detection of the animals, and so the reject, is as likely as not and could not be determined. The pilot in command recalled the lighting as brighter than they would want, a corridor of light with everything outside it black. Brighter edge lights deepen the contrast with the dark strip beside them, which is where the animals came from.

The first officer, who saw the animals, described the lighting as normal and saw the animals clearly once they were on the seal. They also said that with two daylight landings to compare against, they were not well placed to judge. The pilot in command's non-detection is explained by the heads-in procedure after V1, and darkness alone explains why nothing off the seal was seen. No study links a 131% edge light to the detection of an animal.

The intensity is the settled part and the effect on detection is not. The edge lighting was not found to be contributing.

Probable cause

The aircraft overran the end of runway 09 because the take-off was rejected 6 kt above V1 after two kangaroos were seen on the runway and then struck, which left the deceleration about 15% below the certified value. The kangaroos probably reached the sealed surface because a failed northern boundary fence had been left unrepaired and no runway inspection was requested before a departure after last light, in darkness, during the period of recorded kangaroo movement. The operator's controls and training did not cover this case, and the evening service had been introduced without reopening the daylight-only risk assessment.

The operator's training syllabus and Part B 4.6 did not cover an obstruction on the runway during the take-off roll. Part B 4.6.4 required the take-off to be continued at or above V1, and the guidance did not say that this rule rests on data for an engine failure on an otherwise undamaged aeroplane (Figure 4).

The investigation recommends that the operator, with the type certificate holder, develop guidance and simulator training for an obstruction on the runway during the take-off roll, including at and after V1. The guidance should state that the continue-at-V1 rule rests on take-off data for an engine failure on an otherwise undamaged aeroplane.
