What happened

On 18/06/2026 Saltbush Air flight SB512, a 50-seat twin-turboprop registered VH-SBQ, was scheduled to fly from Tarlton Springs to Port Carrick with 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew. All times in this report are local time (UTC+10). SB512 was the fifth sector of the crew's duty, which had begun with a 06:30 sign-on. The evening rotation had been added to the crew's day that morning when a member of the rostered crew reported unfit. The doors closed at 17:16 and the aircraft was scheduled off blocks at 17:20.

Sunset was at 16:58, civil twilight ended at 17:25, and the moon, 14% illuminated, did not rise until 22:41, so the aerodrome was in darkness. Tarlton Springs has no control tower, so the crew made their own broadcasts on the common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF) and had switched on the pilot-activated runway lighting at 17:21:50. The wind was 090 at 6 kt and the weather was not a factor. The aerodrome's only runway inspection that day was at 07:35, before the first arrival, and recorded "movement area clear". No further inspection was requested or offered. The pilot in command had 8,410 hours, 2,140 of them on type, and the first officer had 1,655 hours, 405 on type. Both crew tested negative for drugs and alcohol.

The first officer was the pilot flying. Power was set at 17:30:02 with both engines at 98.4% and 98.5% torque, and the pilot in command called V1 (the speed at or above which a take-off is continued) at 112 kt at 17:30:29. Two seconds later the first officer called "Roos - roos, roos, on the left", having seen two kangaroos crossing the runway from the northern side at a range of about 200 m. The aircraft reached rotation speed, 116 kt, in the same second and no rotate call was made. At 17:30:32 the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control." and rejected the take-off at 118 kt, 6 kt and 3 seconds past V1. The aircraft was then 1,152 m along the runway with 695 m of sealed surface remaining (Figure 3).

At 17:30:33 it struck both animals at 114 kt. The impact damaged three blades of the No. 2 propeller beyond limits and made the No. 2 reverse thrust unavailable for the rest of the stop. Maximum braking was established at 17:30:34, 2 seconds after the decision, and reverse was selected on the No. 1 engine only at 17:30:36. The aircraft left the end of the sealed surface at about 21 kt at 17:30:51. It stopped at 17:30:57, 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area, upright and on the extended centreline, with the nose wheel in graded gravel and 28 m short of the end of the prepared area. There was no fire.

The aerodrome emergency plan was activated at 17:32:10 at the pilot in command's request. Between 17:34:40 and 17:38:48 the passengers disembarked by the forward airstair in 4 minutes 8 seconds, and no evacuation was commanded. The aircraft sustained substantial damage to:

  • the No. 2 propeller, with an inspection of the engine for shock load
  • the right inboard flap and the wing lower surface aft of the No. 2 nacelle
  • the right main gear doors
  • both right main tyres, which deflated through the fuse plugs at 84% of certified brake energy
  • the lower right fuselage, which was punctured

There were nil serious injuries, and one cabin crew member reported bruising during the disembarkation. Two kangaroos were killed, and their carcasses were recovered at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the threshold, with continuous tyre marks from 1,160 m to the runway end. The aerodrome was closed for 4 hours 20 minutes.

The take-off began 11 hours 00 minutes into a planned 13 hour 00 minute duty, against a maximum of 13 hours 30 minutes. Why it happened

The overrun followed from two conditions that were both needed. The aircraft began its stop later than the published stopping data assume, and the strike on two kangaroos then reduced how hard it could decelerate. The sections below follow the chain from how the animals reached the runway, through why nothing was done to look for them, to the decision to stop and its effect.

How the animals reached the runway

The northern boundary fence between the 700 m and 1,300 m runway markers was recorded as failed in works register item WR-221 in 04/2023. The entry noted four displaced panels, strained wire and animals observed passing through, and estimated replacement at $41,000. It was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 budget rounds, each time with the entry "defer, unfunded, review next cycle".

The investigator's site inspection on 23/06/2026 found a continuous 140 m section with the bottom two wires separated from the posts and a clear animal pad through it at the 1,050 m mark (Figure 2). That is 138 m before the point where the first carcass was recovered, on the same side of the runway. The failed fence was likely the route by which the animals reached the movement area, and an intact fence would likely have closed it. Nothing in the evidence shows whether a replacement fence would exclude kangaroos, which can clear a stock fence, and the sighting log does not record where any animal came onto the aerodrome. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether a replacement fence would exclude kangaroos]

The aerodrome held no wildlife plan or hazard assessment that would have placed the fence in a different category. Aerodrome Standard AS-139 10.4.2 requires a documented wildlife hazard management plan only where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes above 27,000 kg maximum take-off weight, or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements in a calendar year. The heaviest scheduled aeroplane at Tarlton Springs is 22,900 kg and it handled 61,400 passenger movements in 2025, so neither limb applied and the aerodrome was not in breach. Section 10.4.1 still required reasonable measures, and a documented plan is not the only way to repair a fence. The council's deferrals were also budget decisions, and a plan does not itself fund works. Even so, it is likely that the lack of any plan allowed the fence to stay in the works register as an ordinary maintenance item, deferred three times with no hazard assessment behind it.

Two kangaroos were on the sealed surface when SB512 arrived. They were recovered at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the threshold, and the first officer saw them crossing from the northern side at a range of around 200 m. The investigation found that this crossing was extremely likely to have led directly to both the reject and the strike (Figure 3).

Why the departure met them

The aerodrome's sighting log for 01/01/2025 to 18/06/2026 holds 46 entries. Of these, 41 record kangaroos on or adjacent to the movement area, 38 of those 41 are timed between one hour before and two hours after last light, and eleven record animals on the sealed surface. On 18/06/2026 that window ran from 16:25 to 19:25, and the roll began at 17:30. The pilot in command's own experience matched the log, since they had seen kangaroos on the strip four or five times, always in the evening. It is likely that departing inside this window contributed to the aircraft meeting the animals. The log records sightings rather than presence, and aerodrome staff go off duty at 18:30, so the pattern may partly reflect when someone was there to see.

Why no inspection was requested

The operator's Operations Manual Part A 8.14.2 states:

  • "At an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, the pilot in command shall request a runway inspection from the aerodrome operator prior to the first departure after last light, and shall not commence the take-off roll until the result of that inspection has been passed." (Figure 1)

Section 8.14.3 requires no specific action at any other aerodrome. Appendix 8A lists an aerodrome only where a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft has been reported there within the preceding 24 months. Nine aerodromes were listed and Tarlton Springs never had been, because no company strike had occurred there. No provision in Part A asks a crew to report a sighting, so eleven sightings of kangaroos on the sealed surface could not put the aerodrome on the list. The list was last reviewed on 03/03/2025, four months before the evening service began. The control could therefore only be triggered by the outcome it exists to prevent. It is very likely that this listing criterion contributed to no inspection being requested, since the requirement, had it applied, would have led to a request and a pass before the roll.

Neither the CVR nor the CTAF recording contains any request for an inspection, any offer of one, or any mention of wildlife before departure. The aerodrome's only inspection that day was at 07:35, before the first arrival. Aerodrome Manual 6.3 allows an inspection at the request of a pilot in command, and the reporting officer, who was on duty at the terminal, confirmed one would have been carried out if asked. Both crew said they did not consider requesting one, and the pilot in command had expected Tarlton Springs to be on the Appendix 8A list. Because no document prompted it, the absence of a request was likely a further step in allowing the animals to reach the runway unseen. An inspection would have covered the section where the pad and both carcasses were found, although one completed some minutes before the roll could not guarantee the runway stayed clear.

Why the roll was in darkness

The route risk assessment for Tarlton Springs, RRA-041 issue 3 (11/2022), carried the operating restriction "daylight operations only pending review of night lighting and diversion fuel policy". Change Management Record CMR-2025-118, approved by the Head of Flying Operations on 22/05/2025, introduced a scheduled evening rotation from 14/07/2025. It assessed the change as "schedule change only", recorded "no new hazard identified", and answered "No" to route risk assessment review required. The daylight restriction was not referenced, RRA-041 was not reopened, and no issue 4 exists. The Safety Department's records show no involvement beyond receipt of the approved record. This was the first company operation into Tarlton Springs after last light, and SB512 left on schedule rather than late.

It is likely that approving the evening service without reopening the assessment led to SB512 rolling in darkness. Honoured, the restriction would have kept every company departure in daylight. The restriction names lighting and diversion fuel, not wildlife, so a review might have approved the service with conditions on those two items and left the departure time unchanged. The approver has left the company, so what a review would have concluded is inference. [NOT ESTABLISHED: why the approver of CMR-2025-118 assessed the change as schedule change only, because the approver has left the company]

The roll began at 17:30, 32 minutes after sunset and 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight, with moonrise not until 22:41 and the moon 14% illuminated. The aerodrome was in full darkness, and the investigator's site visit at the same time of day found that the unlit strip either side of the seal cannot be seen from the centreline. The first officer said the animals "came out of the dark" and that they saw them at around 200 m once they were on the seal. In daylight, animals crossing the graded strip would have been in view earlier in the roll. It is likely that darkness contributed to the animals being seen late. The animals' time on the strip before reaching the seal is not known, and animals arriving seconds before the seal would have been seen late in daylight too. The pilot in command was also looking at the engine page after V1, which is where the procedure places them.

The decision to reject above V1

Part B 4.6.4 states: "At or above V1 the take-off shall be continued. The decision to continue is made before V1 is reached." (Figure 4) Section 4.6 contains no guidance on an obstruction on the runway at any speed, and no amplification of 4.6.4 for a case in which continuing is itself hazardous. Training module 4 holds four scripted scenarios, all aircraft system events, and none presents an obstruction or animal. No crew has met an external obstruction scenario in the five training cycles on file, including 214 crew in the current cycle. Both flight crew told the investigation that every stop or go scenario they had been given was an engine event.

The CVR shows the decision in one exchange. V1 was called at 17:30:29, the first officer called the kangaroos at 17:30:31, and the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control." at 17:30:32 (Figure 5). The FDR shows both power levers moved to flight idle from 118 kt, three seconds and six knots past V1. In the pilot in command's account to the investigation, the decision was made in about a second with no prior training or briefing on the question. The pilot in command also said that they knew the rule and judged continuing to be the worse outcome. The decision was therefore deliberate and not made from unawareness of the rule. It is nonetheless likely that the absence of training and guidance contributed to the reject, because a decision made unrehearsed in about a second differs from one made against a briefed and practised frame. That finding rests on the decision being unrehearsed and not on what guidance would have said.

How the strike and the late start combined

The scheduled accelerate-stop distance was 1,780 m against an accelerate-stop distance available of 1,847 m, a margin of 67 m. That figure assumes the stop begins at or before V1. Here it began at 1,152 m along the runway, leaving 695 m of sealed surface. It is extremely likely that rejecting above V1 contributed to the overrun, because beginning the stop six knots and three seconds late consumed the 67 m margin and more.

The aircraft struck both animals at 114 kt, one second after the reject began. Three No. 2 propeller blades were damaged beyond limits, and the pitch change actuator was cracked through with its oil lost. The blades could therefore not be driven to ground idle or reverse, and the No. 2 reverse did not make. The strike took the No. 2 propeller out of the stop, and the achieved mean deceleration was 2.6 m/s2, approximately 15% below the certified value. The stop from 118 kt took 757 m, whereas at the certified deceleration from the same point the reconstruction puts it at roughly 660 m, inside the 695 m available. Neither the late start nor the reduced deceleration alone reached the runway end. Together they did.

The crew's response limited the outcome. Maximum braking was established 2 seconds after the decision, against a fleet mean of 3.2 seconds, and brake energy reached 84% of the certified maximum without a brake fire. A stop begun at the fleet mean response would very likely have carried the aircraft a further 30 to 40 m, close to the far edge of the safety area. The aircraft left the seal at approximately 21 kt and stopped 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

The alternative of continuing

Performance assessment PEA-2026-0417 reconstructed a continued take-off from the same point. The aircraft would have reached the animals at the same place within a few hundredths of a second, at about 120 kt with the main wheels still on the runway and at take-off power. The same damage to the No. 2 propeller would likely have stopped it feathering. With the propeller windmilling, the right main gear doors unable to close and the right inboard flap damaged, the one-engine climb gradient would have been about 1.0% against the 2.4% minimum. The aircraft would not have cleared the mast 3,400 m beyond the runway end, at night, with no alternate within 200 nm and two carcasses on the only runway to return to (Figure 6). On that reconstruction, rejecting the take-off was likely the safest course available.

That judgement carries limits. The damage was done at flight idle and 114 kt, and the teardown could not say whether the pitch change mechanism would have failed in the same way at take-off power. A feathered propeller would have left about 3.2%, and a gradient below the minimum is not itself a crash. Controllability with the right flap damaged and the propeller windmilling, and the effect of the propeller's imbalance, were not assessed. The assessment does not make the decision compliant with Part B 4.6.4, and it does not show that the pilot in command could have known it in the second available. It also does not reduce the reject's contribution to the overrun.

Considered and not found contributing

A tailwind was considered because a stop that ends beyond the runway invites the question. It is very unlikely that a tailwind played any part. The weather station observations gave a headwind component of 5 to 7 kt on runway 09 and the recorded data gave 4 to 6 kt throughout, with no tailwind at any point.

A loss of No. 2 engine power before the reject was also considered, because a reject at 118 kt is most often an engine event. It is very unlikely that it occurred. Both engines were within 0.1% torque of each other from power set until the levers were retarded, no caution or warning was recorded, and the CVR holds no engine call. The propeller damage and the RPM decay both began at the moment of impact, after the reject decision.

Four northern edge lights were measured on 25/06/2026, seven days after the occurrence, at 118% to 131% of the nominal intensity for stage 3, in the section the animals crossed. It is likely that they were at that intensity on the night, because nothing was done to the lighting in between. Whether that intensity delayed detection of the animals remains under examination. The influence is as likely as not on present evidence. The pilot in command recalled the lighting as brighter than wanted, a corridor of light with everything outside it black. The first officer, who saw the animals, described the lighting as normal and saw them clearly once on the seal, although they added that two daylight landings give them little to compare against. Darkness alone explains why nothing off the seal was seen, and nothing links a raised edge light intensity to detection of an animal. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether any edge light intensity delayed detection of the animals]

A matter that could not be settled

Whether the flight crew were fatigued at the time of the take-off could not be determined either way, and the evidence is evenly balanced. The take-off was 11 hours into the duty, on the fifth sector, after a 06:30 sign-on, and the day had been extended from three sectors to five when the crew took the evening rotation from a crew member who reported unfit. Both crew described themselves as tired. Against that, both described the tiredness as ordinary and not impairing. The duty was planned at 13 hours against a 13 hour 30 minute maximum, and the pilot in command's duty and rest in the preceding 7 days were within limits. The recorded performance shows none of the usual signs, with 0.6 seconds from decision to flight idle, maximum braking at 2 seconds, callouts made on time and no omission on the roll. Settling it would need a detailed fatigue assessment with sleep history and the duty pattern before 18/06/2026, which was not carried out. Even had fatigue been present, it is unlikely to have influenced the occurrence, because the recorded data contradict each way it could have acted and no document prompted the one decision it might have touched. What we found

This section sets out the factors that contributed to the runway overrun, other factors that increased risk without contributing to it, and the remaining findings, including two matters that could not be resolved.

Contributing safety factors

The factors below are given in the order in which each led to the next.

  • Failed northern fence. The aerodrome's works register recorded the northern boundary fence as failed in 04/2023, with animals observed passing through. Replacement was estimated at $41,000 and was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 budget rounds. On 23/06/2026 the investigator found a continuous 140 m section with a clear animal pad through it at the 1,050 m mark, 138 m before the first carcass. Closing that route was very likely to have kept these animals off the aerodrome. Whether a replacement fence would have stopped kangaroos was not established. (Safety issue)
  • No documented wildlife plan. Aerodrome Standard AS-139 10.4.2 requires a documented wildlife hazard management plan only where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes above 27,000 kg maximum take-off weight or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements a year. Tarlton Springs' heaviest scheduled aeroplane is 22,900 kg and it handled 61,400 passenger movements in 2025, so it held no plan and was not in breach. Without a plan there was no hazard assessment behind the fence deferrals, and the fence was treated as an ordinary works item. It is likely that this absence contributed to the fence remaining unrepaired. A plan would not by itself have funded the repair. (Safety issue)
  • Departure in the logged kangaroo peak. The aerodrome's sighting log held 41 kangaroo entries, and 38 of them were timed between one hour before and two hours after last light. Eleven recorded animals on the sealed surface. The take-off roll fell inside that window. It is likely that this timing exposed the departure to animals on the runway. The log records sightings rather than presence, and aerodrome staff go off duty part way through the window, so the pattern may partly reflect when someone was there to see.
  • Inspection criterion in the operator's manual. Operations Manual Part A 8.14.2 requires the pilot in command to request a runway inspection before the first departure after last light at an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, and not to start the roll until the result is passed. An aerodrome is listed only where a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft was reported there in the preceding 24 months. Nine aerodromes were listed and Tarlton Springs never had been. The eleven sightings of kangaroos on the sealed surface could not put it on the list, because they were not strikes and were not reported to the company. The criterion was therefore very likely to have left the control unarmed at this aerodrome. (Safety issue)
  • No runway inspection requested. Neither the cockpit voice recording nor the aerodrome's CTAF recording contains a request for an inspection or any mention of wildlife before departure. The only inspection that day was before the first arrival, and the aerodrome reporting officer confirmed one would have been carried out if asked. Both crew said they did not consider it, and the pilot in command had expected the aerodrome to be listed. It is likely that an inspection would have found or dispersed the animals, although an inspection some minutes before the roll cannot guarantee the runway stays clear.
  • Evening service approved without reopening the risk assessment. Route risk assessment RRA-041 issue 3 carried the restriction "daylight operations only pending review of night lighting and diversion fuel policy". Change record CMR-2025-118, approved on 22/05/2025, introduced a scheduled evening rotation from 14/07/2025 as a "schedule change only", recorded "no new hazard identified", and answered "No" to route risk assessment review required. RRA-041 was not reopened and no issue 4 exists. It is likely that this approval led to the roll in darkness. The restriction names night lighting and diversion fuel rather than wildlife, so a review might have approved the service with conditions that left the departure time unchanged. The approver has left the company and could not be asked. (Safety issue)
  • Roll in darkness. The roll began 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight, with the moon 14% illuminated and moonrise not until 22:41. The investigator's site visit at the same time of day confirmed that the unlit strip either side of the sealed surface cannot be seen from the centreline. The first officer described the animals as coming "out of the dark". It is likely that the darkness delayed sighting until the animals were on the lit surface. The first officer did see them at around 200 m once they were on the seal, and how long they had been on the strip before that is unknown.
  • No training or guidance for an obstruction during the roll. Training syllabus TRG-SYL-07 module 4 has four scripted scenarios, all aircraft system events, and no crew in the five training cycles on file has been presented with an external obstruction. Part B 4.6 states that at or above V1 the take-off shall be continued and contains no guidance on an obstruction at any speed. Neither document tells a crew that an obstruction struck at or after V1 can remove several of the assumptions behind the continue decision. The pilot in command said they made the decision "cold, in about a second". It is likely that this gap contributed to the reject. The pilot in command also stated that they knew the rule and departed from it deliberately, which is why this factor is held below the strongest findings. (Safety issue)
  • Take-off rejected 6 kt above V1. The flight data recorder showed V1 called at 112 kt and both power levers retarded at 118 kt, three seconds and six knots past V1, with 695 m of sealed runway remaining. The scheduled accelerate-stop distance of 1,780 m against an available 1,847 m assumes the stop begins at or before V1, and the 67 m margin did not cover a later start. It is extremely likely that this late reject contributed to the overrun. Rejecting was probably the safest course available. The performance assessment found that a continued take-off would have struck the animals at take-off power, that the damaged No. 2 propeller could not have feathered, and that the one-engine climb would have been about 1.0% against the 2.4% minimum, short of the mast beyond the runway end. That conclusion rests on damage done at flight idle and 114 kt, and a feathered propeller would have left about 3.2%. It does not make the decision compliant with Part B 4.6.4, and it does not show that the pilot in command could have known it in the second available.
  • Two kangaroos on the sealed runway. Two adult kangaroos were recovered at 1,188 m and 1,221 m, and the first officer called them while crossing from the northern side. The call was the only trigger for the reject on the cockpit voice recording. It is extremely likely that their presence led both to the reject and to the strike.
  • Strike on the No. 2 propeller and right main gear. The impact came about one second after the reject began, at 114 kt. Three No. 2 propeller blades were damaged beyond limits and the pitch change actuator was cracked through. The damaged propeller could not reach ground idle or reverse, and the No. 2 reverse interlock did not make. This is virtually certain.
  • Deceleration below the scheduled performance. The mean deceleration from maximum braking was 2.6 m/s², approximately 15% below the value in the certified data, with the No. 2 propeller near flight idle pitch and reverse on No. 1 only. The stop needed 757 m against 695 m available, where the certified deceleration from the same point would have needed roughly 660 m. This is virtually certain. Neither the late start nor the reduced deceleration alone would have carried the aircraft to the end, and the two together did. The aircraft left the sealed surface at approximately 21 kt and stopped 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

Other factors that increased risk

These two factors did not influence this occurrence but remain findings worth acting on.

  • Evening rotation rostered close to the duty limit. The evening rotation operated on 289 days between 14/07/2025 and 18/06/2026. Each time it was rostered as the fourth and fifth sectors of a duty planned at between 12 hours 45 minutes and 13 hours 15 minutes, against a 13 hour 30 minute maximum. On six days a crew member reported unfit and a crew already on duty took the rotation, extending that crew's day from three sectors to five. 18/06/2026 was the sixth, with take-off 11 hours into the duty. This is unlikely to have influenced the occurrence, because the recorded response was at the fast end of the fleet's distribution and no error of omission was recorded. The one decision a long duty could plausibly have affected, not requesting an inspection, was never prompted by any document. The standing exposure remains, and its importance is likely. (Safety issue)
  • Floor escape path lighting checked only every 24 months. The pre-flight test on this type illuminates the ceiling emergency lights and exit signs but does not illuminate or monitor the floor proximity segments, whose only check is a functional test at the 24-month interval. A functional check on 24/06/2026 found two of the six segments aft of row 16 on the left side dark, traced to a failed common ballast. The last functional test was 11 months before the occurrence. No evacuation was commanded and the disembarkation took 4 minutes 8 seconds with the main cabin lights on, so it is unlikely that the gap or the defect influenced the overrun or its outcome. The gap is very likely to matter in a different event, because the true state of the escape path between functional tests is unknown. (Safety issue)

Other findings

  • Maximum braking was established 2 seconds after the decision, against a fleet mean of 3.2 seconds. The anti-skid was active on all four wheels and brake energy reached 84% of the certified maximum without a brake fire. The first officer made the speed callouts and the runway-end call. A response at the fleet mean would have carried the aircraft an estimated further 30 to 40 m, close to the far edge of the runway end safety area. The aircraft stopped 62 m into the 90 m area, upright and on the extended centreline, and the passengers disembarked without an evacuation.
  • Tailwind and a No. 2 engine power loss before the reject were each considered and were not found to contribute. Both are very unlikely. The automatic weather observations and the recorded aircraft data agree on a headwind component of 4 to 6 kt throughout. Both engines were within 0.1% of each other until the reject, and the No. 2 propeller damage and speed decay began only at the moment of impact.
  • Both flight crew were qualified and current. The pilot in command had 8,410 hours, 2,140 on type, and the first officer 1,655 hours, 405 on type, and each had a satisfactory last simulator check. The pilot in command's duty and rest in the previous 7 days were within limits, the duty on the day was planned inside the maximum, and both drug and alcohol tests were negative.
  • Whether the flight crew were fatigued could not be determined. The take-off was 11 hours into a duty that had been extended that morning, and both crew described themselves as tired. Both also described the tiredness as ordinary and not impairing, and the recorded performance showed none of the usual signs. What is being done

This section sets out the action taken or proposed against each safety issue, and its current status.

Evening service and the daylight-only restriction. The route risk assessment for Tarlton Springs restricted operations to daylight, and the evening service was approved without reopening it. The Head of Flying Operations suspended the evening Tarlton Springs service after the occurrence, and the suspension was in place from 19/06/2026. [NOT ESTABLISHED: any safety action recorded for reopening RRA-041]

Runway inspection after last light. Operations Manual Part A 8.14.2 required a runway inspection request before the first departure after last light only at aerodromes listed in Appendix 8A, and Tarlton Springs was not listed because no company wildlife strike had been reported there (Figure 1). The Head of Flying Operations amended Appendix 8A by temporary revision TR-2026-04 on 26/06/2026 to list Tarlton Springs. The 8.14.2 requirement therefore applies to every departure there after last light pending the permanent change, and this action is closed. The investigator in charge also recommended, on 02/07/2026, that the operator replace the listing criterion so an aerodrome is listed on wildlife exposure rather than on a reported strike, and add a route risk assessment trigger for any aerodrome first operated into after last light.
