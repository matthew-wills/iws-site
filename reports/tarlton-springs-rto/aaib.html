Synopsis

On 18/06/2026 Saltbush Air SB512, a 50-seat twin-turboprop registered VH-SBQ, rejected its take-off from runway 09 at Tarlton Springs Aerodrome and overran the runway end. All times are local time (UTC+10). The roll began at 17:30:02, 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight. V1 was called at 112 kt, and at 17:30:31 the first officer called kangaroos on the runway. The take-off was rejected at 118 kt, 6 kt above V1, with 695 m of sealed surface remaining. The No. 2 propeller and right main landing gear struck two kangaroos at 114 kt one second later, and No. 2 reverse was then unavailable. The aircraft left the runway end at approximately 21 kt at 17:30:51 and stopped at 17:30:57, 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

There were 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew on board. There were no serious injuries, and one cabin crew member reported bruising during the disembarkation. The aircraft was substantially damaged, including the No. 2 propeller, the right inboard flap, the wing lower surface, the right main gear doors, both right main tyres and the lower right fuselage. Two kangaroos were killed and the aerodrome closed for 4 hours 20 minutes. [NOT ESTABLISHED: owner of VH-SBQ, the engine type and the regulator that received the notification]

The investigation found that the overrun needed two shortfalls together. The reject began 3 seconds and 6 kt after V1, which was very likely a contributor to the overrun because the scheduled accelerate-stop margin of 67 m assumed a reject at or before V1. The strike also left the stop with a deceleration about 15% below the certified value, and with virtual certainty this contributed to the overrun as well. At the certified deceleration the stop would have needed about 660 m. Rejecting was probably the safest course available, although that did not lower the reject's contribution to the overrun. Operations Manual Part B 4.6 and the recurrent training contained no guidance or scenario for an obstruction on the runway, which probably contributed to the reject above V1.

The animals probably reached the runway through a northern perimeter fence that had been recorded as failed in 04/2023 and deferred at three budget rounds, and the aerodrome's lack of a documented wildlife plan probably contributed to that deferral. The departure fell inside the period in which the aerodrome's log recorded most kangaroo movement, and in darkness, both of which probably contributed. No runway inspection was requested, which was probably a contributor and was very likely linked to Appendix 8A, which listed an aerodrome only after a reported company wildlife strike. The evening service began without the daylight-only route risk assessment being reopened, and that probably contributed to the roll in darkness. The roster of the evening rotation and the monitoring of floor escape path lighting did not influence this occurrence. The effect of edge light intensity and of crew fatigue could not be settled.

After the occurrence the evening service was suspended and Appendix 8A was amended by temporary revision TR-2026-04 to list Tarlton Springs. The aerodrome operator funded the 140 m fence replacement, programmed for 09/2026, and installed a temporary electric wire. Two recommendations are open, with responses due 30/09/2026. They concern an Appendix 8A criterion based on wildlife exposure, and guidance and simulator training for an obstruction during the take-off roll.

Factual information

This section sets out the facts of the occurrence under the headings that the material supports.

History of the flight

The aerodrome's only runway inspection on 18/06/2026 was carried out at 07:35 before the first scheduled arrival, and its recorded result was "movement area clear". No further inspection was carried out or requested that day. SB512, the last scheduled sector of the day, was the fifth sector of the crew's duty. The evening rotation had been added to that duty on the morning of 18/06/2026 when a member of the rostered crew reported unfit. The aircraft carried 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew. The doors were closed at 17:16 and the aircraft was scheduled off blocks at 17:20.

Tarlton Springs has no control tower, so the crew made their own broadcasts on the common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF). At 17:21:50 the crew keyed the pilot-activated runway lighting with three transmissions, and the pilot in command remarked on the cockpit voice recorder (CVR) that the lights had a thirty-minute timer. The crew taxied for runway 09 and made a lining-up broadcast at 17:29:10. The automatic weather information broadcast the crew heard gave the wind as 090 at 6 kt. The CTAF recording contains no request for a runway inspection, no offer of one and no mention of wildlife before departure. The captain told the investigation that wildlife was not discussed with anyone during the turnaround.

The first officer was pilot flying. Power was set at 17:30:02, with both engines at 98.4% and 98.5% torque, and the 80 kt cross-check at 17:30:15 was normal. V1, 112 kt, was called at 17:30:29. At 17:30:31 the first officer called the animals, saying "Roos - roos, roos, on the left, they're on the -", and VR, 116 kt, was reached in the same second. No rotate call was made. At 17:30:32, at 118 kt, the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control." and the first officer replied "You have control." Both power levers were at flight idle within 0.6 seconds, 6 kt and 3 seconds past V1, with 695 m of sealed runway remaining.

The first officer had seen two kangaroos crossing the runway from the northern side to the south, at an estimated range of around 200 m at first sight. At 17:30:33 the aircraft struck both animals at 114 kt. The right main landing gear and the No. 2 propeller took the impact. Maximum braking was established at 17:30:34. Reverse was selected on No. 1 only at 17:30:36, and the pilot in command said that number two was not coming. The aircraft left the sealed surface at 17:30:51 at 21 kt and stopped at 17:30:57, 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area (RESA), upright and on the extended centreline, with the nose wheel in graded gravel. There was no fire.

After the stop the pilot in command asked whether everyone was alright, set the parking brake, and shut both engines down at 17:31:26. At 17:32:31 the pilot in command asked the first officer whether they had seen the animals coming. The first officer replied that they "came out of the dark on the left of the strip" and that there had been nothing to see and then two of them. The pilot in command said "I never had them. I was heads-in on the engine page after V one." The aerodrome reporting officer activated the aerodrome emergency plan at 17:32:10 at the pilot in command's request on the CTAF, dispatched a vehicle and broadcast the runway as obstructed and the aerodrome closed. No injuries were reported at that stage and no assistance was requested. The passengers disembarked by the forward airstair between 17:34:40 and 17:38:48.

The pilot in command submitted an air safety report, which is protected and is used here by role only. In it the pilot in command described judging that continuing would leave the aircraft airborne at night with damage that could not be assessed, over country with no usable option. The pilot in command said that the stop above V1 was not a considered departure from procedure, that all previous stop or go training had concerned engine problems, and that no training had addressed an object on the runway after V1. The pilot in command also said that no company document had prompted a request for a runway inspection at Tarlton Springs. In interview the pilot in command and the first officer each described themselves as tired at that point in the duty. Two further interviews are not finalised and nothing is drawn from them here.

Recorded information

The flight data recorder (FDR) data were reported in FDA-2026-0417-R1, issued 24/06/2026. They showed both engines at take-off torque, within 0.1% of each other, from power set until the power levers were retarded at 17:30:32. No engine parameter exceedance, caution or warning was recorded before that point, and fuel flow, ITT and propeller RPM on both engines were within normal scatter. The recorded wind gave a headwind component of 4 to 6 kt throughout, with no tailwind component at any point.

The reject began at 118 kt, three seconds after V1 and six knots above it. Power lever movement to flight idle took 0.6 seconds and maximum braking was established 2 seconds after the decision, against a fleet mean of 3.2 seconds from decision to maximum braking, which is at the fast end of the operator's recorded distribution for rejected take-offs. At impact the torque had fallen to 12% and 9%, and the data show a lateral acceleration spike of 0.31 g right and a vertical spike of 1.48 g. Anti-skid was active on all four wheels. The No. 2 propeller RPM decayed from the moment of impact, the No. 2 reverse interlock did not make, and the No. 2 blade angle stayed near the flight idle value for the rest of the stop.

Distance reconstruction from recorded groundspeed put the aircraft 1,152 m along runway 09 at the reject, which left 695 m of sealed surface. The stopping distance actually achieved from 118 kt was 757 m. The mean deceleration from 17:30:34 to 17:30:44 was 2.6 m/s2, which was approximately 15% below the value used in the certified accelerate-stop data for this weight and runway condition. In the graded gravel it fell to 1.9 m/s2.

Injuries to persons

There were no serious injuries. One cabin crew member reported bruising during the disembarkation. The aircraft carried 44 passengers, 2 flight crew and 1 cabin crew. [NOT ESTABLISHED: minor injury and uninjured counts by category beyond the single bruising report]

Damage to aircraft

The No. 2 propeller had three blades damaged beyond repairable limits, with leading edge deformation and one blade tip separated, and animal tissue was recovered from two blade roots. The pitch change actuator dome was cracked through and its oil lost. The engine was subject to a shock-load inspection, and a borescope of both engines found no pre-existing damage.

On the right inboard flap, which was at the take-off setting at impact, the lower skin was crushed and torn over 0.9 m of span. The wing lower surface aft of the No. 2 nacelle had two punctures, of 30 mm and 55 mm, with no damage to the main spar and no fuel leak. The right main gear doors were distorted, and the forward door was bowed 85 mm outboard against a running clearance to the nacelle of 22 mm. Both right main tyres deflated through the fuse plugs. The brake energy absorbed was calculated at 84% of the certified maximum, with no brake fire and no hydraulic leak, and the anti-skid system tested serviceable. The lower right fuselage skin had abrasion and two punctures consistent with propeller debris. The structures survey separated the strike damage from the overrun damage, and the overrun damage was confined to the nose gear, its doors and the lower forward fuselage.

Two kangaroos were killed. The aerodrome was closed for 4 hours 20 minutes. The aircraft stopped 28 m short of the end of the prepared safety area, and the terrain beyond it is ungraded.

  • "10.4.2 The operator of a certified aerodrome must prepare, implement and maintain a documented wildlife hazard management plan where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes having a maximum take-off weight exceeding 27,000 kg, or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements in a calendar year."

Organisational and management information

Section 8.14 of the Operations Manual Part A provides, in part (Figure 1):

  • "8.14.2 At an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, the pilot in command shall request a runway inspection from the aerodrome operator prior to the first departure after last light, and shall not commence the take-off roll until the result of that inspection has been passed."
  • "8.14.3 At all other aerodromes, no specific action is required. Crews should remain alert to the possibility of wildlife on the movement area."

Appendix 8A lists an aerodrome where "a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft has been reported at that aerodrome within the preceding 24 months". As current on 18/06/2026 it listed nine aerodromes. Tarlton Springs was not among them and had never been listed. The list was last reviewed on 03/03/2025. Section 4.6 of Part B, which covers the rejected take-off, provides in part (Figure 4):

  • "4.6.3 Between 80 kt and V1 the take-off shall be rejected only for: fire or fire warning; engine failure; a configuration warning; loss of directional control; or the pilot in command's judgement that the aircraft is unsafe or unable to fly."
  • "4.6.4 At or above V1 the take-off shall be continued. The decision to continue is made before V1 is reached."

Section 4.6 contains no guidance on an obstruction seen on the runway at any speed, and no amplification of 4.6.4 for a case in which continuing is itself hazardous.

Survival aspects and emergency response

There was no fire. No evacuation was commanded and no slides were deployed, and passengers disembarked by the forward airstair 4 minutes 8 seconds after the aircraft stopped, with the main cabin lights on throughout. The aerodrome reporting officer activated the aerodrome emergency plan at 17:32:10, and aerodrome staff attended with a vehicle.

During the disembarkation the first officer noticed sections of the aisle floor strip unlit while walking back through the cabin. A functional check of the cabin emergency lighting on 24/06/2026, raised following the first officer's account, found that with the system armed and the aircraft on battery power, two of the six floor proximity escape path segments on the left side aft of row 16 did not illuminate. The fault was traced to a failed ballast common to both segments. The pre-flight emergency lighting test on this type illuminates the ceiling emergency lights and the exit signs and does not illuminate or monitor the floor proximity segments. The last dedicated functional test was 11 months before the occurrence, within the maintenance programme's 24-month interval. The disembarkation was conducted without urgency, and the check recorded that nothing about the defect affected what happened on the night. Whether the same ballast part is fitted across the fleet is with engineering and the significance of the monitoring interval is still being examined.

Tests and research

Performance assessment PEA-2026-0417 was issued on 01/07/2026 by Saltbush Air Performance Engineering, using the type certificate holder's drag data and the damage found in ESR-2026-0417. It reconstructed a take-off continued at 17:30:32, which did not happen. VR was reached at 17:30:31, and a continued take-off would have rotated from about 17:30:31.5 at about 3 degrees a second. From the reject point at 1,152 m the aircraft would have reached the impact point, about 1,210 m, within a few hundredths of a second of the time it actually did, at about 120 kt and 4 to 5 degrees nose-up, with the main wheels still on the runway. Liftoff would have followed at about 17:30:34, about 1,300 m along the runway. The No. 2 propeller tip would have been about 1.3 m above the runway, against a measured standing height of 1.6 m for the larger animal, and a strike on the No. 2 propeller and the right main gear was assessed as close to certain, at take-off power rather than flight idle.

For the undamaged aircraft at 20,800 kg, flap 10, 412 ft and +8 °C, the one-engine-inoperative second-segment gradient was 4.1% gross and 3.3% net, against the 2.4% minimum, and the net path would have cleared the mast by about 200 ft. The take-off was limited by accelerate-stop distance, not by climb. The assessment then gave six cases, with gross and net gradients and heights at the mast, where the net path must pass 219 ft:

  • Case A, undamaged as planned: 4.1% gross and 3.3% net, clearing the mast by about 200 ft.
  • Case B, propeller feathered, gear up, doors open, flap damaged: 3.2% gross and 2.4% net, clearing the mast by about 90 ft. The assessment noted that a feathered propeller was not possible after the damage found.
  • Case C, central case with the propeller unfeathered, gear up, doors open, flap damaged: 1.0% gross and 0.2% net, with an expected path about 130 ft, about 55 ft below the top of the mast.
  • Case D, as C at the favourable end of every range: 1.9% gross and 1.1% net, with an expected path about 250 ft and a net path about 140 ft, short of the 219 ft required.
  • Case E, propeller unfeathered, gear down, flap damaged: -0.2% gross and -1.0% net, with no climb.
  • Case F, as E at the unfavourable end of every range: -1.3% gross and -2.1% net, with no climb.

In every case in which the No. 2 propeller does not feather, the gradient was below the 2.4% minimum and the net flight path did not clear the mast by 35 ft. The assessment did not assess controllability at V2 with the right flap damaged and the right propeller windmilling, or the effect of the propeller's imbalance. It also noted that RRA-041 records no alternate within 200 nm at night, so that after a continued take-off the aircraft would have had to return to Tarlton Springs, at night and over unlit country, to a runway with the two carcasses on it. The climb paths are shown against the mast (Figure 6).

Structures report ESR-2026-0417 was issued on 29/06/2026 and propeller teardown report PTR-2026-0417-02 on 30/06/2026. The teardown found the pitch change actuator dome cracked through and the oil transfer tube fractured, with the pitch change oil lost. With the actuator breached the blades cannot be driven to feather or into the ground and reverse range, and the pitch lock holds them at or near the angle they had when oil pressure was lost. The report gave this as the reason the No. 2 reverse interlock did not make, and stated that the propeller could not have been feathered after the impact. With one blade tip gone the propeller is severely out of balance while it turns, and the teardown did not assess what sustained imbalance would have done to the engine mounts or the nacelle. The structures survey assessed retraction of the right main gear as possible but not assured and door closure as not possible, because the forward door is linked to the gear leg and would have met the nacelle structure on retraction. It could not establish whether the leg would have reached its uplock with the door fouled, or whether the right inboard flap would have retracted past the torn fairing. All of the damage was done at flight idle and 114 kt, and the teardown noted that at take-off power and a higher speed the propeller would have absorbed more energy at impact and that it could not say whether the pitch change mechanism would have failed in the same way.

Analysis

The analysis follows the chain from the organisational conditions through the local conditions to the strike and the stop, and it first sets out what the evidence excluded.

What the analysis had to settle, and what the evidence excluded

The analysis had to explain how two kangaroos came to be on a lit runway at 17:30 on 18/06/2026, why no one looked for them, why the take-off was rejected 6 kt above V1, and why the aircraft then stopped 62 m beyond the runway end.

Several matters were not in question. The crew held current qualifications. The pilot in command had 8,410 hours, 2,140 of them on type, with a satisfactory simulator check on 11/03/2026 and a satisfactory line check on 02/12/2025. The first officer had 1,655 hours, 405 on type, and a satisfactory simulator check on 28/04/2026. Both crew tested negative for drugs and alcohol. The brakes were serviceable. The anti-skid system tested serviceable after the occurrence, and no brake fire or hydraulic leak was found.

Two hypotheses were examined and excluded. A tailwind was very unlikely because the automatic observations gave a headwind component of 5 to 7 kt and the recorded wind gave 4 to 6 kt throughout, with no tailwind at any point. A power loss on No. 2 engine before the reject was very unlikely because both engines held 98.4% and 98.5% torque, within 0.1% of each other, until 17:30:32. No caution, warning or exceedance was recorded before that point, the borescope of both engines found no pre-existing damage, and the cockpit voice recorder holds no engine-related call. The No. 2 propeller damage and RPM decay began at 17:30:33, after the reject, so they followed from the strike and did not cause the reject.

Why animals reached a lit runway in the dark

Aerodrome Standard AS-139 10.4.2 requires a documented wildlife hazard management plan only where the aerodrome is used by aeroplanes above 27,000 kg maximum take-off weight or serves more than 350,000 passenger movements in a calendar year. Tarlton Springs' heaviest scheduled aeroplane was 22,900 kg and it handled 61,400 passenger movements in 2025, so neither limb applied. The aerodrome held no plan, no hazard assessment and no dispersal programme, and it was not in breach. Section 10.4.1 still obliged the operator to take reasonable measures, and a sighting log that nobody reviewed was the only record of wildlife.

The absence of a plan probably contributed to what followed. The failed northern fence sat in the works register as an ordinary maintenance item, and no hazard assessment stood behind it when the council deferred it. Item WR-221, raised in 04/2023, recorded four displaced panels and strained wire between the 700 m and 1,300 m markers, with animals observed passing through, and estimated replacement at $41,000. It was deferred at the 2023, 2024 and 2025 budget rounds, each time as "defer, unfunded, review next cycle". The deferrals were budget decisions, and a plan does not of itself fund a fence, so there is no evidence that a plan would have been funded any better. The contribution of the missing plan is therefore held at likely and below that of the other organisational factors, because that contrary argument is not answered by the evidence.

The unrepaired fence was the physical route onto the movement area. WR-263, raised in 02/2026, recorded the section as deteriorating further. The site inspection on 23/06/2026 found a continuous 140 m section with the bottom two wires separated from the posts, and a clear animal pad through the fence at the 1,050 m mark (Figure 2). That pad lies 138 m before the first carcass at 1,188 m and on the same side of the runway (Figure 3). The route is the physical evidence. What an intact fence would have done remains inference, because WR-221 gave no specification, nothing shows that a replacement would exclude kangaroos, which can clear a stock fence, and the sighting log does not record where any of its 41 kangaroos came onto the aerodrome. Even so, an intact fence would have closed the one route the evidence shows these animals used, and it is likely that the unrepaired fence contributed to the animals reaching the runway. The time of the departure added to the exposure. The sighting log for 01/01/2025 to 18/06/2026 holds 46 entries, 41 of them kangaroos on or adjacent to the movement area. Of those 41, 38 were timed between one hour before and two hours after last light, and eleven recorded animals on the sealed surface of runway 09/27. Last light on 18/06/2026 was 17:25, so the window ran from 16:25 to 19:25 and the roll at 17:30 fell inside it. The pilot in command's account matched the log: they had seen kangaroos on the strip four or five times, always in the evening. The log records sightings rather than presence, and aerodrome staff go off duty at 18:30, part way through the window, so the pattern may partly reflect when someone was there to see. Even so, it is likely that departing within the logged peak contributed to the animals meeting the aircraft.

On the evidence, two adult kangaroos were on the sealed runway at 1,188 m and 1,221 m from the threshold. The first officer called them at 17:30:31 and the impact followed at 17:30:33, so it is extremely likely that their presence on the surface led to both the reject and the strike. The failed fence, the absence of a plan and the logged evening peak were contributing safety factors, and so causal in the sense used in this report, because they led to the animals being on the runway at that time.

Why no one looked before the roll

Part A 8.14.2 of the operator's Operations Manual requires the pilot in command, at an aerodrome listed in Appendix 8A, to request a runway inspection before the first departure after last light and not to begin the roll until the result is passed (Figure 1). Section 8.14.3 requires no specific action at other aerodromes. Appendix 8A lists an aerodrome only where a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft has been reported there in the preceding 24 months. Nine aerodromes were listed. Tarlton Springs had never been listed because it had had no company strike, and the list had last been reviewed on 03/03/2025, four months before the evening service began. No provision in Part A asks a crew to report a sighting, so eleven sightings on the sealed surface could not put the aerodrome on the list. The control could be triggered only by the outcome it existed to prevent. It is very likely that this criterion contributed to the absence of an inspection.

No inspection was requested. Neither the cockpit voice recorder nor the aerodrome's CTAF recording holds any request for one, any offer of one, or any mention of wildlife before departure. The only inspection that day was at 07:35, before the first arrival, with the result "movement area clear". Aerodrome Manual 6.3 provides for an inspection on the request of a pilot in command, and the reporting officer, who was at the terminal during the turnaround, confirmed that one would have been carried out if asked. SB512 was the only departure after last light that day. An inspection would have covered the 1,050 m to 1,250 m section where the pad and both carcasses were found, and aerodrome staff disperse animals when they find them. An inspection some minutes before the roll could not guarantee that the runway stayed clear, which is why the contribution is not put higher. It is likely that the absence of an inspection contributed to the animals being on the runway at the roll.

Both crew said they did not consider requesting an inspection, and the pilot in command had expected Tarlton Springs to be on the Appendix 8A list and was surprised to find it was not. No document put the question in front of either of them, so the omission is explained by the missing prompt and not by individual error.

Why the departure was after last light

Route risk assessment RRA-041 issue 3, dated 11/2022, is the current assessment for Tarlton Springs. It carried the operating restriction "daylight operations only pending review of night lighting and diversion fuel policy". CMR-2025-118, approved by the Head of Flying Operations on 22/05/2025, added an evening rotation from 14/07/2025, the first company operation into the aerodrome after last light. It assessed the change as "schedule change only", recorded "No new hazard identified" and answered "No" to route risk assessment review required. The restriction was not referenced, RRA-041 was not reopened, no issue 4 exists, and the Safety Department's records show no involvement beyond receipt of the approved record. The process asked whether the change was new, not whether the existing assessment still held.

Honoured, the restriction would have kept every company departure in daylight. The restriction named night lighting and diversion fuel and not wildlife, so a review might have addressed those two items, approved the service with conditions and left the departure after last light unchanged. The approver has left the company and cannot be asked what was in front of them, so what a review would have concluded is inference. A review that assessed a night operation at an aerodrome with a single runway, no lighting redundancy and no alternate within 200 nm at night would very likely have examined the conditions of the evening operation, and it is very likely that the failure to reopen the assessment contributed to SB512 rolling in darkness. The evening service was suspended on 19/06/2026. [NOT ESTABLISHED: why the approver assessed CMR-2025-118 as a schedule change only].

The darkness itself bore on what the crew could see. The roll began at 17:30, 32 minutes after sunset and 5 minutes after the end of civil twilight. Moonrise was not until 22:41 and the moon was 14% illuminated. The only lighting was the runway edge lighting the crew had switched on at 17:21:50. The site visit at the same time of day on 23/06/2026 confirmed that the unlit graded strip either side of the seal cannot be seen from the centreline. The first officer said on the cockpit voice recorder that the animals "came out of the dark on the left of the strip", and told the investigation that they "didn't appear, they arrived". In daylight, animals crossing the graded strip would have been in view before they reached the seal.

The contrary evidence is that the first officer did see the animals, at around 200 m, once they were on the seal, and that how long the animals had been on the strip before that is unknown. Animals that came onto the strip only seconds before reaching the seal would have been seen late in daylight too. The pilot in command was looking at the engine page after V1, where the procedure places them, so a daylight sighting would still have rested on the first officer. These points limit the effect of the darkness without removing it, and it is likely that the darkness contributed to the reject. The decision to reject above V1

Part B 4.6.4 states: "At or above V1 the take-off shall be continued. The decision to continue is made before V1 is reached." V1 was called at 112 kt at 17:30:29. The first officer's sighting call came at 17:30:31 and the pilot in command said "Stopping. I have control." at 17:30:32 (Figure 5). The power levers reached flight idle from 17:30:32 at 118 kt, 3 seconds and 6 kt past V1, with 695 m of sealed surface left. The scheduled accelerate-stop distance of 1,780 m against an accelerate-stop distance available of 1,847 m left a margin of 67 m, and it assumes the reject begins at or before V1. A stop begun 3 seconds and 6 kt late consumed more than that margin, so the reject above V1 was extremely likely to have contributed to the overrun. A continued take-off would have avoided the overrun, and that is the whole of this test.

Neither the training nor the procedure addressed the situation. The four scripted scenarios in TRG-SYL-07 module 4 are engine failure at 90 kt, engine fire at 100 kt, a configuration warning at 85 kt, and engine failure at V1 plus 5 kt, continued. All four are aircraft system events, and none presents an obstruction at any speed. No crew had been presented with an external obstruction scenario in the five cycles on file, and 214 crew completed the module in the current cycle. Part B 4.6 (Figure 4) contains no guidance on an obstruction seen on the runway and no amplification of 4.6.4 for a case in which continuing is itself hazardous. Appendix 4C states that the go case assumes an engine failure at V1 on an otherwise undamaged aeroplane, with the failed propeller feathering automatically and the gear retracted, but neither the appendix nor the syllabus tells a crew that an obstruction struck at or after V1 can remove several of those assumptions at once.

The pilot in command said they knew the rule and had taught it, that it was not a considered departure from procedure, and that every stop or go scenario they had been given had been an engine event. The first officer gave a similar account independently. The contrary reading is that a crew who know the rule are not changed by training they did not need to learn it, and that the pilot in command rejected because they judged the alternative worse. That reading is reasonable, but it does not account for a decision made in about a second against no briefed or practised frame, and an unrehearsed decision made cold differs from one made against a rehearsed one. It is likely that the absence of training and guidance contributed to the reject above V1. The gap applies to the whole fleet and is a safety issue beyond this occurrence. The investigator in charge has recommended guidance and simulator training for an obstruction during the take-off roll, with a response due 30/09/2026.

The performance assessment PEA-2026-0417 reconstructed a continued take-off from the same point (Figure 6). The aircraft would have reached the animals at the same place within a few hundredths of a second, rotating at about 120 kt with the main wheels still on the runway, and a strike on the No. 2 propeller and the right main gear was assessed as close to certain, at take-off power. The teardown found that the damage which stopped the No. 2 reverse would also have prevented feathering. With the propeller windmilling, the gross gradient was about 1.0% in the central case and 1.9% at the favourable end of every range, against the 2.4% minimum, and the net path was short of the mast 3,400 m beyond the runway end. The aircraft would have been airborne at night, with no alternate within 200 nm and two carcasses on the only runway to return to. On that reconstruction, the reject was probably the safest course available.

That conclusion has limits. The damage was done at flight idle and 114 kt, and the teardown report could not say whether the pitch change mechanism would have failed in the same way at take-off power. A propeller that feathered would have left about 3.2%, and a gradient below the minimum does not of itself mean a crash. Controllability at V2 with the right flap damaged and the propeller windmilling was not assessed. The assessment does not reduce the reject's contribution to the overrun and does not make the decision compliant with 4.6.4. It also does not show that the pilot in command could have known its result in the second available.

The strike and the stop

The impact came at 17:30:33 at 114 kt, one second after the reject began and with both engines already near flight idle. The flight data recorder shows a lateral acceleration spike of 0.31 g right and a vertical spike of 1.48 g. Three No. 2 propeller blades were damaged beyond limits, animal tissue was found in two blade roots, and the pitch change actuator was cracked through with its oil lost. With the actuator breached, the blades could not be driven to ground idle or reverse, and the No. 2 reverse interlock did not make. The achieved mean deceleration of 2.6 m/s2 was approximately 15% below the value in the certified data, which assumes both propellers at ground idle. The strike was therefore virtually certain to have reduced the deceleration of the stop, and the reduced deceleration was virtually certain to have contributed to the overrun.

At the certified deceleration from the same point the reconstruction puts the stop at roughly 660 m, inside the 695 m remaining. At the deceleration achieved it needed 757 m. The late start of the stop and the lower deceleration each reached the end of the runway by their own route, and both were needed. The reject above V1 alone, with the certified deceleration, would have stopped on the seal, and the strike alone, with a stop begun at V1, would have had the margin of 67 m available. These were causal factors in the sense used in this report.

One action reduced the outcome. The power levers reached flight idle 0.6 seconds after the decision and maximum braking was established at 17:30:34, 2 seconds after it, against a recorded fleet mean of 3.2 seconds. Anti-skid was active on all four wheels and brake energy reached 84% of the certified maximum, with no brake fire. A stop begun at the fleet mean response would have carried the aircraft an estimated further 30 to 40 m, close to the far edge of the runway end safety area. It is very likely that this response reduced the severity of the outcome. The aircraft left the seal at 21 kt and stopped 62 m into the 90 m safety area, upright and on the extended centreline, 28 m short of the end of the prepared surface.

Factors that did not contribute, or could not be settled

Edge lighting above nominal intensity

A photometric check on 25/06/2026, seven days after the occurrence, measured four northern edge lights between the 900 m and 1,200 m markers at 118% to 131% of nominal intensity for stage 3, in the section the animals crossed. The lighting is pilot-activated and runs at one setting that crews cannot vary. The controller was found at stage 3, the commissioning setting, and no work on the lighting or its controller was recorded between the occurrence and the check. It is therefore likely that the four lights were at the measured intensity on the night, although nobody measured them at the time.

Whether that intensity delayed detection of the animals is still being examined and, on the evidence so far, cannot be determined either way. The pilot in command recalled the lighting as "brighter than I'd want", a corridor of light with everything outside it black. Brighter edge lights would deepen the contrast with the dark strip beside them, which is where the animals came from. The first officer, who saw the animals, described the lighting as normal and said they saw them clearly once the animals were on the seal at around 200 m, although with only two daylight landings to compare against they were not well placed to judge. The pilot in command's non-detection has a procedural explanation, because they were looking at the engine page after V1. Darkness alone explains why nothing off the seal was seen, and no evidence links an edge light at 131% to the detection of an animal. No conclusion is drawn on it.

Flight crew fatigue

Whether the crew were fatigued to a degree that degraded performance was examined and could not be settled, and the evidence is evenly balanced. The take-off was 11 hours into a duty that began with a 06:30 sign-on, on the fifth sector, and the day had been extended from three sectors to five that morning when a member of the rostered crew reported unfit. Both crew described themselves as tired, and the pilot in command said that if asked to fly another sector they would have said no.

Against that, both crew described the tiredness as ordinary and not impairing. The pilot in command said that "for the two seconds that mattered, I was there", and the first officer said it was a normal five-sector day and that they had had a proper break in Port Carrick. The duty was planned at 13 hours against a 13 hour 30 minute maximum, and the pilot in command's duty and rest in the previous 7 days were within limits with no variations. The recorded performance showed none of the usual signs: 0.6 seconds to flight idle, 2 seconds to maximum braking, callouts on time and no error of omission on the roll. Settling the matter would need a detailed fatigue assessment with sleep history and the duty pattern before 18/06/2026, which was judged not required because every mechanism fatigue would have worked through here is contradicted by recorded data. It remains unresolved, and nothing from it is carried into the conclusions.

Evening rotation roster

The evening rotation operated on 289 days between 14/07/2025 and 18/06/2026. Each time it was rostered as the fourth and fifth sectors of a duty signing on between 06:00 and 07:00 and planned at between 12 hours 45 minutes and 13 hours 15 minutes, against a 13 hour 30 minute maximum. On six of those days a crew member reported unfit and the rotation went to a crew already on duty, extending that day from three sectors to five. The occurrence day was the sixth.

This roster was unlikely to have contributed to this occurrence, because the mechanisms it would have worked through are contradicted by recorded data. The response was fast, no error of omission occurred on the roll, and the one decision a long duty might have affected, requesting an inspection, was never prompted by any document. It remains a likely risk because it is a standing exposure on every operation of the rotation, at an aerodrome with no alternate within 200 nm at night and limited engineering support, and it is greatest when a crew member reports unfit.

Floor escape path lighting

The pre-flight emergency lighting test on this type illuminates the ceiling emergency lights and exit signs but not the floor proximity escape path segments, whose only check is a dedicated functional test at a 24-month interval. The check on 24/06/2026, raised after the first officer noticed unlit sections of the aisle floor strip during the disembarkation, found two of the six segments aft of row 16 on the left side dark on battery power. The fault was traced to a failed common ballast. The last functional test was 11 months before the occurrence, within the interval, so neither the crew nor a line engineer could have detected the defect.

On the evidence so far this was unlikely to have influenced the overrun or its outcome, because no evacuation was commanded, the cabin lights were on throughout and the disembarkation took 4 minutes 8 seconds without incident. It is very likely to matter as a monitoring gap, because between functional tests the state of the escape path lighting on any aircraft of the type is unknown and the gap has already hidden one defect on this aircraft. Whether the same ballast part is fitted across the fleet is with engineering, and the matter is still being examined.

Safety action and recommendations

This section sets out the action already taken in response to the occurrence and then the recommendations made where a safety issue remained open.

Safety action taken

After the occurrence the Head of Flying Operations suspended the evening Tarlton Springs service, and the suspension was in place from 19/06/2026. It removed the scheduled departure after last light from Tarlton Springs, which was the exposure created when the evening service was approved without reopening the daylight-only route risk assessment. It is a suspension rather than a change to the approval process, so it answers that safety issue in part only. Nothing in the evidence shows that the change management process, or the route risk assessment that the approval did not reopen, has been altered.

On 26/06/2026 the operator amended Appendix 8A by temporary revision TR-2026-04 to list Tarlton Springs. Part A 8.14.2 therefore applies to every departure from Tarlton Springs after last light, and a crew must request a runway inspection and must not begin the take-off roll until the result is passed. The action is closed.
