Investigation summary

All times in this report are local time (UTC+8).

What happened

At 02:47:12 on 12/08/2026, a 220 t class rigid vehicle (unit 407) descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit at Bindarra Gold Mine, empty at 37 km/h, struck the right side of light vehicle LV-118 as it came out of the RL 312 dewatering spur. The impact speed after braking was 24 km/h. LV-118 was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow.

The light vehicle driver, a grade control technician employed by a survey subcontractor, was conscious throughout. The driver was extricated at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision, and was flown to the metropolitan trauma centre at 07:40 with a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax, a serious injury. LV-118 was destroyed.

What the investigation found

The spur was opened under works order WO-26-4471, classified as routine earthworks, so no management of change record was raised. Nothing was referred to the traffic management plan owner, the survey department or the technical services group, and this very likely contributed to what followed. The in-cab map showed no intersection at RL 312, and the intersection had no sign or stop control although its 41 m sight line was well short of the 96 m stopping distance. The absence of a stop control and warning sign probably contributed to the light vehicle driving out of the spur without stopping.

The collision awareness system was suppressed at the spur by the S4-LOAD geofence, extended up the ramp in June 2026, and operators did not know it. This probably contributed, through an expectation that a quiet system meant an empty ramp, although an unsuppressed alert would have sounded only about half a second after the beacon came into view.

The plan required a positive call only at nine listed intersections and could add one only at the annual review, so the spur was not listed, which very likely contributed to neither driver making a call. The 41 m sight line against the 96 m stopping distance very likely contributed to neither driver knowing of the other until six seconds before impact. Issuing an access card with no check of the pit driving assessment probably contributed to the light vehicle driver not stopping at the spur mouth. Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 defined no significant change and no basis for the call list, and probably contributed by making inaction the default, a finding held at the standard of proof and no higher.

The Stage 4 night roster of fourteen consecutive nights increased risk although it was unlikely to have contributed here, because the recorded response was inside the allowance in the plan. The night shift extrication arrangement is still being examined. Whether airborne dust affected the sighting could not be settled. Whether the driver of the 220 t class vehicle was fatigued was about as likely as not. A wet surface and reduced brake performance were each very unlikely.

The driver of the 220 t class rigid vehicle (unit 407) braked within 1.4 seconds of the beacon coming into view and steered right, which reduced the impact speed from 37 km/h to 24 km/h.

What has been done as a result

The Mining Manager directed at 05:10 on 12/08/2026 that a positive call be made before entering any Stage 4 intersection, listed or not. The RL 312 spur was barricaded and closed, and two of ten other unlisted roads measured were closed pending a stop control. Suppressed proximity events were added to the monthly collision awareness report on 28/08/2026. Recommendations on the works order exclusion, the positive-call list and the geofence boundaries are open.

Safety message

An intersection opened without referral leaves the plan, the map, the signs and the alerts unaware of it. A quiet collision alert system or a missing sign does not mean the road is empty, and light vehicles should stop and look again where sight distance is short.

The occurrence

At 02:47:12 on 12/08/2026, a 220 t class rigid vehicle descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit at Bindarra Gold Mine, empty, struck the right side of a light vehicle, LV-118, as it came out of the RL 312 dewatering spur. The impact speed was 24 km/h. The light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow. Its driver, a grade control technician employed by a survey subcontractor, was seriously injured.

Before the shift

The RL 312 spur was a 240 m access road cut under works order WO-26-4471 to reach dewatering bore DW-14. The works order recorded the earthworks as complete on 31/07/2026. On 02/08/2026 bore DW-14 was energised and the spur was first used by light vehicles. The works log records that the "ROAD CLOSED - CONSTRUCTION" sign at the spur mouth was removed that day, and nothing replaced it. No warning sign, give-way control or delineation was added afterwards.

At about 22:10 on 04/08/2026 a dewatering technician came out of the spur in front of a descending vehicle about 60 m away, reversed back into the spur and let it pass. The technician submitted hazard report HAZ-2026-0917 at 23:48 the same night, and it read in part:

  • "The problem is you cannot see up the ramp from that road. There is a rise in the ramp and a windrow along the side and you are out on the road before you can see anything. There is no sign on the ramp telling the trucks the road is there and there is no give way on our side."
  • "The trucks are doing forty coming down empty. I do not think they know the road is there."

The Dewatering Superintendent closed the report on 06/08/2026 with an entry recording that the dewatering crew had been reminded at the pre-start on 05/08 to take care and to give way to heavy vehicles. The risk rating was reduced from Medium to Low on closure. The report was not referred to the mining department, the contractor, the survey department or the owner of the traffic management plan.

On 09/08/2026 the light vehicle driver's site access card was issued at the gatehouse after a 20 minute briefing on muster points, speed limits and personal protective equipment. No Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment was held or recorded for the driver. The driver worked night shifts on 09/08, 10/08 and 11/08.

The night shift

At 02:22:41 dispatch called Stage 4 back to work from crib. The pit channel was logged continuously from that point. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle called at each listed intersection the vehicle passed, and dispatch acknowledged each call. The driver called at the RL 360 crossover going up at 02:28, at the ROM pad turn-off at 02:37, and at the RL 360 crossover going down at 02:46:21, 51 seconds before the collision. LV-118 made no transmission on the channel that shift beyond logging on at 18:04 at the start of shift. Neither vehicle made a call about the RL 312 spur. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit.

The approach

The driver of LV-118 finished a set of levels at the DW-14 collar and drove the 240 m of spur towards the main ramp, reaching the spur mouth at 02:47:09.2. The driver's movements before the mouth rest on the driver's own account. In that account, as summarised within the investigation, the driver slowed at the mouth, looked right up the ramp, saw nothing and drove out, turning right to go back up the ramp at about 25 km/h. On read-back the driver corrected an earlier statement that the vehicle had stopped, and did not believe it had come to a complete stop.

The 220 t class vehicle was descending empty with the retarder on. Its last steady data point was at 02:47:06.0, at 37 km/h at RL 317, 53 m above the spur mouth. The survey places the first sight of the light vehicle's beacon at that distance. The survey gives the first sight of the spur mouth from the driver's eye height as 41 m. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle recalled the beacon appearing over the crest at about forty metres, whereas the survey and the download put it at 53 m.

At 02:47:07.4, 38.5 m above the spur mouth, service brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa in 0.4 seconds, the retarder went to maximum and 14 degrees of right steering was applied. That was 1.4 seconds after the last steady data point. The driver steered right, towards the wall, because the open side of the road to the left dropped more than 100 m.

At 02:47:09.2 the collision awareness system logged a proximity event at 21.4 m, closing at 32 km/h, with the light vehicle at the spur mouth. The alert was suppressed and nothing was shown in the cab. The in-cab road network, last published on 24/07/2026, showed the main ramp with no intersection at RL 312.

The collision and response

Impact occurred at 02:47:12.0 at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth and 3.1 m out from the toe of the inner windrow. The accelerometer of the 220 t class vehicle logged 1.9 g longitudinal. The light vehicle was crossing the descending lane, turning right, with its right side towards the 220 t class vehicle. It was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp, going over at about 11.2 m, and came to rest on its left side against the inner windrow, facing down-ramp. The 220 t class vehicle stopped at about 02:47:19, 19 m below the intersection.

The driver of the 220 t class vehicle called dispatch at 02:47:41. At 02:48:01 dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic, and at 02:48:44 told the driver the ramp was clear above and below and that it was safe to get out. The driver went to the light vehicle driver, who was conscious and talking, and stayed there until the emergency response team arrived. The light vehicle's door on the resting side was against the ground.

Dispatch paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The first response vehicle entered the pit at 02:51 and reached the scene at 02:54. The second responder arrived at 03:07 with the hydraulic rescue tool. The driver was extricated through the rear of the vehicle at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision, reached the surface medical facility at 03:41 and was flown to the metropolitan trauma centre at 07:40. The driver's injuries were a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax. The timing of the extrication relative to the arrival of the second responder remains under examination.

Physical evidence and damage

The scene examination found the ramp dry, on a 9.1 per cent descending grade. The braked tyre scuff of the 220 t class vehicle began 38.5 m up-ramp of the spur mouth and ran 58 m to its resting position, deviating 1.4 m to the right over its first 20 m. A continuous gouge ran 18.4 m down-ramp from the impact position, changing at 11.2 m from tyre scuff to body panel gouge. Right-side glass and mirror fragments lay from 4.8 m to 14 m down-ramp of the impact position.

Damage to the light vehicle was concentrated on the right side from the B pillar forward, and the vehicle was destroyed. Its roll-over protection structure was deformed inward at the right rear mounting and was not breached. The driver's seatbelt was fastened and the webbing showed loading marks. The 4.5 m beacon whip sheared at its base and was recovered 9 m from the vehicle, and its lamp worked when tested. Damage to the 220 t class vehicle was confined to the front left bumper, the left mudguard and the lower two steps of the access ladder, with nothing above the bumper line. It returned to service on 14/08/2026.

Immediate actions

The Stage 4 pit stood down for 9 hours 15 minutes and the main ramp below RL 340 was closed for 22 hours. The RL 312 spur was barricaded and closed to all traffic. Both vehicles were isolated and left in position, and the scene was photographed and surveyed before recovery. The occurrence was notified to the State Mines Inspectorate as a reportable injury. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle and the night shift supervisor were stood down and tested for drugs and alcohol, and both results were negative. At 05:10 the Mining Manager directed that a positive call on the pit channel be made before entering any intersection in the Stage 4 pit, listed in the plan or not, until further notice.

Systems and documents

Regulatory guideline

Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 revision 4 was issued by the State Mines Inspectorate in 2021 under regulation 6.22 of the Mining Operations Safety Regulations 2018. Compliance with the guideline is one way of discharging the regulation, which requires that "the [the other attendee 1] shall manage the risks arising from the interaction of vehicles at the mine". The guideline provides:

  • "3.2 The [the other attendee 1] shall prepare and maintain a traffic management plan for the mine. The plan shall be reviewed at intervals not exceeding twelve months, and following any significant change to the mine's road network, vehicle fleet or operating pattern." (MSG-14)
  • "3.5 The plan is a site document. It is not approved by, and need not be submitted to, the Inspectorate, except on request following an occurrence or an inspection." (MSG-14)
  • "5.1 The [the other attendee 1] shall nominate the intersections, crossings and other interaction zones at the mine at which positive communication between vehicles is required before entry, and shall record them in the plan." (MSG-14)
  • "5.4 Where a light vehicle and a heavy vehicle may interact, the [the other attendee 1] shall provide for the light vehicle to be detectable by the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1]. Detectability may be provided by line of sight, elevated marking, radio communication, a proximity detection system, or a combination of these." (MSG-14)

The guideline does not define "significant change" and gives no example of one. It does not say who nominates the interaction zones or on what basis, and it sets no minimum. It does not say which of the five means of detectability must be provided, or how many.

Safety analysis

This analysis follows the conditions that allowed a light vehicle and a loaded-route 220 t class vehicle to meet at an unmarked intersection, through the actions of the two drivers, to the impact and its outcome.

How the intersection came to exist without controls

ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2 requires a management of change record "before any of the following: a change to the mine's road network". Clause 5.3 excludes "routine earthworks carried out under a works order within an approved pit design, where no new plant or vehicle type is introduced". Works order WO-26-4471, raised on 28/07/2026, cut 240 m of access road to bore DW-14 at RL 312. It classified the work as routine earthworks within the approved Stage 4 design, recorded management of change as not required under clause 5.3, and left its handover field reading "N/A".

The classification removed the three referrals that clause 5.4 attaches to a management of change record: to the traffic management plan owner, to the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and to the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system configuration. The works order form at Schedule 4 had no equivalent fields. None of the three referrals was made. The in-cab road network last published on 24/07/2026 therefore showed the main ramp with no intersection at RL 312 on the night of the occurrence, and the spur appeared on neither Schedule 1 nor Appendix B of the plan.

The same route had been taken before. Of the 14 new roads cut in Stage 4 since the review of 12/11/2025, three were raised under a management of change record and were referred to the plan's owner, while 11 were raised under a works order and none was referred.

A referral is not in itself a control, because the plan's owner might have left the spur to the annual review, and no out-of-cycle review had been held in the plan's eleven years. However, the referrals were standing parts of the procedure and the three roads that went through it received them. Each referral would have sent the new intersection to someone who could act on a survey showing it failed the clause 6.2 sight distance rule. It is very likely that opening the spur under a works order, with nothing referred to anyone, contributed to the absence of a stop control and warning signs and to the collision alerts being suppressed at the spur.

The external guideline

MSG-14 clause 3.2 requires the traffic management plan to be reviewed within twelve months "and following any significant change to the mine's road network, vehicle fleet or operating pattern". The guideline does not define a significant change and gives no example. Clause 5.1 requires the mine to nominate the intersections at which positive communication is required, and sets no minimum and no basis for the nomination. Clause 3.5 makes the plan a site document that is not approved by the Inspectorate. Bindarra's plan met the guideline on its face, because it had been reviewed within twelve months and it nominated nine interaction zones, so the mine was not in breach. Clause 5.4 of MSG-14, which allows detectability to be provided by any one of five means, is context only and is a different provision from clause 5.4 of ORR-PRO-022.

A defined trigger would have required the plan to be reopened when a road was added, and a stated basis for nomination would have given an intersection failing the sight distance rule a claim on the list. Against that, the mine could have held an out-of-cycle review under its own clause 9.1 at any time and could have listed every intersection it had, so the guideline did not prevent either step. The Investigation concluded that the guideline probably contributed by making inaction the default, and this is held at likely and no higher. It is the weakest of the organisational findings, and the organisational failure within the mine carried most of the weight.

Positive calls

BIN-TMP-004 clause 4.3.1 requires a positive call on the pit channel before entering any intersection listed at Appendix B. Clause 4.3.2 provides that at other intersections vehicles give way under clause 4.1 and "No call is required." Appendix B listed nine intersections and was reissued only at the annual review. Its note allowed an intersection to be added only where it appeared on Schedule 1, and Schedule 1 was also reissued only at the annual review. No provision allowed an addition between reviews, and none required a new road to be referred to the plan's owner. The spur was on neither document. The next date on which it could have been listed was 12/11/2026, three months after the collision.

The pit channel recording shows the practice that followed from this. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle called, and was acknowledged, at the RL 360 crossover going up at 02:28 and coming down at 02:46:21, and at the ROM pad turn-off at 02:37. The last call was 51 seconds before the collision. No call was made about the spur. The light vehicle's only transmission on the channel that shift was logging on at 18:04, at the start of the shift. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle, the light vehicle driver and the night shift supervisor each independently described the same practice: calls were made at the nine intersections on the crib room board and nowhere else. The supervisor said they had never corrected anyone for not calling elsewhere and that calls everywhere would make the channel unusable. The training file of the driver of the 220 t class vehicle shows a 23 slide traffic management induction module that names the nine listed intersections and does not say what to do at an unlisted one.

Local practice therefore matched the document exactly, and the rule applied as written. It is very likely that the requirement for calls only at listed intersections, with additions only at the annual review, contributed to neither driver making a call before entering the intersection. A call from either vehicle would have been heard by the other, by dispatch and by the supervisor, because the channel was open and the light vehicle was on it. The absence of a call itself likely contributed, though not with certainty, because a call gives knowledge and not separation. The two vehicles would still have had to resolve who went first over 41 m of sight line, and a call that was heard but not answered would still have left a driver deciding whether to proceed.

The collision awareness system

The collision awareness system on the Stage 4 fleet operated at Level 8, advisory only, and Level 9 intervention was available and not enabled. Advisory alerts were raised when a tag entered the 60 m ring above a 10 km/h closing rate, except inside a suppression geofence, where no alert was raised at any range and a proximity event was logged only below 25 m. The S4-LOAD geofence was drawn on 14/03/2025 around the loading face to stop nuisance alerts. On 09/06/2026 it was extended northward when the face advanced, and the boundary was carried up the main ramp for the first time, to a point 180 m up-ramp of the RL 312 intersection. The extension was a system configuration task by the contractor's technical services group, was not recorded in the plan and was not reviewed against it. The spur, cut seven weeks later, joined the ramp inside the suppressed area.

The system logged the light vehicle at 02:47:09.2 at 21.4 m and 32 km/h closing, and suppressed the alert, so nothing was presented in the cab. In the 12 months before the occurrence the 220 t class vehicle raised 1,684 advisory alerts, all outside the geofence. Across the fleet, 68 proximity events inside the geofence were suppressed, nine of them involving a light vehicle on the main ramp below the boundary, and all nine were dated after 09/06/2026. The suppressed events were recorded in the system but the technical services group's standing report did not include them, so nobody had looked.

The driver of the 220 t class vehicle said in interview: "I assumed there was nothing to go off at. You don't see light vehicles on that ramp at night, so if the thing's quiet, the road's empty." The driver added that nobody had told them the system was switched off there and that they learned it from the supervisor afterwards. The supervisor knew the system was off at the dig from about the middle of 2025, but did not know the geofence had been extended in June 2026 or that it now reached the ramp above the spur. The supervisor said that nobody had drawn the difference for them. No training record anywhere on site, for any person, covers the geofences. The finding that operators did not know rests on these two accounts and on the absence of any training record or notice. No other driver was asked.

Two readings of the evidence are carried. On the first, the expectation mattered. For the first ten months of the extract the ramp below the crest was outside the geofence and the system was live, so a quiet system there meant an empty ramp. From June the system was silent whatever was on the road. A driver who believed it would sound for a light vehicle had one less reason to think anyone could be there, on a ramp that showed no intersection on the driver's screen and none on the road. On the second reading, the alert itself would have added little. Unsuppressed, it would have sounded when the light vehicle's tag entered the 60 m ring at about 02:47:06.5, half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on, so it would have told the driver what the driver was already reacting to. The system is advisory only. The effect of the expectation on what the driver did cannot be measured, and the driver's own remedy was a sign, not the system. The Investigation concluded that the suppression of alerts at the spur, unknown to operators, probably contributed to neither driver being aware of the other until the vehicle came over the crest. This is held at likely, at the standard of proof and no higher.

No stop control or warning sign, and the sight line

BIN-TMP-004 clause 6.2 requires the available sight distance at every intersection to be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle at the posted speed and "in no case less than 120 m", and where that cannot be achieved requires control by a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller. The survey of 13/08/2026 gave 41 m against a stopping distance of 96 m for a 220 t class vehicle, empty, at the posted 40 km/h on a 9 per cent descent, so the second limb applied. Clause 6.4, which requires a warning sign 50 m in advance on each approach, a give-way control and delineation at the mouth, applies only to intersections shown on the Schedule 1 drawing, which did not include the spur. At the time of the occurrence the intersection had no warning sign on either approach, no stop or give-way control, no delineation and no posted speed. The construction sign at the mouth had been removed on 02/08/2026 when the spur was first used, and nothing replaced it.

The geometry set the time available. The spur mouth lies 34 m below a convex grade break. From an eye height of 5.80 m the driver of the 220 t class vehicle first had line of sight to the spur mouth at 41 m, and to the top of the 4.5 m beacon whip on an approaching light vehicle at 53 m. From the spur mouth, at an eye height of 1.45 m, a light vehicle driver first had line of sight to a descending vehicle at 38 m, and the 1.6 m inner windrow took the vehicle's lower body out of view over the last 25 m of the spur's approach. At 37 km/h the first sight of the beacon came about six seconds before impact. Braking at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 needs about 68 m from 37 km/h, so a vehicle at the posted speed could not stop short of the spur whatever its driver did. With twice the sight line, the same 1.4 second response and the same braking would have stopped it short. The sight line of 41 m against 96 m very likely contributed to neither driver being aware of the other until the vehicle came over the crest.

A stop at the spur mouth would probably have shown the driver the 220 t class vehicle. On the recorded times, the vehicle was inside the light vehicle driver's 38 m of view from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the light vehicle reached the mouth. A technician who came out of the same spur in front of a descending vehicle seven days earlier reversed back into it and let it pass. The Investigation concluded that the absence of a stop control and warning sign probably contributed to the light vehicle driving out without stopping, and this is held at likely and below certainty for three reasons. First, the margin from a standstill at 38 m is thin, about four seconds of a vehicle at 37 km/h. Second, the light vehicle driver was already slowing and looking, on a give-way rule the driver described applying. Third, the effect of a sign on the driver of the 220 t class vehicle rests on that driver's own account after the event: "If there'd been a sign on my side of that crest I'd have been at twenty and I'd have been looking for him." A sign at an intersection absent from the in-cab map would also have been a single cue.

The warning had been given in writing. The hazard report of 04/08/2026 described the rise, the windrow, the absence of a sign and of a give-way, and the trucks not knowing the road was there. It was closed on 06/08/2026 with a reminder to the crew, the risk rating was reduced from Medium to Low, and the report was not referred to the mining department, the contractor, the survey department or the plan's owner. Three other hazard reports in the 24 months to 11/08/2026, at three different locations, described a light vehicle and a heavy vehicle meeting unexpectedly at an unlisted intersection. All four were closed within three days with a reminder that heavy vehicles have right of way, and none changed Appendix B, Schedule 1 or any physical control.

The light vehicle driver's action

BIN-TMP-004 clause 4.1 requires light vehicles to "give way to heavy vehicles at all times and in all circumstances". The light vehicle driver described applying that rule. The driver said they slowed at the spur mouth, looked right up the ramp, saw nothing and drove out, turning right to go up the ramp, at about 25 km/h. On read-back the driver corrected the account from stopped to slowed and did not believe they had come to a complete stop. The collision awareness log places the vehicle at the spur mouth at 02:47:09.2, and impact followed 2.8 seconds later, 6.2 m into the ramp. The driver's look came a second or two before the 220 t class vehicle came over the crest, when it was beyond anything the driver's position allowed them to see. There was no stop or give-way control, stop line, warning sign or posted speed at the mouth.

The light vehicle driver had never seen the traffic management plan and had done no Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment. ORR-STD-009 clause 7.3 allows a contractor's general induction to be accepted where a recognition agreement is held, and Hallorn Survey & Geotechnical had held one since 2023. Clause 7.4 states that this equivalence does not extend to the site supplement or to the pit driving assessment, "which shall be completed at site by every person who will drive a vehicle in an operating pit and shall not be substituted". Clause 7.5 makes the engaging department responsible for verifying this before a card is issued. The gatehouse issued the card on 09/08/2026 against the recognition agreement alone, after a 20 minute briefing on muster points, speed limits and personal protective equipment. It held no record of either item and was not asked for one, and nothing in the access card system distinguished a person who might drive in an operating pit from one who might not.

Had the assessment been checked, the driver would not have been carded to drive in an operating pit until they had completed it, and it is where a light vehicle driver is taught to approach an intersection that cannot be seen up. The driver said that if told to call, they would have called. However, the driver was already applying the first rule the assessment teaches, and the plan requires no call at an unlisted intersection, so that statement cannot be extended to RL 312. No copy of the assessment's content is held. It is likely that issuing access cards with no check of the assessment contributed to the light vehicle driving out of the spur without stopping, and this is held below the other influence findings for those reasons.

Driving out without stopping was the proximate step, and without the light vehicle on the ramp there is no collision. It is extremely likely that the light vehicle's movement out of the spur without stopping contributed to neither driver being aware of the other until the 220 t class vehicle came over the crest. A stop at the mouth would very likely have shown the driver the 220 t class vehicle, because it was already within the driver's 38 m of view, although a stop is only as good as the look from it.

Convergence and impact

The driver of the 220 t class vehicle was descending empty at 37 km/h with no intersection on the in-cab road network, no warning sign, no call on the channel and no alert. The light vehicle driver was coming out of the spur towards a mouth with 38 m of sight line, no give-way control, no call and no knowledge of the 220 t class vehicle. The first sighting available to the driver of the 220 t class vehicle was the beacon at 53 m, six seconds before impact. Neither the radio log, the download, the collision awareness log nor any of the interviews contains an indication that either driver knew of the other before then. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit, and there is no permit, call-in or board by which the supervisor could have known. It is extremely likely that neither driver was aware of the other until the 220 t class vehicle came over the crest, and the collision followed directly because nothing in the remaining six seconds could prevent it.

The scene examination shows how the impact developed. The front left of the 220 t class vehicle struck the right side of the light vehicle, which was crossing the descending lane to turn right, at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp and 3.1 m out from the toe of the inner windrow. The accelerometer of the 220 t class vehicle logged 1.9 g longitudinal. The light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp, and the gouge changed from tyre scuff to body panel gouge at 11.2 m, where it rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow. Its roll-over protection structure was deformed inward at the right rear mounting and was not breached, and the seatbelt was fastened with loading marks on the webbing. The driver sustained a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax, and was conscious throughout. The door on the resting side was against the ground, so extrication was through the rear. The impact and the roll-over are the means by which the convergence produced these injuries, and both are virtually certain.

What worked

The driver of the 220 t class vehicle responded within 1.4 seconds of the beacon coming into view. The download shows service brake pressure rising from 0 to 620 kPa in 0.4 seconds from 02:47:07.4, the retarder to maximum and 14 degrees of right steering in the same movement. That is inside the 1.5 seconds the plan's own stopping distance calculation allows. Braking reduced the impact speed from 37 km/h, which it would have been with no braking, to 24 km/h. Steering right deviated the braked scuff 1.4 m over its first 20 m, away from the open pit side, where the drop was more than 100 m, and towards the wall. This kept the push running down the ramp, and the light vehicle came to rest against the inner windrow. The 220 t class vehicle stopped 19 m below the intersection, and its driver then called the collision, stayed in the cab until dispatch had stopped the ramp, and stayed with the injured driver until the response team arrived.

Dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic at 02:48:01, 49 seconds after the collision and 20 seconds after the first call, and paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The night shift supervisor described the dispatcher's handling as textbook. The roll-over structure and the seatbelt held, and the injuries are those that follow when a structure holds and the restraint is worn.

Matters examined and not found contributing

A wet or slippery ramp surface was examined and was very unlikely. The met station recorded no precipitation and the last rainfall was 26 days earlier. The last water cart pass was at 01:10. The investigator found the ramp dry throughout at 06:20, and the braked scuff ran continuously for 58 m with no sign of a locked or sliding wheel. The achieved deceleration of 0.78 m/s2 net matches what the unit achieves dry.

Reduced brake performance was also very unlikely. The monthly brake test on 03/08/2026 gave 0.80 m/s2 net, and the test on 14/08/2026, before the unit returned to service, gave 0.82. The occurrence download gives 0.78 over the 4.6 seconds of braking, which is within the scatter of the two tests. The pre-start check sheet for 11/08/2026 recorded no defects, and no brake, retarder or park brake defect was raised in the previous twelve months.

Matters examined and not settled

Airborne dust on the ramp above the intersection

The existence of airborne dust on the ramp above the grade break was found likely, and whether it influenced the sighting could not be settled.

The water cart's last pass on that section was 1 hour 37 minutes before the occurrence against a standing 90 minute interval, so a pass was 7 minutes overdue. The fixed dust monitor on the RL 328 bench, 180 m up-ramp of the intersection and above the grade break, logged PM10 at 142 micrograms per cubic metre in the 02:40 to 02:50 interval, against a shift mean of 88. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle said there was dust hanging around, that the cart had been through earlier and that it dries out, and the descent was behind the vehicle's own lights into an unlit pit. Dust hanging in the lights over the crest is a condition in which a driver picks out a beacon late.

However, 142 is well below the site trigger of 250 and measures particulate at the monitor head, and nothing at Bindarra measures visibility. The light vehicle driver was firm that the air was clear at the spur, but that account describes the spur below the windrow, not the ramp above the crest where the 220 t class vehicle was. The beacon was in view from 53 m, the first point at which the survey says it could be seen at all, and the download puts the 220 t class vehicle there at its last steady data point. A sighting at the geometric limit leaves dust little to explain, and a 1.4 second response, inside the plan's allowance, leaves no room for a delay to hide in. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle also declined to say that dust made a difference.

It could not be resolved because the sighting is inferred from the last steady data point and the survey and was not recorded, so a fraction of a second lost at the edge of the lights cannot be excluded. [NOT ESTABLISHED: any measure of what could be seen from either cab on the night.]

Fatigue of the driver of the 220 t class vehicle

Whether the driver was fatigued to a degree that degraded performance is about as likely as not, and the evidence is evenly balanced.

The driver was on night 10 of 14 and 8 hours 47 minutes into a 12 hour 30 minute shift, in the window of lowest circadian alertness. The driver's voluntary sleep record gave between 5 and 7 hours on each of the ten days before, and 6 hours 30 minutes on the last of them. The driver described being tired "the way you are on night ten", and detecting a hazard in an unlit pit is the task fatigue degrades first. Against that, the driver said: "Not falling asleep tired ... I was awake and I was driving properly." Every limit in the contractor's fatigue standard was met, and the recorded performance shows none of the usual signs, with a 1.4 second response inside the 1.5 seconds the plan assumes and correct, immediate braking and steering.

Settling it would need a detailed fatigue assessment, which was not pursued. Even if fatigue were established, braking at 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m with a response time of zero still ends in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24.

Other factors that increased risk

The Stage 4 roster of 14 nights on and 7 off, 18:00 to 06:30, met every limit in the contractor's fatigue management standard. It virtually certainly existed, and its contribution to this collision was unlikely, because the recorded response contradicts a slowed one and the 1.4 seconds cost about 7 km/h of impact speed and not the collision. It remains a standing exposure, because every heavy vehicle driver is at the wheel of a 220 tonne vehicle in the small hours of a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night, and at an intersection with a longer sight line than RL 312 a slower response could be the difference between stopping and not. It is likely that this increased risk.

The night shift extrication arrangement is a matter still being examined. One qualified mines rescue paramedic was rostered at the surface medical facility, and the second responder, who must be present before any extrication, was called from the accommodation village 11 km from the gatehouse in the vehicle that carries the hydraulic rescue tool. Dispatch paged the team at 02:48, the first response vehicle reached the scene at 02:54, the second responder arrived with the tool at 03:07, and the driver was extricated at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The post-incident review measured a delay of approximately 9 minutes, from 02:58 to 03:07, and recorded no effect on the clinical outcome because the injuries were stable and the patient was conscious throughout. The collision and its injuries were complete before the response was paged, so its contribution to this occurrence was unlikely. It is very likely that the arrangement increased risk, because nothing in it scales with the severity of a casualty.

Findings

From the evidence available, the following findings are made with respect to the collision between a 220 t class vehicle and a light vehicle on the main ramp of the Stage 4 pit at Bindarra Gold Mine. These findings should not be read as apportioning blame or liability to any particular organisation or individual.

Contributing factors

  • It is virtually certain that the available sight distance at the RL 312 spur intersection was 41 m, against a stopping distance of 96 m for the 220 t class vehicle at the posted speed. This very likely contributed to the collision because the first sight of the light vehicle's beacon was at 53 m and the vehicle at 41 m, so the driver of the 220 t class vehicle could not stop short of the spur from the posted speed.
  • It is virtually certain that neither driver made a positive call on the pit channel before entering the intersection. This likely contributed to the collision because a call from either vehicle would have been heard by the other, although a call gives knowledge and not separation, and the two vehicles would still have had to resolve who went first over a short sight line.
  • It is virtually certain that the driver of the light vehicle drove out of the spur onto the ramp without stopping. This very likely contributed to the collision because the 220 t class vehicle was within view from the spur mouth, so a stop there would very likely have shown it to the driver. The driver's movements before reaching the spur mouth rest on the driver's own account.
  • It is very likely that neither driver was aware of the other until the 220 t class vehicle came over the crest, and this very likely contributed to the collision because no action to avoid it was possible in the six seconds that remained. The light vehicle's movements before the spur mouth rest on its driver's account alone.
  • It is virtually certain that the front left of the 220 t class vehicle struck the right side of the light vehicle at 24 km/h, and it is virtually certain that this impact contributed to the injury because it is the means by which the convergence of the two vehicles became a collision.
  • It is virtually certain that the light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side, and it is virtually certain that this contributed to the driver's serious injuries. The roll-over protection structure was deformed but not breached and the seatbelt was fastened.
  • It is virtually certain that the RL 312 spur was opened under a works order classified as routine earthworks, so no management of change record was raised and no referral was made to the traffic management plan owner, the survey department or the contractor's technical services group. This very likely contributed to the occurrence because it left the intersection without signage and with suppressed collision alerts. Eleven of the fourteen new Stage 4 roads cut since the last plan review were opened the same way and none was referred to the plan's owner. (Safety Issue)
  • It is virtually certain that the traffic management plan required a positive call only at the nine intersections listed at its Appendix B, and that an intersection could be added only at the annual review. This very likely contributed to the occurrence because the call was not required at the spur, and the plan had no route for adding the spur between reviews. (Safety Issue)
  • It is virtually certain that the intersection had no stop control, warning sign, delineation or posted speed although its sight distance did not meet the plan's sight distance requirement. This likely contributed to the occurrence because a stop control at the spur mouth would have held the light vehicle there. The margin from a stop at the mouth was thin, at 38 m of view, and the effect of a warning sign on the driver of the 220 t class vehicle rests on that driver's own account. (Safety Issue)
  • It is very likely that the collision awareness system raised no alert because the spur joined the ramp inside a suppression geofence that had been extended up the ramp in June 2026, and that operators did not know this. The operators' lack of knowledge rests on two accounts and the absence of any training record. This likely contributed to the occurrence through an expectation that a quiet system meant an empty ramp, which is an effect that cannot be measured. An alert, had one been raised, would have sounded only about half a second after the beacon came into view. (Safety Issue)
  • It is virtually certain that site access cards were issued with no check that the light vehicle pit driving assessment had been completed, and that the driver of the light vehicle had not completed it. This likely contributed to the occurrence because the assessment is where a driver is taught how to approach an intersection that cannot be seen up. The driver was already applying the rule that light vehicles give way to heavy vehicles, and the plan required no call at an unlisted intersection. (Safety Issue)
  • It is virtually certain that Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 did not define a significant change to the road network or set a basis for choosing the intersections at which positive communication is required. This likely contributed to the occurrence because it made doing nothing the default for a plan owned and reviewed within the mine. The mine was not in breach of the guideline, and it could have held an out-of-cycle review or listed every intersection on its own. (Safety Issue)

Other factors that increased risk

  • It is virtually certain that the contractor's Stage 4 roster required 220 t class vehicle drivers to work up to fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts, and the driver of the 220 t class vehicle was on night 10 of 14. It is unlikely that this contributed to the collision because the recorded response time of 1.4 seconds was inside the 1.5 seconds assumed in the plan's stopping distance calculation, and a response time of zero would still have ended in a collision at about 17 km/h. It is likely that the roster increased risk because it places drivers at the wheel in the hours of lowest alertness on every swing. (Safety Issue)

Other findings

  • It is virtually certain that the driver of the 220 t class vehicle braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon coming into view, and it is very likely that this reduced risk because it reduced the impact speed from 37 km/h to 24 km/h and kept the push running down the ramp, away from the open edge.
  • It is very unlikely that the ramp surface was wet or slippery at the intersection, so this was not found to have contributed to the occurrence.
  • It is very unlikely that the service brakes of the 220 t class vehicle delivered less than their tested deceleration during the stop, so this was not found to have contributed to the occurrence.

Safety issues and actions

This section sets out each organisational safety issue, the party responsible where it was established, and any safety action taken or proposed. Issues for which no action was recorded are stated as such.

Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 set no review trigger and no basis for the positive-call list

MSG-14, issued by the State Mines Inspectorate, required the traffic management plan to be reviewed after a significant change to the road network without defining that term, and required the mine to nominate its positive-call intersections without setting a minimum or a basis.

Bindarra's plan met the guideline on its face on the day of the occurrence. It had been reviewed within twelve months and it nominated nine interaction zones. The guideline also provided that the plan was a site document, not approved by the Inspectorate.

The mine could have held an out-of-cycle review under its own plan at any time, and nothing in the guideline stopped it listing every intersection. However, the guideline left both questions to a document reviewed once a year with no outside check, so doing nothing became the default. On that reasoning it is likely that the guideline contributed to positive calls being required only at listed intersections.

No safety action was recorded against this issue. [NOT ESTABLISHED: any action on the MSG-14 wording, and any response from the State Mines Inspectorate.]

A works order opened the RL 312 spur, so nothing was referred to anyone

Works order WO-26-4471 was classified as routine earthworks within the approved Stage 4 design, so no management of change record was raised for a new intersection on a heavy vehicle road.

ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2 requires a management of change record before any change to the mine's road network. Clause 5.3 excludes routine earthworks under a works order within an approved pit design where no new plant is introduced. The works order recorded management of change as not required under clause 5.3, and its handover field read "N/A". The works order form carried none of the three referrals that clause 5.4 requires inside a management of change record. These are referrals to the traffic management plan owner, to the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and to the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system geofences. None of the three was made.

The same gap affected other roads. Of the 14 new roads cut in Stage 4 since the 12/11/2025 review, the three raised under a management of change record were referred to the plan's owner and none of the 11 raised under a works order was.

A referral would have sent the new intersection to the plan's owner with a survey, and would have sent the geofence question to the technical services group. These are standing parts of the procedure and the three management of change roads were referred. Against this, a referral is not a control, and the plan's owner might have left the spur to the annual review. The plan had also never had an out-of-cycle review. It is very likely that the classification contributed to the absence of a stop control and signs and to the geofence suppressing alerts at the spur.

Access cards were issued with no check of the pit driving assessment

The gatehouse issued the light vehicle driver's site access card on 09/08/2026 against the contractor's recognition agreement alone, with no check that the Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment had been completed.

The assessment must be completed at site by every person who will drive in an operating pit. Clause 7.5 makes the engaging department responsible for verifying this before a card is issued. The gatehouse held no record of either item and was not asked for one, and the access card system did not distinguish a person who may drive in an operating pit from one who may not. Positive calls were required only at listed intersections and were added only at the annual review

The plan provides that an intersection is added where it appears on the road layout drawing at Schedule 1 and the review assesses it as requiring positive communication. Schedule 1 was reissued only at the annual review, and nothing required a new road to be referred to the plan's owner. The RL 312 spur appeared on neither document. The light vehicle made no transmission that shift after logging on. The next date on which the spur could have been listed was 12/11/2026, three months after the collision.

Collision alerts were suppressed at the spur by a geofence operators did not know about

The collision awareness system was suppressed at the RL 312 spur by the S4-LOAD geofence, which the contractor's technical services group had extended up the main ramp in June 2026, and operators had not been told.

The geofence was extended on 09/06/2026 as a system configuration task, which carried its boundary up the ramp to a point 180 m up-ramp of where the spur was later cut. The extension was not recorded in the traffic management plan and was not reviewed against it. No training record on site, for any person, covered the geofences. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle and the night shift supervisor each said they did not know the system was off on the ramp. Across the fleet, 68 proximity events inside the geofence were suppressed in the twelve months to 11/08/2026, nine of them a light vehicle on the main ramp below the boundary, all after 09/06/2026. These events were recorded in the system but the standing report did not include them, so they had never been reported. The system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m, 2.8 seconds before the collision, and raised no alert in the cab. It is almost certain that the suppression existed and that operators did not know of it.

The driver of the 220 t class vehicle had learned over fourteen months that a quiet system meant an empty ramp, and wrote in a safety report that the pit was taken to be empty when it was quiet. Against this, an unsuppressed alert would have sounded at about 02:47:06.5, half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on, so it would have told the driver only what was already being reacted to. The system is advisory only, and the effect of the expectation on the driver's actions was not shown. The suppression probably contributed, through that expectation, to neither driver being aware of the other until the 220 t class vehicle came over the crest.

Proactive action (Technical Services Manager, Tarrowen Mining Services, in progress, dated 28/08/2026): suppressed proximity events were added to the monthly collision awareness report on 28/08/2026, broken down by geofence and by vehicle class, and the previous twelve months were re-cut on the same basis for the Stage 4 fleet. The first report was due 30/09/2026.

Recommendation (Investigator in charge, open, dated 01/09/2026, response due 30/09/2026): that a change to a suppression geofence boundary be treated as a change to a documented control under ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2, and that operators be told in writing where the collision awareness system is suppressed and why.

No stop control or warning sign was fitted at an intersection that failed the sight distance rule

The RL 312 intersection had no stop control, give-way control, warning sign, delineation or posted speed, although its available sight distance of 41 m was below the stopping distance of 96 m that BIN-TMP-004 clause 6.2 required it to meet.

Clause 6.2 requires the available sight distance at every intersection, listed or not, to be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle at the posted speed and in no case less than 120 m. Where that cannot be achieved the intersection must be controlled by a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller. The survey gave 41 m against 96 m, so the control requirement applied. Clause 6.4, which requires warning signs and a give-way control, applies only to intersections on the road layout drawing at Schedule 1, and the spur was not on it. The construction sign at the spur mouth was removed on 02/08/2026 and nothing replaced it. A hazard report on 04/08/2026 named the missing sign and the missing give-way, and was closed on 06/08/2026 with a reminder to the crew.

A stop at the spur mouth would very likely have shown the 220 t class vehicle to the light vehicle driver, because the vehicle was within the 38 m of view from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the light vehicle reached the mouth. A week earlier the technician in the same position reversed into the spur and let the heavy vehicle pass. Against this, the margin from a stop at 38 m is thin, the light vehicle driver was already slowing and looking, and the effect of a warning sign on the driver of the 220 t class vehicle rests on that driver's own view after the event. It is likely that the absence of these controls contributed to the light vehicle driving out of the spur without stopping.

Action taken (Mining Manager, Orrabinda Resources, dated 14/08/2026): the RL 312 spur was barricaded and closed to all traffic on the night of the occurrence and remains closed. On 14/08/2026 survey measured the available sight distance at the other ten unlisted roads cut in Stage 4 since the last review. Two of the ten were below the clause 6.2 minimum and were closed pending a stop control. The action is closed for the RL 312 spur, and the two further roads remain open as actions.

Heavy vehicle drivers were rostered for fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts

The contractor's Stage 4 heavy vehicle roster was 14 nights on and 7 off, with 12 hour 30 minute shifts, within a fatigue management standard that permits up to 14 consecutive night shifts.

Every limit in the standard was met. The driver of the 220 t class vehicle was on night 10 of 14, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift. The recorded response of 1.4 seconds from the beacon coming into view to the brake pressure rise was inside the 1.5 seconds the plan's stopping distance calculation assumes. With braking at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m, a response time of zero would still have ended in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24 km/h. It is unlikely that the roster contributed to this occurrence.

The roster remains a standing exposure, because every heavy vehicle driver on the Stage 4 swing is at the wheel of a 220 tonne vehicle in the small hours of a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night. At an intersection with a longer sight line than RL 312, a slower response could separate stopping from not stopping. It is likely that the roster increased risk in this way.

Night shift extrication depends on a second responder called from the village

The Bindarra night shift was rostered with one qualified mines rescue paramedic, and the second responder, who must be present before any extrication, was called in from the accommodation village 11 km from the gatehouse.

On the night, dispatch paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The first response vehicle entered the pit at 02:51 and reached the scene at 02:54. The second responder arrived at 03:07 in the second response vehicle, which is garaged at the village and carries the hydraulic rescue tool. The driver was extricated at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The post-incident review found the extrication was delayed by approximately 9 minutes, measured from 02:58, when the paramedic's primary survey was complete, to the second responder's arrival. It also recorded that the delay did not affect the clinical outcome because the injuries were stable and the patient was conscious throughout.

The collision and the injuries were complete before the response was paged, so it is unlikely that the arrangement influenced this occurrence or its outcome. The arrangement would apply equally to a casualty with a more urgent need, and it is very likely that it increased risk on every night shift.
