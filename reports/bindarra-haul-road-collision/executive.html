(Fictional case study for training. Not a real investigation.)

What happened

At 02:47:12 on 12/08/2026 a 220 t class rigid [the other attendee 1] descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit at Bindarra Gold Mine struck the right side of a light vehicle, LV-118. All times in this report are local time (UTC+8). The light vehicle was coming out of the RL 312 dewatering spur, a 240 m access road to a new dewatering bore. It carried one occupant, a grade control technician employed by a survey subcontractor, on their third night shift at the mine. The spur had been opened to light vehicles on 02/08/2026, and the construction sign at its mouth was removed that day and not replaced. On 04/08/2026 at about 22:10 a dewatering technician pulling out of the same spur met a descending [the other attendee 1] and reversed back to let it pass. The technician reported this in hazard report HAZ-2026-0917, which was closed on 06/08/2026 with a reminder to the crew. The light vehicle driver's site access card had been issued on 09/08/2026 after a 20 minute gatehouse briefing.

The [the other attendee 1] was descending empty at 37 km/h in darkness. At 02:47:06 it was 53 m above the spur mouth, the point from which the survey showed the light vehicle's beacon could first be seen over a crest in the ramp. The [the other attendee 1] driver braked and steered right, towards the wall and away from a drop of more than 100 m, within 1.4 seconds of that point. Service brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa at 02:47:07.4 and the retarder went to maximum. The collision awareness system (the [the other attendee 1]'s proximity warning) logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m at 02:47:09.2 but raised no alert in the cab.

The [the other attendee 1] struck the light vehicle at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth. Without braking the impact would have been at 37 km/h. The light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow (the earth bank along the pit side of the ramp). The [the other attendee 1] stopped 19 m below the intersection. The light vehicle's roll-over protection structure was deformed but not breached, and the seatbelt was fastened.

Dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic at 02:48:01, 49 seconds after the collision. The [the other attendee 1] driver stayed in the cab until told the ramp was clear, then went to the light vehicle driver and stayed until the emergency response team arrived. The light vehicle driver was conscious throughout and was extricated through the rear of the vehicle at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The driver was flown to the metropolitan trauma centre at 07:40 with a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax (a collapsed section of lung). The light vehicle was destroyed. The [the other attendee 1] was damaged at the front left and returned to service on 14/08/2026. The Stage 4 pit stood down for 9 hours 15 minutes, the main ramp below RL 340 was closed for 22 hours, and the occurrence was notified to the State Mines Inspectorate as a reportable injury. No other person's injury is recorded, and [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether the [the other attendee 1] driver was injured].

Why it happened

This section explains how the conditions at the RL 312 intersection, the way the intersection came to exist, and the controls that did not apply combined so that neither driver knew of the other until about six seconds before impact.

The intersection could not be seen safely. The Bindarra traffic management plan (BIN-TMP-004) clause 6.2 requires the sight distance at every intersection to be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle using the road at the posted speed, and in no case less than 120 m. The survey of 13/08/2026 measured 41 m against a stopping distance of 96 m for an empty 220 t class [the other attendee 1] at the posted 40 km/h. The [the other attendee 1] driver could first see the light vehicle's beacon at 53 m, over a grade break the crews call the crest, and a driver at the spur mouth could first see a descending [the other attendee 1] at 38 m. With twice the sight line, the same 1.4 second response and the same braking, the [the other attendee 1] would have stopped short of the spur. It is very likely that this sight line contributed to neither driver seeing the other in time.

The intersection reached the pit without anyone being asked to review it. Management of change (the mine's formal review of a proposed change) under ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2 applies to any change to the road network. Clause 5.3 excludes routine earthworks under a works order within an approved pit design. Works order WO-26-4471 cut 240 m of access road to a dewatering bore, classified the work as routine earthworks, recorded management of change as not required and left the handover field as "N/A". None of the three referrals that a management of change record would have triggered was made, to the traffic management plan owner, the survey department for the in-cab road network, or the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system. The in-cab road network, last published on 24/07/2026, showed no intersection at RL 312. Of 14 new roads cut in Stage 4 since the last plan review, the 3 raised under a management of change record were referred and none of the 11 raised under a works order was. The referral steps worked where they were triggered, but it is very likely that opening the spur under a works order led to the missing signs and to the collision alert suppression described below.

With no referral, the intersection received none of the physical controls the plan describes. Clause 6.2 requires a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller where the sight distance cannot be achieved, so it applied to this intersection. Clause 6.4 requires a warning sign 50 m in advance on each approach, a give-way control and delineation at the mouth, but only for intersections on the Schedule 1 road layout drawing, and the spur was not on it. The "ROAD CLOSED - CONSTRUCTION" sign at the mouth was removed on 02/08/2026 when the spur was first used, and nothing replaced it. A dewatering technician wrote up a near miss at the same place on 04/08/2026, naming the missing sign and give-way. The report was closed two days later with a reminder to the crew and was not referred to anyone responsible for the plan.

The [the other attendee 1] driver said that a sign on the approach would have had the [the other attendee 1] at 20 km/h and looking for a vehicle. A stop at the spur mouth gives only 38 m of view, about four seconds of a [the other attendee 1] at 37 km/h, and the effect of a sign on the [the other attendee 1] rests on the [the other attendee 1] driver's own account. Even so, it is likely that the missing stop control and warning sign contributed to the light vehicle driving out of the spur without stopping.

The mine's call procedure did not apply either. BIN-TMP-004 clause 4.3.1 requires a call on the pit channel before entering any of the nine intersections listed at Appendix B, and clause 4.3.2 requires no call anywhere else. An intersection is added to Appendix B only if it appears on Schedule 1, and Schedule 1 is reissued only at the annual review, next due 12/11/2026. No provision lets an intersection be added between reviews. The spur was not listed, so no call was required. The [the other attendee 1] driver had called at the RL 360 crossover 51 seconds before the collision and was acknowledged, and the light vehicle made no transmission that shift after logging on. The [the other attendee 1] driver, the light vehicle driver and the night shift supervisor each described the same practice, which is that calls are made at the nine listed intersections and nowhere else. It is very likely that this arrangement led to neither driver making a call. A call gives knowledge and not separation, but it is likely that the absence of a call contributed to neither driver knowing of the other until the [the other attendee 1] came over the crest.

The collision awareness system also did not warn the [the other attendee 1] driver. The suppression geofence S4-LOAD was drawn in March 2025 to stop nuisance alerts at the loading face and was extended on 09/06/2026, which carried its boundary up the main ramp to 180 m up-ramp of where the spur was later cut. No alert is raised inside a geofence at any range. The system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m, 2.8 seconds before impact, and raised nothing in the cab. The [the other attendee 1] driver and the night shift supervisor each said they did not know the system was off on the ramp. The [the other attendee 1] driver had taken a quiet system to mean an empty ramp, and no training on site covers the geofences. An unsuppressed alert would have sounded about half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on, and the system is advisory only. The alert would therefore have added little by itself. The expectation that the ramp was empty is a real but unmeasurable effect, and it is likely that the suppression contributed to the [the other attendee 1] driver not being aware of the light vehicle.

The light vehicle driver was carded to drive in the pit without the training intended for it. ORR-STD-009 clause 7.4 requires every person who will drive in an operating pit to complete the Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment, which cannot be substituted. Clause 7.5 makes the engaging department responsible for verifying this before a site access card is issued. The gatehouse issued the card on 09/08/2026 against the contractor's recognition agreement alone, after a 20 minute briefing, and holds no record of the assessment. The light vehicle driver slowed at the spur mouth, looked up the ramp and drove out at about 25 km/h, and does not believe the vehicle came to a complete stop. The [the other attendee 1] was within the driver's 38 m of view from about two seconds before the light vehicle reached the mouth, so a stop would very likely have shown it. The light vehicle driver was already applying the rule that light vehicles give way to heavy vehicles, and the plan requires no call at an unlisted intersection, so the assessment would not have added a call at RL 312. It is likely that the missing check contributed, and it is extremely likely that driving out without stopping contributed to the collision.

Behind these controls sits the external guideline. MSG-14 clause 3.2 requires the plan to be reviewed within twelve months and after any significant change to the road network, but defines neither term nor gives an example. Clause 5.1 requires the mine to nominate the intersections needing positive communication and sets no basis for doing so. Bindarra's plan met the guideline on its face and the mine was not in breach. The mine could have held an out-of-cycle review under its own clause 9.1 at any time, and none has been held in the plan's eleven years. The guideline did not stop anyone from acting. It is likely that it contributed by making inaction the default, but this conclusion was only just reached at the standard of proof and is not settled beyond it.

These factors together made it extremely likely that neither driver was aware of the other until the [the other attendee 1] came over the crest. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit, and no permit, call-in or record exists by which the supervisor could have known. At impact the [the other attendee 1] was travelling at 24 km/h, down from 37 km/h. The [the other attendee 1] driver's brake and steering response within 1.4 seconds of the beacon appearing, inside the 1.5 seconds the plan allows, reduced the impact speed and turned the [the other attendee 1] away from the open edge of the road.

The roster did not contribute. The [the other attendee 1] driver was on night 10 of a 14 night roster, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift, and every limit in the contractor's fatigue standard was met. The 1.4 second response was inside the allowance, and braking at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m with no response time at all would still have ended in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24 km/h. It is unlikely that the roster contributed to this collision, although the exposure it creates is a standing one.

Two other explanations were examined and not found to be contributing. It is very unlikely that a wet or slippery ramp played a part, because the weather station recorded no rain, the investigator found the ramp dry, and the [the other attendee 1] achieved 0.78 m/s2 against a tested 0.80 and 0.82 on the same dry grade. It is very unlikely that the service brakes under-performed, because tests eight days before and three days after the collision gave 0.80 and 0.82 m/s2, and the download of the collision gave 0.78.

Two further matters could not be settled, airborne dust on the ramp above the intersection and the fatigue of the [the other attendee 1] driver. It is likely that dust was present, because the water cart pass was 7 minutes overdue, the dust monitor beside the ramp logged PM10 at 142 micrograms per cubic metre against a shift mean of 88, and the [the other attendee 1] driver described dust hanging on the ramp. The reading was well below the site trigger of 250, and nothing at Bindarra measures visibility. The light vehicle driver said the air was clear, although that account describes the spur and not the ramp above the crest. Whether dust affected the [the other attendee 1] driver's sighting is about as likely as not. The beacon was in view at 53 m, the first point the survey says it could be seen, and the response was inside the allowance. The sighting is inferred from the last steady data point and the survey and was not recorded, so a small delay at the edge of the [the other attendee 1]'s lights cannot be excluded.

It is as likely as not that the [the other attendee 1] driver was fatigued enough to degrade performance. The driver was on night 10, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift and in the period of lowest alertness, with between 5 and 7 hours of sleep on each of the preceding days. The [the other attendee 1] driver described the tiredness as ordinary, and the recorded response showed no sign of impairment. A detailed fatigue assessment would be needed to settle it, and this was not pursued.

What we found

This section sets out what the investigation found about the collision between the truck and the light vehicle (LV-118) at the RL 312 intersection. It begins with the factors that contributed, then the other factors that increased risk, and ends with the remaining findings.

Contributing safety factors

  • Spur opened under a works order, so nothing was referred (Safety issue). The mine's Management of Change procedure (ORR-PRO-022) requires a management of change record before any change to the road network. Clause 5.3 excludes routine earthworks under a works order within an approved pit design. Works order WO-26-4471 cut the 240 m spur to the dewatering bore, classed it as routine earthworks and recorded that management of change was not required. The three referrals a management of change record would have triggered were therefore never made. They were to the traffic management plan owner, to the survey department for the in-cab road map, and to the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system. Of the 14 new Stage 4 roads cut since the last plan review, 3 were raised under a management of change record and referred, and the 11 raised under a works order were not. This was very likely a contributing factor, because it led directly to the missing controls described below.
  • Guideline sets no review trigger and no basis for the positive-call list (Safety issue). Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 requires the traffic management plan to be reviewed within twelve months and after any significant change to the road network. It defines neither "significant change" nor gives an example. It also requires the mine to nominate the intersections where a radio call is required but sets no minimum and no basis for the choice. The mine's plan met the guideline on its face and the mine was not in breach. The guideline was likely a contributing factor, but this finding sits at the lowest level that meets the standard and should not be read as settled. The mine could have held an out-of-cycle review, or listed every intersection, without any change to the guideline. The guideline probably made doing nothing the default.
  • Positive calls required only at listed intersections, added only at the annual review (Safety issue). The mine's traffic management plan (BIN-TMP-004) requires a radio call on the pit channel before entering any of nine listed intersections and requires no call anywhere else. An intersection reaches the list only by appearing on the road layout drawing, which is reissued only at the annual review. Nothing in the plan allows an addition between reviews or requires a new road to be referred to the plan's owner. The RL 312 spur was on neither the drawing nor the list. The pit channel recording shows the practice: the truck driver called and was acknowledged at each listed intersection passed that night, the last 51 seconds before the collision, and made no call at the spur. The truck driver and the night shift supervisor each described the same practice, that calls are made at the nine intersections on the crib room board and nowhere else. This was very likely a contributing factor.
  • Collision alerts suppressed at the spur by a geofence operators knew nothing of (Safety issue). A geofence is a mapped boundary inside which the collision awareness system raises no alert. The boundary around the loading face was extended up the main ramp on 9 June 2026, to a point 180 m up-ramp of where the spur was later cut. The system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m from the truck and raised nothing in the cab. No training record on site covers the geofences, and both the truck driver and the night shift supervisor said they did not know the system was off on the ramp. The truck driver said he had never had an alert on the ramp in fourteen months and took a quiet system to mean an empty ramp. The system is advisory only, and an unsuppressed alert would have sounded about half a second after the beacon came into view, so the alert itself would have added little. The more likely contribution was the truck driver's expectation, so this factor was likely contributing, although its effect cannot be measured.
  • No stop control or warning sign at an intersection failing the sight distance rule (Safety issue). Clause 6.2 of the plan requires available sight distance at every intersection to be at least the stopping distance of the largest vehicle and never less than 120 m. Where that cannot be achieved, it requires a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller. The survey measured 41 m against a stopping distance of 96 m. The intersection had no warning sign on either approach, no stop or give-way control, no delineation and no posted speed, because the clause 6.4 signage applies only to intersections shown on the road layout drawing. The construction sign was removed on 2 August 2026 and nothing replaced it. A dewatering technician had reported the missing sign and give-way in a hazard report seven days before the collision. The report was closed in two days with a reminder to the crew. The truck driver said that with a sign on his side of the crest he would have been at twenty and looking for the light vehicle. This was likely a contributing factor, although the margin from a stop at the spur mouth was thin.
  • Access cards issued with no check of the pit driving assessment (Safety issue). The Contractor Management Standard (ORR-STD-009) requires every person who will drive in an operating pit to complete the Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment at site. It makes the engaging department responsible for verifying this before a card is issued. The gatehouse issued the light vehicle driver's card on 9 August 2026 against the contractor's recognition agreement after a 20 minute briefing, holding no record of the assessment and not asking for one. The driver had not done the assessment and had not seen the traffic management plan. The assessment would have taught him to stop and look again at a mouth he could not see out of, rather than only slow. He was already applying its first rule, to give way to heavy vehicles, and the plan required no call at an unlisted intersection. This was likely a contributing factor.
  • Sight line of 41 m against a 96 m stopping distance. The spur joins the ramp 34 m below a convex grade break. From the truck cab the spur mouth first came into line of sight at 41 m, and the light vehicle's beacon at 53 m. A driver at the spur mouth first had line of sight to a descending truck at 38 m. The stopping distance of the truck empty at the posted 40 km/h was 96 m. A truck at the posted speed could not stop for anything at this intersection, whatever the driver did. This was very likely a contributing factor, and it was the condition that left the two drivers without warning of each other.
  • Neither driver made a positive call before entering the intersection. The pit channel was recorded continuously, and no transmission by either vehicle mentions the spur, the bore or a light vehicle in the pit. The light vehicle made no transmission that shift after logging on. No call was required at an unlisted intersection, and the local practice matched the plan exactly. A call from either vehicle would have been heard by the other, by dispatch and by the supervisor, so the absence of a call was likely a contributing factor. A call gives knowledge and not separation, and the two vehicles would still have had to resolve who went first.
  • Light vehicle drove out of the spur without stopping. The light vehicle driver said he slowed at the mouth, looked right up the ramp, saw nothing and drove out at about 25 km/h. He does not believe he came to a complete stop. From the mouth the truck came into his view nearly two seconds before he reached the mouth, so a full stop there would very likely have shown it to him. On the evidence this was very likely a contributing factor, and it put the light vehicle across the truck's path. The technician who came out of the same spur in front of a truck on 4 August avoided a collision by reversing back into it.
  • Neither driver aware of the other until the truck came over the crest. The truck driver first had sight of the beacon 53 m away, about six seconds before the impact. The truck driver estimated about forty metres, which is an estimate of a distance seen briefly, and the survey figure of 53 m is the measure. The truck's in-cab map showed no intersection at RL 312. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit and said: "Nobody tells me. There's no book, there's no call, there's no permit." This was very likely a contributing factor.
  • Impact and roll-over. The front left of the truck struck the right side of the light vehicle at 24 km/h, pushed it 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled it onto its left side against the inner windrow. This is established with virtual certainty. The light vehicle driver, who was conscious throughout, suffered a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax. The roll-over protection structure was deformed but not breached, and the seatbelt was fastened.

Other factors that increased risk

  • Truck operators rostered for fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts (Safety issue). The Stage 4 truck roster is 14 nights on and 7 off, inside a fatigue management standard that permits up to 14 consecutive night shifts, and every limit in the standard was met. The truck driver was on night 10 of 14 and the collision came 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift. The roster was unlikely to have influenced this collision. The recorded response of 1.4 seconds was inside the 1.5 seconds the plan allows. Braking at the recorded rate from 53 m with no delay at all would still have ended in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24 km/h. The roster nonetheless remains a likely important exposure, because every truck operator is at the wheel of a large truck in the small hours of a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night.
  • Night shift extrication waits on a second responder called from the village (Safety issue). This matter is still being examined. The night shift has one qualified mines rescue paramedic at the surface medical facility. The second responder, who must be present before any extrication begins, is called from the accommodation village 11 km from the gatehouse in the vehicle that carries the hydraulic rescue tool. The driver was extricated 27 minutes after the collision. The post-incident review measured the delay at approximately 9 minutes, from the completion of the paramedic's primary survey to the second responder's arrival, and recorded that it did not affect the clinical outcome because the injuries were stable. The response was paged after the collision and the injuries were complete by then, so the arrangement was unlikely to have influenced this occurrence. It is very likely important because the structure of the arrangement would not change with the severity of a casualty.

Other findings

  • Truck driver's response. The truck driver braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon coming into view, inside the 1.5 seconds the plan allows. This reduced the impact from 37 km/h to 24 km/h and took about 58 per cent of the impact energy out of the collision. The steering turned the truck towards the wall and away from the open pit edge, a drop of more than 100 m, and the light vehicle came to rest against the pit side windrow. This was very likely a decrease in risk.
  • Wet or slippery ramp surface. This was very unlikely. The weather station recorded no rain, the last rainfall was 26 days before, and the investigator found the ramp dry. The braking achieved was the figure the truck achieves on a dry grade.
  • Service brakes delivering less than their tested deceleration. This was very unlikely. The monthly test eight days before gave 0.80 m/s2, the test three days after gave 0.82 and the occurrence download gave 0.78. No brake defect had been raised in the previous twelve months.
  • Airborne dust on the ramp above the intersection (unresolved, still being examined). The evidence of dust being present is likely. The water cart's last pass was 7 minutes overdue, and the dust monitor beside the ramp logged 142 micrograms per cubic metre against a shift mean of 88. The truck driver described dust hanging on the descent but would not say it made a difference. The light vehicle driver said the air was clear. The reading was well below the site trigger of 250, and nothing at the mine measures what can be seen from a cab. Whether dust influenced the sighting is about as likely as not. The beacon was in view from the geometric limit of 53 m, which leaves dust little to explain. The sighting was inferred from the last steady data point and the survey, so a loss of a fraction of a second at the edge of the lights cannot be excluded.
  • Truck driver fatigue during the approach (unresolved). Whether the truck driver was fatigued enough to degrade performance is about as likely as not. The truck driver said he was tired "the way you are on night ten" but was awake and driving properly. He had recorded between 5 and 7 hours of sleep on each of the preceding ten days. The recorded response showed none of the usual signs of impairment. A detailed fatigue assessment was not pursued, because even if fatigue were established, a response time of zero would still have ended in a collision.

What is being done

The mine and its contractors took three actions after the collision, and the Investigator in charge made three recommendations that remain open.

Positive calls required only at listed intersections, added only at the annual review. The Mining Manager, Orrabinda Resources, issued a site-wide direction at 05:10 on 12/08/2026, two hours twenty-three minutes after the collision. It requires a positive call on the pit channel before entering any intersection in the Stage 4 pit, "listed in the traffic management plan or not, until further notice". The direction was read at the following four pre-starts and posted beside the Appendix B board in the Stage 4 crib room, and it stays in force until BIN-TMP-004 is amended. The plan still provides no route for an intersection to reach Appendix B between annual reviews, so the Investigator in charge recommended that BIN-TMP-004 provide one and that it require a new road to be referred to its owner when opened to traffic. The next date on which the RL 312 spur could have been listed was 12/11/2026. The recommendation is open, with a response due 30/09/2026.

Spur opened under a works order, so nothing was referred to anyone. The Investigator in charge recommended that clause 5.3 of ORR-PRO-022 stop excluding earthworks that create a new intersection with a heavy vehicle road. As an alternative, the works order form at Schedule 4 would carry the three referrals that the management of change record holds at clause 5.4. Those referrals are to the traffic management plan owner, the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system geofences. Of the fourteen new roads cut in Stage 4 since the review of 12/11/2025, the three raised under a management of change record were referred and none of the eleven raised under a works order was. The recommendation is open, with a response due 30/09/2026.

No stop control or warning sign at an intersection failing the sight distance rule. The Mining Manager, Orrabinda Resources, had the RL 312 spur barricaded and closed to all traffic on the night of the occurrence, and it remains closed. On 14/08/2026 survey measured the available sight distance at the other ten unlisted roads cut in Stage 4 since the last review, before the November reissue of Schedule 1. Two of the ten were below the clause 6.2 minimum and were closed pending a stop control. The action is closed for the RL 312 spur, and the two further roads remain open as actions. The [the other attendee 1] driver said the investigation should look at putting a sign on the intersection and the road on the in-cab map.

Collision alerts suppressed at the spur by a geofence operators knew nothing of. On 28/08/2026 Tarrowen technical services added suppressed proximity events to the monthly collision awareness report, broken down by geofence and by vehicle class, and re-cut the previous twelve months on the same basis for the Stage 4 fleet. The 68 suppressed events inside S4-LOAD, nine of them involving a light vehicle on the main ramp below the boundary, had been recorded in the system throughout and had never been reported to anybody. The Technical Services Manager, Tarrowen Mining Services, is responsible for this action, which is in progress, with the first report due 30/09/2026. The Investigator in charge also recommended that a change to a suppression geofence boundary be treated as a change to a documented control under clause 5.2 of ORR-PRO-022, and that operators be told in writing where the system is suppressed and why. That recommendation is open, with a response due 30/09/2026. The [the other attendee 1] driver made the same point about telling operators where the system is switched off.

Access cards issued with no check of the pit driving assessment. No action or recommendation has been recorded against this issue.

Night shift extrication waits on a second responder called from the village. No action or recommendation has been recorded against this matter, which is still being examined.

[the other attendee 1] rostering for fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts. No action or recommendation has been recorded against this issue.

The night shift supervisor also recommended a permit, a call-in or some record by which the supervisor of an operating pit knows which light vehicles are in it. No action has been recorded against that suggestion.
