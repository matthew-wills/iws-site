What happened

A light vehicle driver was seriously injured in a collision with a haul truck on the main ramp. All times in this report are local time (UTC+8).

At 02:47:12 on 12/08/2026, a 220 t class rigid haul truck (HT-407) was descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit at Bindarra Gold Mine, empty. It struck the right side of a light vehicle (LV-118) that was coming out of the RL 312 dewatering spur at 24 km/h, after braking from 37 km/h. The truck pushed the light vehicle 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled it onto its left side against the inner windrow. The light vehicle carried one occupant, a grade control technician employed by a survey subcontractor, who was on the driver's third night shift at the mine, having worked 09/08, 10/08 and 11/08. It was dark, the Stage 4 pit has no fixed lighting, no rain had fallen, and the ramp surface was dry on a 9.1 per cent descending grade.

The spur was a 240 m access road cut under a works order to reach a new dewatering bore. It was completed on 31/07/2026 and opened to light vehicles on 02/08/2026. The "ROAD CLOSED - CONSTRUCTION" sign at its mouth was removed that day and nothing replaced it. On 04/08/2026 at about 22:10, a dewatering technician came out of the same spur in front of a descending truck about 60 m away, reversed back and let it pass. The technician submitted hazard report HAZ-2026-0917 at 23:48, and it was closed on 06/08/2026 with a reminder to the crew, with the risk rating reduced from Medium to Low. The light vehicle driver's site access card was issued on 09/08/2026 after a 20 minute gatehouse briefing.

The light vehicle's movements before the spur mouth were estimated from the driver's own account, which put it leaving the bore collar at about 02:45. The truck's last steady data point was at 02:47:06.0, at 37 km/h and 53 m above the spur mouth. From 02:47:07.4 service brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa, with the retarder at maximum and 14 degrees of right steering applied. At 02:47:09.2 the truck's collision awareness system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m and 32 km/h closing, and the alert was suppressed. Impact was at 02:47:12.0 at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth, and the truck stopped at about 02:47:19, 19 m below the intersection.

Dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic at 02:48:01 and paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The first response vehicle entered the pit at 02:51 and reached the scene at 02:54, and the second responder arrived with the hydraulic rescue tool at 03:07. The driver, who was conscious throughout, was extricated through the rear of the vehicle at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The driver reached the surface medical facility at 03:41 and was flown to the metropolitan trauma centre at 07:40, having sustained a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax.

The light vehicle was destroyed. Its roll-over protection structure was deformed inward at the right rear mounting but not breached, and the seatbelt webbing carried loading marks. The haul truck was damaged at the front left. The Stage 4 pit stood down for 9 hours 15 minutes and the main ramp below RL 340 was closed for 22 hours. The occurrence was notified to the State Mines Inspectorate as a reportable injury under notification ORR-OCC-2026-0332. The haul truck operator and the night shift supervisor were stood down for the remainder of the shift, and both tested negative for drugs and alcohol. Why it happened

The collision followed from a new intersection that no one inside the mine was required to check, which left each of the mine's own defences inactive at that place.

How the intersection came to exist

Works order WO-26-4471 cut 240 m of access road to a dewatering bore at RL 312. It classified the work as "Routine earthworks within the approved Stage 4 design" and recorded "Management of change not required, ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.3". Its handover field read "N/A". Clause 5.3 excludes routine earthworks under a works order from the management of change procedure. A management of change record would have referred the change to three parties: the traffic management plan owner, the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system geofences (the mapped zones in which the system suppresses alerts). The works order form carries none of these referrals, and none was made. Since the last plan review on 12/11/2025, the three new Stage 4 roads raised under a management of change record were referred and none of the eleven raised under a works order was. It is very likely that opening the spur under a works order meant nothing was referred to anyone, and so no one assessed the intersection's controls or its geofence cover.

What that did to the mine's defences at RL 312

The in-cab road network published on 24/07/2026 was still current on the night and showed the main ramp with no intersection at RL 312. The survey measured 41 m of available sight distance at the spur against a 96 m stopping distance for the truck. Clause 6.2 of the traffic management plan (BIN-TMP-004) requires at least the stopping distance and in no case less than 120 m, and otherwise requires a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller. The intersection met neither limb. The clause 6.4 requirements for a warning sign, a give-way control and delineation applied only to intersections on the Schedule 1 road layout drawing, which did not include the spur. The spur therefore had no warning sign on either approach, no stop or give-way control, no delineation and no posted speed. The "ROAD CLOSED - CONSTRUCTION" sign was removed on 02/08/2026 when the spur was first used, and nothing replaced it. It is likely that the absence of a stop control and warning sign contributed to the light vehicle driving out of the spur without stopping. A stop at the mouth would have held the light vehicle there while the truck came into its view. The margin was thin, because from the mouth the windrow and the grade break gave only 38 m of sight line.

The collision awareness system had been changed in a similar way. The S4-LOAD suppression geofence was drawn in 2025 to stop nuisance alerts at the loading face. It was extended on 09/06/2026 as a configuration task by the technical services group, which carried its boundary up the main ramp to a point 180 m up-ramp of where the spur was later cut. The change was not recorded in, or reviewed against, the traffic management plan. [NOT ESTABLISHED: the size of the S4-LOAD extension of 09/06/2026, because the configuration holds the new boundary and not the old one.] The spur therefore joined the ramp inside the suppressed area. The system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m and raised no alert.

The positive call requirement was inactive for a related reason. Clause 4.3.1 required a call on the pit channel before entering any of the nine Appendix B intersections, and clause 4.3.2 required none anywhere else. An intersection reaches Appendix B only by appearing on Schedule 1 and being assessed at the annual review, and nothing in the plan allowed it to be added between reviews or required a new road to be referred to the plan's owner. The next review was due on 12/11/2026. It is very likely that this arrangement meant no call was made before the spur intersection by either driver, as the plan did not require one there.

The external guideline

Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 does not define a "significant change" to the road network in clause 3.2, sets no basis in clause 5.1 for nominating the intersections that need positive calls, and leaves the plan under clause 3.5 as a site document the Inspectorate does not approve. The plan met the guideline on its face. The guideline did not stop the mine acting, because the plan's own clause 9.1 allowed an out-of-cycle review at any time and the mine could have listed every intersection. However, no such review had been held in the plan's eleven years, and the guideline left both questions to the same annually reviewed site document. The evidence indicates the guideline likely made doing nothing the default, and no more than that.

The light vehicle driver

The light vehicle driver's site access card was issued on 09/08/2026 against the contractor's recognition agreement, after a 20 minute gatehouse briefing. ORR-STD-009 clause 7.4 states that equivalence does not extend to the light vehicle pit driving assessment, and clause 7.5 makes the engaging department responsible for verifying it before a card is issued. The gatehouse neither held nor asked for the assessment, and the driver had never heard of it. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether the Bindarra site supplement was issued to the light vehicle driver, because Hallorn's records for it are outstanding.] It is likely that this contributed to the driver's approach to the spur, because the assessment is where a driver learns to stop and look again at a mouth that cannot be seen out of. The driver was already applying the rule that light vehicles give way to heavy vehicles, and the plan required no call at an unlisted intersection, so the assessment would not necessarily have led to a call.

The driver slowed at the mouth, looked up the ramp, saw nothing and drove out at about 25 km/h, and did not believe the vehicle came to a complete stop. The truck was within the driver's 38 m of view from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the vehicle reached the mouth at 02:47:09.2. A look at that earlier moment would have shown the truck, so it is extremely likely that driving out without stopping led to the two vehicles being unaware of each other until the last seconds. A dewatering technician had done the opposite on 04/08/2026, reversing back into the spur to let a truck pass. The technician reported the same sight line in a hazard report that night. The report was closed two days later with a reminder to the crew and was not referred to the plan's owner. Three earlier hazard reports describing the same interaction at other intersections were also closed with reminders.

The truck operator

The haul truck operator made no call before the spur. The radio log shows HT-407 had called, and been acknowledged, at the RL 360 crossover going down at 02:46:21, 51 seconds before the collision. The light vehicle made no transmission after logging on at 18:04. The haul truck operator, the light vehicle driver and the night shift supervisor each described the same practice without prompting: calls are made at the nine listed intersections and nowhere else. The night shift supervisor added that requiring calls everywhere would make the channel unusable. The practice matched the plan exactly. A call from either vehicle would have been heard by the other, so it is likely that the absence of a call contributed to neither driver being aware of the other.

The haul truck operator also held a mistaken expectation about the collision awareness system. In fourteen months the operator had never had an alert on the ramp below the crest, and took that to mean the ramp was empty when the system was quiet. For the first ten of those months this was true, because the ramp was outside the geofence. From June the system was silent there whatever was on the road. No training record on site, for any person, covers the geofences, and the night shift supervisor knew the system was off at the loading face but not that the boundary now reached the ramp. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether other operators held the same belief about the collision awareness system, because no other operator has been asked.] [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether technical services told anyone at Orrabinda Resources, or anyone above the night shift supervisor at Tarrowen, of the June 2026 extension.] On balance the effect of this expectation was likely real but could not be measured. An unsuppressed alert would have sounded at about 02:47:06.5, half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on. The system is advisory only and the operator's own remedy was a sign, not the system. It is therefore likely, and no more, that the suppressed alert contributed to the operator's lack of awareness.

How the two vehicles converged

The truck was descending empty at 37 km/h and the first sight available to the operator was the light vehicle's beacon at 53 m, six seconds before impact, which is what the survey predicts for the geometry. The very short sight line, set against a stopping distance of 96 m, meant the truck could not stop for anything at the intersection at the posted speed. It is very likely that this sight line contributed to neither driver being aware of the other until the truck came over the crest. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit, and no permit, call-in or board existed by which the supervisor could have known. Given the combination of conditions above, the convergence was virtually certain to lead to the collision, because nothing in the remaining six seconds could have prevented it.

What limited the outcome

The operator's response took 1.4 seconds from the beacon coming into view to a rise in brake pressure, inside the 1.5 seconds the plan's stopping distance calculation assumes. The operator braked and steered right towards the wall, away from an open edge and a drop of more than 100 m. It is very likely that this reduced the risk, because the impact was at 24 km/h against 37 km/h with no braking. The truck's front left struck the right side of the light vehicle, pushed it 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled it onto its left side. These events are virtually certain. The roll-over protection structure was not breached and the driver's seatbelt was fastened, and the driver suffered a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax.

Other matters considered

The ramp surface was very unlikely to have been wet or slippery. The weather record shows no rain, the surface was dry when examined, and the deceleration achieved matched the truck's dry figure. The service brakes were very unlikely to have delivered less than their tested deceleration. Tests on 03/08/2026 and 14/08/2026 gave 0.80 and 0.82 m/s2 net, and the occurrence download gave 0.78.

Airborne dust is a matter still being examined, and no conclusion can be drawn from it. It is likely that dust was present above the intersection, but whether it affected the sighting is about as likely as not. On one side, the water cart pass was 7 minutes overdue, and the dust monitor 180 m up-ramp logged 142 micrograms per cubic metre in the 02:40 to 02:50 interval against a shift mean of 88. The haul truck operator also described dust hanging on the ramp. On the other side, the reading is well below the site trigger of 250 and nothing at Bindarra measures visibility. The light vehicle driver was firm that the air was clear, and the beacon was in view at the geometric limit of 53 m, which leaves dust little to explain.

Fatigue of the haul truck operator is inconclusive. The operator was on night 10 of 14, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift, with 6 hours 30 minutes of sleep the previous day, and said "Tired, the way you are on night ten". The operator also said "I was awake and I was driving properly", every limit in the contractor's fatigue standard was met, and the 1.4 second response was inside the plan's allowance. A detailed fatigue assessment was not pursued because even a response time of zero would still have ended in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24. For the same reason it is unlikely that the 14 night roster influenced this collision.

The 9 minute wait for the second responder during the extrication is a matter still being examined. The collision, the injuries and their severity were complete before the response team was paged at 02:48, so its influence on the occurrence is unlikely. The post-incident review recorded that the delay did not affect the clinical outcome. What we found

This investigation applied "Likely or probable": a proposition is established at a probability of 65% or more and not established at 35% or less, and anything between is inconclusive.

Contributing safety factors

The factors below relate to each other as a chain. A works order opened a new intersection in an operating pit without any referral, and the mine's controls then left that intersection without a call requirement, a stop control, a warning sign or an in-cab alert. The two drivers converged on it without knowing of each other, and the collision followed.

How the intersection came to exist without controls

The Management of Change procedure ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2 requires a management of change record before any change to the mine's road network. Clause 5.3 excludes routine earthworks carried out under a works order within an approved pit design where no new plant or vehicle type is introduced. Works order WO-26-4471 cut 240 m of access road to bore DW-14 at RL 312, classified the work as routine earthworks within the approved Stage 4 design, recorded that management of change was not required, and left its handover field reading "N/A". The three referrals that a management of change record would have triggered under clause 5.4 were therefore not made. They would have gone to the traffic management plan owner, to the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and to the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system geofences. The in-cab road network last published on 24/07/2026 showed the main ramp with no intersection at RL 312 on the night. The works order classification was very likely a contributing factor, because the three roads raised under a management of change record since the last plan review were referred to the plan's owner. Of the eleven raised under a works order, none was. The weight given to this is limited only by the possibility that the plan's owner, once told, would have left the spur to the annual review, because no out-of-cycle review has been held in the plan's eleven year history. (Safety issue)

The external guideline sat behind this. Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 clause 3.2 requires the traffic management plan to be reviewed within twelve months and after any significant change to the road network, and neither defines the term nor gives an example. Clause 5.1 requires the operator to nominate the intersections at which positive communication is required, and sets no minimum and no basis for the nomination. Under clause 3.5 the plan is a site document that is not approved by the Inspectorate. Bindarra's plan met the guideline on its face because it had been reviewed within twelve months and it nominated nine interaction zones, so the mine was not in breach. The guideline probably contributed, because a defined trigger and a stated basis for nomination would have given a new intersection a route onto the call list between annual reviews. However, the mine could have held an out-of-cycle review under its own clause 9.1 at any time and could have listed every intersection it had, so the guideline made inaction the default and did not compel it. This finding is held at the standard of proof and no higher. (Safety issue)

Why no call, sign or control applied at the spur

Bindarra's traffic management plan BIN-TMP-004 clause 4.3.1 requires a positive call on the pit channel before entering any intersection listed at Appendix B, and clause 4.3.2 requires no call anywhere else. Appendix B lists nine intersections. An intersection is added only where it appears on the road layout drawing at Schedule 1 and the annual review assesses it as needing positive communication. Schedule 1 is dated 12/11/2025 and is reissued only at the annual review. No provision allows an intersection to be added between reviews, and none requires a new road to be referred to the plan's owner when it is cut. The RL 312 spur was on neither document, so a call was not required there. The pit channel recording shows that HT-407 called, and was acknowledged, at each listed intersection it passed, the last 51 seconds before the collision at the RL 360 crossover. The truck operator, the light vehicle driver and the night shift supervisor each described the same practice independently, which is that calls are made at the nine intersections on the crib room board and nowhere else. The arrangement that left the spur off the list very likely contributed, because the operator called at every listed intersection that night and the channel was open with the light vehicle on it. A call made and not acknowledged would still have left a driver deciding whether to go. The next date on which the spur could have been listed was 12/11/2026. (Safety issue)

Clause 6.2 of the plan requires the available sight distance at every intersection, listed or not, to be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle at the posted speed and in no case less than 120 m. Where that cannot be achieved it requires a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller. The survey gave 41 m against a stopping distance of 96 m, so the second limb applied to this intersection. Clause 6.4 requires a warning sign 50 m in advance on each approach, a give-way control on the minor road and delineation at the mouth, but it applies only to intersections shown on the road layout drawing, and the spur was not on it. The intersection therefore had no warning sign on either approach, no stop or give-way control, no delineation and no posted speed. The construction sign at the mouth was removed on 02/08/2026 and nothing replaced it. The absence of a stop control and warning sign probably contributed. A stop at the mouth would have held the light vehicle there while the truck came into view, because on the recorded times the truck was within the driver's 38 m of view from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the driver reached the mouth. The operator said that a sign on the approach would have had the truck at 20 km/h and looking for the light vehicle. However, the margin from a stop at 38 m is thin, about four seconds of a truck at 37 km/h, and the effect of a sign on the truck rests on the operator's own view after the event. (Safety issue)

The truck's collision awareness system operates at Level 8, operator advisory. Inside a suppression geofence it raises no alert at any range and logs a proximity event only below 25 m. The S4-LOAD geofence was extended northward on 09/06/2026 and carried its boundary up the main ramp to a point 180 m up-ramp of the intersection, so the spur, cut from 28/07/2026, joined the ramp inside the suppressed area. The change was a system configuration task by the contractor's technical services group, was not recorded in the traffic management plan and was not reviewed against it. The log shows a proximity event at 02:47:09.2 at 21.4 m with the alert suppressed. No training record anywhere on site covers the geofences, and the truck operator and the night shift supervisor each said they did not know the system was off on the ramp. It is extremely likely that the suppression existed and that operators did not know of it, and the suppression likely contributed. The two readings of its effect did not agree. On one reading, the operator had learned over fourteen months that a quiet system meant an empty ramp, and wrote in a safety report that the quiet had been taken to mean the pit was empty, so the expectation was a real effect that cannot be measured. On the other, an unsuppressed alert would have sounded at about 02:47:06.5, half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on, so it would have told the operator only what the operator was already reacting to. The system is advisory only, and the operator named a sign and not the system as the remedy. The finding is held at the standard of proof and no higher. (Safety issue)

The light vehicle driver's preparation

ORR-STD-009 clause 7.4 states that a contractor's induction does not extend to the Bindarra site-specific supplement or to the light vehicle pit driving assessment, which every person who will drive in an operating pit must complete at site. Clause 7.5 makes the engaging department responsible for verifying this before a card is issued. The gatehouse issued the driver's card on 09/08/2026 against the contractor's recognition agreement alone after a 20 minute briefing, and it holds no record of either item and is not asked for one. The driver's training file records the contractor's online module and no pit driving assessment, and the driver had never seen BIN-TMP-004. The access card arrangement probably contributed, because the assessment is where a driver is taught to approach a mouth that cannot be seen out of by stopping and looking again, and on the recorded times a stop would very likely have shown the truck. Against this, the driver was already applying the first rule of the assessment, giving way to heavy vehicles, and the plan requires no call at an unlisted intersection, so the assessment would not have taught a call at RL 312. The finding is held below the other influence findings because no copy of the assessment's content is held. (Safety issue)

Conditions at the intersection and the drivers' actions

The survey of 13/08/2026 placed the spur mouth 34 m below a convex grade break. An operator at an eye height of 5.80 m first has line of sight to the spur mouth at 41 m and to the top of the 4.5 m beacon whip at 53 m. A driver at the mouth at an eye height of 1.45 m first has line of sight to a descending truck at 38 m, and the 1.6 m inner windrow takes the truck's lower body out of view before that. A 220 t class truck empty at the posted 40 km/h on a 9 per cent descending grade needs 96 m to stop. A truck at the posted speed therefore could not stop for anything at this intersection. The short sight line was very likely a contributing factor, because with twice the sight line the same 1.4 second response and the same braking would have stopped the truck short of the spur. A dewatering technician had reported the same sight line from the same position in a hazard report seven days earlier.

Neither driver made a call. The pit channel was logged continuously and contains no transmission by either vehicle about the spur, the bore, or a light vehicle being in the pit. The light vehicle made no transmission that shift after logging on at 18:04 (10:04 UTC). The absence of a call likely contributed, because a call from either vehicle would have been heard by the other, by dispatch and by the supervisor. However, a call gives knowledge and not separation, and the two vehicles would still have had to resolve who went first.

The light vehicle driver slowed at the mouth, looked right up the ramp, saw nothing and drove out, turning right, and corrected on read-back an earlier statement that the vehicle had stopped. The driver put the speed at about 25 km/h and did not believe the vehicle came to a complete stop. The driver's look came before the truck was within view of that position, and the truck was within 38 m from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the driver reached the mouth. That the vehicle drove out without stopping was extremely likely a contributing factor, because without the light vehicle on the ramp there is no collision, and a stop at the mouth would very likely have shown the truck. The only qualification is that a stop is only as good as the look from it at 38 m.

It is extremely likely that neither driver was aware of the other until the truck came over the crest. The radio log, the download, the collision awareness log and the three interviews contain no indication that either driver knew of the other beforehand. The first sighting available to the truck operator was the beacon at 53 m, six seconds before impact. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit, and no permit, call-in or record exists by which the supervisor could have known. This convergence was extremely likely a contributing factor because the collision followed directly from it, and nothing in the six seconds that remained could have prevented it.

The collision and its mechanism of injury

The truck's front left struck the right side of the light vehicle at 02:47:12 at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth. The light vehicle was turning right across the descending lane with its right side to the truck. The light vehicle was then pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow, with the roll-over protection structure deformed inward at the right rear mounting but not breached and the seatbelt fastened with loading marks on the webbing. Each of the impact and the push and roll-over is established with virtual certainty as the mechanism by which the convergence produced the occupant's fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and small pneumothorax.

Other factors that increased risk

The Stage 4 haul roster is 14 nights on and 7 off, 18:00 to 06:30, a 12 hour 30 minute shift with a 30 minute crib, inside the contractor's fatigue management standard, which permits up to 14 consecutive night shifts. The truck operator was on night 10 of 14, and the collision was 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift. Every limit in the standard was met. It is virtually certain that the roster existed as described, and it is unlikely that it influenced this occurrence. The download gives 1.4 seconds from the beacon coming into view to the brake pressure rise, inside the 1.5 seconds that the plan's stopping distance calculation allows. Braking at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m, a response time of zero would still have ended in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24 km/h. The roster remains a standing exposure that is likely important, because it places every haul truck operator at the wheel of a 220 tonne vehicle in the early hours of a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night, where the defence against an unlit hazard at an unlisted intersection is the operator's own detection and response. (Safety issue)

The night shift emergency response is still being examined. The night shift is rostered with one qualified mines rescue paramedic at the surface medical facility, and the second responder, who must be present before any extrication, is called in from the accommodation village 11 km from the gatehouse, in the second response vehicle that carries the hydraulic rescue tool. The team was paged at 02:48, the first response vehicle reached the scene at 02:54, the second responder arrived at 03:07, and the driver was extricated at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The post-incident review measured the delay at approximately 9 minutes, from 02:58 to 03:07, and recorded no effect on the clinical outcome because the injuries were stable and the patient was conscious throughout. Existence is virtually certain. Influence on this occurrence is unlikely because the injuries were complete before the response was paged. Importance is very likely, because the extrication capability on night shift is a single person plus a drive from the village, and no part of the arrangement scales with the severity of what has happened. (Safety issue)

Other findings

The truck operator braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon appearing, and this very likely reduced risk. Service brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa in 0.4 seconds, the retarder went to maximum and 14 degrees of right steering was applied. Impact was at 24 km/h instead of the 37 km/h it would have been without braking, and the steering took the truck away from the open edge, where the pit edge dropped more than 100 m, and towards the wall. The light vehicle came to rest against the inner windrow and was not driven over the edge. The operator then stopped 19 m below the intersection, reported the collision, stayed in the cab as dispatch instructed, and afterwards stayed with the driver until the response team arrived. Dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic 49 seconds after the collision.

Two hypotheses were examined and are very unlikely. A wet or slippery ramp surface is very unlikely because the met station recorded no precipitation, the last rainfall was 26 days earlier, the investigator found the ramp dry at 06:20, and the achieved deceleration matched the dry figure. Degraded service brakes are very unlikely because the tests on 03/08/2026 and 14/08/2026 gave 0.80 and 0.82 m/s2 net, the occurrence download gave 0.78, and no brake defect had been raised in the twelve months before.

Two matters were not resolved, and neither is a finding. Airborne dust on the ramp above the intersection is still being examined. It is likely that some dust was present, because the water cart pass was 7 minutes overdue and the monitor 180 m up-ramp logged PM10 at 142 micrograms per cubic metre against a shift mean of 88, well below the site trigger of 250. Whether the dust affected the truck operator's sighting is about as likely as not. The operator described dust hanging on the descent but would not say it made a difference, and the light vehicle driver was firm that the air was clear. The beacon was in view from 53 m, the first point at which the survey says it could be seen, and the response was inside the plan's allowance. However, the sighting is inferred from the last steady data point and the survey and was not recorded, and nothing at Bindarra measures visibility, so a fraction of a second lost at the edge of the lights cannot be excluded. Resolving it would need a measure of what could be seen from the cab, which does not exist for that night. Dust was therefore not found to be contributing.

Fatigue of the truck operator during the approach is about as likely as not. The operator was on night 10 of 14 and inside the window of lowest circadian alertness, and described being tired "the way you are on night ten". The operator's sleep record gave between 5 and 7 hours on each of the preceding days and 6 hours 30 minutes on the last. This factor appears in no causal chain and has no recorded test result. The related factor "Haul operators rostered for fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts" is identified as failing Influence but is linked to no timeline entry. The status of the fatigue factor needs confirming. - Causal chain "Truck operator braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon appearing -> Light vehicle driver seriously injured" links this factor directly to the critical event, apparently as a mitigating response. The same factor is also attached to the 02:48:01 dispatch entry, which concerns the post-collision traffic stop rather than the operator's braking. - [Why it happened] - "Sight line of 41 m at the spur against a 96 m stopping distance" and the approved "What happened" text (first sight of the beacon placed by survey at 53 m above the spur mouth) give two different distances. They may be measured to different reference points (the spur mouth, the driver's eye position, or the impact point 6.2 m into the ramp). Please confirm that the reference points are stated consistently. - "Night shift extrication waits on a second responder called from the village" is marked Finalised: no, but it appears in both the safety issues list and the other risk factors list. Please confirm its status before the report is issued. - The positive event "Truck operator braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon appearing" has its causal link recorded as leading to the injury outcome. The scaffold supplies no term, so the bullets say only "ruled out". Confirm this is the intended wording. - Narrowed terms: the scaffold gives "very likely" where the record has "extremely likely".
