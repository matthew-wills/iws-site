Executive summary

What happened

At 02:47:12 on 12/08/2026 a 220 t class rigid heavy vehicle, descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit at Bindarra Gold Mine empty, struck the right side of light vehicle LV-118 as it came out of the RL 312 dewatering spur. The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] braked from 37 km/h and the impact was at 24 km/h. The light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow. Times in this report are local time (UTC+8).

The light vehicle driver, a grade control technician employed by a survey subcontractor, was conscious throughout and sustained a fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and a small pneumothorax. The driver was extricated 27 minutes after the collision and flown out at 07:40. The Stage 4 pit stood down for 9 hours 15 minutes and the main ramp below RL 340 was closed for 22 hours. [NOT ESTABLISHED: whether the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] was injured.]

What the investigation found

The probable cause was that the light vehicle drove out of the spur onto the ramp without stopping, into the path of the heavy vehicle, with neither driver aware of the other until the beacon came into view 53 m above the spur. This happened because the spur had been opened under works order WO-26-4471 with nothing referred to anyone, was not listed for a positive call, and had 41 m of sight line against a 96 m stopping distance. This determines no fault or liability.

The conditions behind that sequence built on one another. The guideline, MSG-14, set no review trigger and no basis for the positive-call list, and it likely contributed because it made doing nothing the default, although this is held close to the standard of proof. The works order classified the spur as routine earthworks, so no management of change record was raised and no referral reached the traffic management plan owner, the survey department or the technical services group. This very likely contributed, and the same was true of eleven of the fourteen new Stage 4 roads since the last plan review. The plan therefore listed nine intersections for positive calls and not the spur, which very likely contributed to neither driver calling. The spur also had no stop control or warning sign although it failed the clause 6.2 sight distance rule, which likely contributed.

The collision awareness system was suppressed at the spur by the S4-LOAD geofence, which operators did not know of. It likely contributed through an inferred expectation that a quiet ramp was empty, an effect that cannot be measured. The driver's access card was issued without a check of the pit driving assessment, which likely contributed, although this was held lower because the assessment's content was not available. The driver's slowing without a full stop at a mouth with 38 m of view very likely contributed.

Three other matters were examined. The fourteen-night roster was unlikely to have contributed and was likely a standing risk. The extrication wait of approximately 9 minutes for a second responder was unlikely to have influenced this outcome, and on the tests so far it was a very likely risk-increasing gap. Whether dust affected the sighting could not be settled and is about as likely as not. The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] braked and steered right within 1.4 seconds of the beacon appearing, which took the impact from 37 km/h to 24 km/h and steered away from the open pit edge.

What can be done

Operators should treat any new intersection on a heavy vehicle road as a change that must reach the traffic management plan, the in-cab map and the collision awareness configuration, however the work is ordered. Changes to suppression geofences should be treated as changes to a documented control, and operators told where alerts are suppressed.

The spur was barricaded on the night of the occurrence and remains closed. The Mining Manager issued a site-wide direction at 05:10 requiring a positive call before entering any Stage 4 intersection, listed or not. Two of ten other unlisted roads measured on 14/08/2026 failed the sight distance minimum and were closed. From 28/08/2026 Tarrowen reports suppressed proximity events monthly. Recommendations to amend ORR-PRO-022 and BIN-TMP-004, and on geofence changes, remain open.

Factual information

History of the occurrence

Before the shift

The RL 312 dewatering spur was a 240 m access road cut under works order WO-26-4471 to reach dewatering bore DW-14. The works order recorded the earthworks complete on 31/07/2026. The bore was energised and the spur was first used by light vehicles on 02/08/2026. A "ROAD CLOSED - CONSTRUCTION" sign had stood at the spur mouth while the road was being cut. The works log recorded it removed on 02/08/2026 and nothing was installed in its place.

On 04/08/2026 at about 22:10 a dewatering technician came out of the spur in front of a descending heavy vehicle about 60 m away. The technician reversed back into the spur and let it pass. The technician submitted hazard report HAZ-2026-0917 at 23:48 the same night. On 06/08/2026 the Dewatering Superintendent closed the report with an entry recording a reminder to the crew at the 05/08 pre-start. The risk rating went from Medium on submission to Low on closure.

On 09/08/2026 the gatehouse issued a site access card to the light vehicle driver after a 20 minute briefing. The first of the driver's three night shifts at Bindarra began that evening.

The night shift

At 02:22:41 dispatch called the Stage 4 pit back to work after crib. The pit channel was logged continuously from then, and the investigator listened to the whole period. The heavy vehicle, designated 407, called and was acknowledged at the RL 360 crossover going up at 02:28, at the ROM pad turn-off at 02:37, and at the RL 360 crossover coming down at 02:46:21, which was 51 seconds before the collision. Neither vehicle made any transmission about the RL 312 spur, the dewatering bore or a light vehicle being in the pit. The light vehicle, LV-118, made no transmission after logging on at 18:04.

The approach

From about 02:45 to about 02:47 the light vehicle left the bore collar and drove the 240 m of spur towards the main ramp. This timing is estimated. It rests on the driver's account of the pickup and the length of the spur, and the driver's own estimate of "about ten to three" is later than the recorded collision and was not used. Nobody on the shift knew the light vehicle was in the pit.

The heavy vehicle was descending the main ramp empty. The fleet management system download recorded it at 39 km/h at RL 384 and 38 km/h at RL 351 before the following data points:

  • 02:47:06.0: 37 km/h at RL 317, 53 m above the spur mouth. This was the last steady data point, and the survey places the first sight of the light vehicle's beacon at this distance.
  • 02:47:07.4: 37 km/h at 38.5 m above the spur mouth. Service brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa in 0.4 seconds, the retarder went to maximum and there was 14 degrees of right steering input.
  • 02:47:09.2: 32 km/h at 21.4 m above the spur mouth. The collision awareness system (CAS) logged a proximity event with LV-118 at 21.4 m, closing at 32 km/h. The alert was suppressed and nothing was presented in the cab.

The collision

The impact occurred at 02:47:12.0 at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth and 3.1 m out from the toe of the inner (pit side) windrow. The accelerometer logged 1.9 g longitudinal. LV-118 was turning right out of the spur to go up the ramp, across the descending lane, with its right side towards the heavy vehicle. The front left of the heavy vehicle struck the right side of LV-118. The light vehicle was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow, going over at about 11.2 m. The heavy vehicle stopped 19 m below the intersection at about 02:47:19 and its park brake was applied.

The response

The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] called the collision to dispatch at 02:47:41. At 02:48:01, 49 seconds after the collision, dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic. Dispatch told the [the other attendee 1] to stay in the cab until the ramp was stopped above and below, and released the [the other attendee 1] at 02:48:44. The [the other attendee 1] then went down to the driver and stayed until the emergency response team arrived. The [the other attendee 1] reported that the driver was conscious and talking, and that the door was against the ground.

Dispatch paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The first response vehicle entered the pit at 02:51 and reached the scene at 02:54. The second responder arrived with the hydraulic rescue tool at 03:07. The driver was extricated through the rear of the vehicle at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision, and reached the surface medical facility at 03:41. The driver was flown to the metropolitan trauma centre at 07:40.

The occurrence was notified as ORR-OCC-2026-0332. The Stage 4 pit stood down for 9 hours 15 minutes and the main ramp below RL 340 was closed for 22 hours. The spur was barricaded and closed to all traffic. The Mining Manager issued a site-wide direction at 05:10 requiring a positive call on the pit channel before entering any intersection in the Stage 4 pit, listed in the plan or not, until further notice.

Aerodrome and site information

The main ramp at RL 312 runs approximately north-west on a 9.1 per cent descending grade, 24 m wide between windrows. Traffic keeps left, so a descending heavy vehicle runs on the open, pit side of the road. The surface was dry and well maintained. The spur joins the ramp on a descending heavy vehicle's left, on the inside of a left-hand bend, at an included angle of 71 degrees.

The survey pickup SUR-26-1188 of 13/08/2026 placed the spur mouth 34 m below a convex grade break where the descent steepens, which the crews call the crest. The survey gave these sight distances:

  • A heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] at an eye height of 5.80 m first has line of sight to the spur mouth at 41 m.
  • The top of a 4.5 m beacon whip on a light vehicle approaching the mouth is in view from 53 m, while the vehicle's body is not.
  • A driver at the spur mouth, at an eye height of 1.45 m, first has line of sight to a descending heavy vehicle at 38 m. The 1.6 m inner windrow takes the heavy vehicle's lower body out of view before that, for the last 25 m of the spur's approach.

The stopping distance of a 220 t class heavy vehicle travelling empty at the posted 40 km/h on a 9 per cent descending grade, calculated from the manufacturer's data with a 1.5 second response, is 96 m. The plan's minimum sight distance is 120 m.

At the time of the occurrence the intersection had no warning sign on either ramp approach, no give-way or stop control at the spur mouth, no delineation or marker posts and no posted speed for the spur. The in-cab fleet management system road network for Stage 4 was last published on 24/07/2026 and did not contain the spur. The in-cab display therefore showed the main ramp with no intersection at RL 312.

The scene examination recovered these marks, measured from the up-ramp edge of the spur mouth:

Regulatory guideline

Mines Safety Guideline MSG-14 revision 4 was made under regulation 6.22 of the Mining Operations Safety Regulations 2018. The regulation requires that the [the other attendee 1] "shall manage the risks arising from the interaction of vehicles at the mine". The guideline provides:

  • "3.2 The [[the other attendee 1]] shall prepare and maintain a traffic management plan for the mine. The plan shall be reviewed at intervals not exceeding twelve months, and following any significant change to the mine's road network, vehicle fleet or operating pattern."
  • "3.5 The plan is a site document. It is not approved by, and need not be submitted to, the Inspectorate, except on request following an occurrence or an inspection."
  • "5.1 The [[the other attendee 1]] shall nominate the intersections, crossings and other interaction zones at the mine at which positive communication between vehicles is required before entry, and shall record them in the plan."
  • "5.4 Where a light vehicle and a heavy vehicle may interact, the [[the other attendee 1]] shall provide for the light vehicle to be detectable by the heavy vehicle [[the other attendee 1]]. Detectability may be provided by line of sight, elevated marking, radio communication, a proximity detection system, or a combination of these."

The guideline does not define "significant change" and gives no example of one. It does not say on what basis interaction zones are nominated and sets no minimum.

Traffic management plan

Bindarra Traffic Management Plan BIN-TMP-004 revision 7, owned by the Mining Manager, Orrabinda Resources, was issued on 12/11/2025 with the next review due on 12/11/2026. It provides:

  • "4.1 Light vehicles shall give way to heavy vehicles at all times and in all circumstances."
  • "4.3.1 A positive call shall be made on the relevant pit channel before entering any intersection listed at Appendix B, and entry shall not be made until the call is acknowledged or the channel is confirmed clear."
  • "4.3.2 At intersections not listed at Appendix B, vehicles shall give way in accordance with clause 4.1. No call is required."
  • "6.2 At every intersection, the available sight distance shall be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle using the road at the posted speed, and in no case less than 120 m. Where this cannot be achieved, the intersection shall be controlled by a stop control, signalised control or traffic controller."
  • "6.4 Every intersection shown on the road layout drawing at Schedule 1 shall be provided with an intersection warning sign 50 m in advance on each approach, a give-way control on the minor road, and delineation at the mouth."
  • "9.1 This plan shall be reviewed annually, and following any significant change to the mine's road network."

Appendix B lists nine intersections requiring positive communication: the ROM pad turn-off, the RL 360 crossover, the Stage 2 ramp junction, the Stage 2 dump access, the Stage 3 ramp junction, the workshop apron entry, the explosives magazine access, the raw water pump station access and the light vehicle parking bay at RL 400. The note to Appendix B states: "This appendix is reviewed and reissued at the annual review of this plan (clause 9.1). An intersection is added to this appendix where it appears on the road layout drawing at Schedule 1 and the review assesses it as requiring positive communication."

Schedule 1 is dated 12/11/2025 and is reissued at the annual review. The RL 312 spur appears on neither Schedule 1 nor Appendix B. No provision in the plan allows an intersection to be added between annual reviews, and none requires a new road to be referred to the plan's owner when it is cut. Appendix B is reproduced on a laminated board in each crib room, and the Stage 4 board is the 12/11/2025 issue. The signage requirement at clause 6.4 applies to intersections shown on Schedule 1.

Annual reviews were completed on 14/11/2023, 10/11/2024 and 12/11/2025. No out-of-cycle review had been carried out under clause 9.1 in the plan's eleven year history. The survey department's road register recorded 14 new roads cut in Stage 4 since the 12/11/2025 review, 11 under a works order and 3 under a management of change record. The three with a management of change record were referred to the plan's owner and none of the 11 was.

Management of change and the works order

Management of Change procedure ORR-PRO-022 revision 3 provides:

  • "5.2 A management of change record shall be raised before any of the following: a change to the mine's road network; the introduction of a new vehicle type; a change to a pit design; a change to a documented control."
  • "5.3 Clause 5.2 does not apply to routine earthworks carried out under a works order within an approved pit design, where no new plant or vehicle type is introduced. Such work is managed under the works order."
  • "5.4 Where a management of change record is raised, the change owner shall complete the impact assessment at Schedule 2, which includes referral to the traffic management plan owner, the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and the contractor's technical services group for the collision awareness system configuration."

The three referrals at clause 5.4 exist only inside the management of change record. The works order form at Schedule 4 has no equivalent fields.

Collision awareness system

The CAS on the Stage 4 heavy vehicle fleet operated at Level 8, heavy vehicle advisory. Level 9, automated intervention, was available on the fleet and was not enabled at Bindarra. Advisory alerts were raised when a vehicle tag entered the 60 m detection ring at a closing rate above 10 km/h, except inside a suppression geofence. Inside a suppression geofence no alert was raised at any range, and a proximity event was logged only once the range fell below 25 m.

The S4-LOAD suppression geofence was drawn on 14/03/2025 around the Stage 4 loading face to stop nuisance alerts between the excavator, the trucks and the service vehicles working there. It was extended northward on 09/06/2026 when the loading face advanced, which carried the boundary up the main ramp for the first time. The extended boundary lies 180 m up-ramp of the RL 312 spur intersection, so the spur, cut seven weeks later, joined the ramp inside the suppressed area. The contractor's technical services group made the extension as a system configuration task. It was not recorded in the traffic management plan and was not reviewed against it. Analysis

This analysis follows the conditions at the RL 312 spur intersection through the actions of the two drivers to the collision and its outcome, then deals with what worked, with matters that increased risk, with matters not settled, and with matters considered and not found contributing.

General

The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] had seven years on 220 t class rigid trucks, a verification of competency on type issued in 06/2025 and an annual refresher completed in 02/2026. Qualifications and recency were not in question. Both the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] and the night shift supervisor were tested for drugs and alcohol after the occurrence, and both results were negative.

The light vehicle, LV-118, was serviceable. It was fitted with a certified roll-over protection structure and a 4.5 m amber beacon whip, and its inspection sheet for the week of 09/08/2026 recorded every item serviceable. The beacon lamp worked when tested after recovery. The condition of the road surface and the performance of the heavy vehicle's brakes were also examined, and are dealt with under the matters considered and not found contributing.

The approach and the unseen intersection

Clause 6.2 of the traffic management plan required the available sight distance at every intersection to be not less than the stopping distance of the largest vehicle using the road at the posted speed, and in no case less than 120 m. The survey of 13/08/2026 gave a stopping distance of 96 m for an empty 220 t class heavy vehicle at the posted 40 km/h on a 9 per cent descent. It measured 41 m of available sight distance at the spur. The intersection therefore failed the clause on both limbs, and a heavy vehicle at the posted speed could not stop for anything that appeared at it.

The geometry fixed what each driver could see. The spur mouth lay 34 m below a convex grade break, which the crews call the crest. From an eye height of 5.80 m the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] first had line of sight to the spur mouth at 41 m, and to the top of the beacon whip on a light vehicle approaching the mouth at 53 m, but not to the vehicle's body. The light vehicle driver, at the mouth at an eye height of 1.45 m, first had line of sight to a descending heavy vehicle at 38 m. The 1.6 m inner windrow took the heavy vehicle's lower body out of view before that. The last steady data point from the heavy vehicle was 37 km/h at 53 m above the mouth, at 02:47:06.0, so the beacon was in view about six seconds before the impact.

Braking alone from 37 km/h at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 needed about 68 m. With twice the sight line, the same 1.4 second response and the same braking would have stopped the heavy vehicle short of the spur. The sight line condition was virtually certain, and it was very likely a contributor to the collision. The contrary consideration is that a short sight line harms only when something is in it, and the other conditions described below put the light vehicle there.

Neither driver knew of the other until the beacon came over the crest. The heavy vehicle was descending with no intersection on its in-cab road network, no warning sign, no call on the channel and no alert. The light vehicle was leaving the spur with no give-way control and no knowledge of the heavy vehicle. The radio log, the download, the collision awareness log and the interviews show no sign that either driver knew of the other before that point. The night shift supervisor did not know a light vehicle was in the pit, and no permit, call-in or board existed by which the supervisor could have known. The investigation found it extremely likely that this mutual unawareness led directly to the collision, because the six seconds left after the first sighting could not prevent it.

Why the intersection was unarmed

Each of the controls that should have made the intersection known to both drivers depended on an earlier step that was never taken at the spur. The first of those steps sits in the external guideline.

MSG-14 clause 3.2 requires the traffic management plan to be reviewed within twelve months and after any significant change to the road network. Clause 5.1 requires the [the other attendee 1] to nominate the intersections at which positive communication is required. The guideline defines neither a significant change nor a basis for the nomination, and under clause 3.5 the plan is a site document that is not approved by the Inspectorate. Bindarra met the guideline on its face, because the plan had been reviewed inside twelve months and nominated nine interaction zones. The mine could nonetheless have held an out-of-cycle review under its own clause 9.1 at any time, and could have listed every intersection it had. The Inspectorate's guideline therefore did not stop anyone from acting. The investigation found it likely that the guideline contributed, because with no defined trigger and no stated basis for nomination, doing nothing became the default. That conclusion is held only just at the standard of proof.

Inside the mine, ORR-PRO-022 clause 5.2 required a management of change record before any change to the road network. Clause 5.3 excluded routine earthworks under a works order within an approved pit design where no new plant is introduced. WO-26-4471 cut 240 m of access to bore DW-14, classified it as routine earthworks, recorded management of change as not required, and left the handover field reading N/A. None of the three referrals that clause 5.4 attaches to a management of change record was made. Those referrals go to the traffic management plan owner, to the survey department for the fleet management system road network, and to the technical services group for the collision awareness system geofences. The works order form has no equivalent fields. The pattern was not confined to this road. Of the 14 new Stage 4 roads since the review of 12/11/2025, all 3 raised under a management of change record were referred to the plan's owner, and none of the 11 raised under a works order was. As a result the in-cab map published on 24/07/2026 never showed the spur, and the S4-LOAD geofence was never examined against it. It is very likely that the works order classification contributed. The plan's owner might have left the spur to the annual review even if it had been referred, and no out-of-cycle review had been held in the plan's eleven years. That possibility did not outweigh the fact that the referrals were standing parts of the procedure and were made for the three roads that followed it.

The plan's positive call requirement depended in turn on that missing step. Clause 4.3.1 required a call only before entering one of the nine Appendix B intersections, and clause 4.3.2 required none elsewhere. An intersection reached Appendix B only by appearing on the Schedule 1 drawing, which was dated 12/11/2025 and reissued only at the annual review. No provision allowed an addition between reviews, so the next date on which the spur could have been listed was 12/11/2026, three months after the collision. Three earlier hazard reports, from 03/2025, 09/2025 and 02/2026, described a light vehicle and a heavy vehicle meeting unexpectedly at other unlisted intersections. Each was closed within three days with a reminder to the crew, and none was referred to the plan's owner. The pit channel recording shows the practice working as written: the heavy vehicle was acknowledged at the RL 360 crossover going down at 02:46:21, 51 seconds before the collision, and made no call at the unlisted spur. The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1], the light vehicle driver and the night shift supervisor each described the same practice independently, namely that calls are made at the nine board intersections and nowhere else. The gap in the plan very likely contributed. The absence of a call from either vehicle likely contributed in turn, because a call would have been heard by the other driver, by dispatch and by the night shift supervisor. A call would have given knowledge and not separation, and the two vehicles would still have had to settle who went first over 41 m of sight line.

Clause 6.4 required a warning sign 50 m in advance on each approach, a give-way control on the minor road and delineation at the mouth, but only at intersections shown on the Schedule 1 drawing. The spur was not on it. On the night the spur had no warning sign, no stop or give-way control, no delineation and no posted speed. The construction sign at the mouth had been removed on 02/08/2026, when the spur was first used, and nothing replaced it. A dewatering technician wrote on 04/08/2026 that "There is no sign on the ramp telling the trucks the road is there and there is no give way on our side". The report was closed two days later with a reminder to the crew to take care and give way, and its risk rating was reduced from Medium to Low. A stop control at the mouth would have held the light vehicle where the heavy vehicle came into its view, and the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] said that with a sign on that side of the crest "I'd have been at twenty and I'd have been looking for him." Against that, 38 m is only about four seconds of a heavy vehicle at 37 km/h, which is a thin margin even from a standstill. The effect of a sign on the heavy vehicle driver also rests on that driver's own view after the event, and a sign at an intersection absent from the in-cab map would have been a single cue. On balance, the absence of a stop control and warning sign likely contributed.

The gatehouse was the last place where the light vehicle driver's preparation could have been checked. ORR-STD-009 clause 7.4 states that equivalence for a contractor's induction does not extend to the Bindarra light vehicle pit driving assessment, which every person driving in an operating pit must complete at site and which shall not be substituted. Clause 7.5 makes the engaging department verify it before a card is issued. The gatehouse issued the card on 09/08/2026 against Hallorn's recognition agreement after a 20 minute briefing, held no record of the assessment or the site supplement, and was not asked for one. The driver had never heard of the assessment and had never seen the plan. The assessment is where a light vehicle driver is taught to approach an intersection that cannot be seen out of, by stopping and looking again and not merely slowing, and the driver said that a call would have been made if the driver had been told to make one. The access card gap likely contributed, but it is held lower than the others. No copy of the assessment's content is on file. The driver was already applying its first rule, that light vehicles give way to heavy vehicles at all times. The plan also required no call at an unlisted intersection, so the assessment would not have taught a call at RL 312.

The light vehicle leaving the spur without stopping

Clause 4.1 required light vehicles to give way to heavy vehicles at all times and in all circumstances. The driver described applying that rule. On reading back the account, the driver corrected it from stopped to slowed. The driver said that the look right up the ramp showed nothing, that the vehicle drove out at about 25 km/h turning right, and that the driver did not believe the vehicle came to a complete stop. The collision awareness log places the light vehicle at the spur mouth at 02:47:09.2 with the heavy vehicle 21.4 m above it, and the impact came 6.2 m into the ramp 2.8 seconds later.

On the recorded times the driver's look came a second or two before the heavy vehicle came over the crest, when it was beyond anything the driver's position allowed the driver to see. The heavy vehicle was within the driver's 38 m of view from about 02:47:07.4, nearly two seconds before the driver reached the mouth. A full stop at the mouth would therefore very likely have shown the driver the heavy vehicle, and the driver would very likely have let it pass, as the technician did on 04/08 by reversing back into the spur. The limit on that is that a stop is only as good as the look from it, and at 38 m the margin was thin. The investigation found it extremely likely that driving out without stopping was a contributing step, because without the light vehicle on the ramp there is no collision. The step followed from the gaps above. The access card system did not ensure the driver had been taught how to approach a blind mouth, and the spur gave the driver no stop control, line, sign or posted speed.

The collision awareness system

The collision awareness system on the Stage 4 heavy vehicle fleet operated at Level 8, advisory only. Inside a suppression geofence it raised no alert at any range and logged a proximity event only once the range fell below 25 m. S4-LOAD was drawn on 14/03/2025 around the loading face to stop nuisance alerts. The technical services group extended it northward on 09/06/2026 as a system configuration task when the face advanced, which carried the boundary up the main ramp for the first time and put it 180 m up-ramp of the spur site. The extension was not recorded in the traffic management plan and was not reviewed against it. When the spur was cut seven weeks later, it joined the ramp inside the suppressed area. The system logged the light vehicle at 21.4 m and raised nothing in the cab.

The statistics show how the silence came to be read. In the twelve months to 11/08/2026 the heavy vehicle raised 1,684 advisory alerts, every one outside the geofence. Across the fleet, 68 proximity events inside it were suppressed, nine of them a light vehicle on the main ramp below the boundary, all after 09/06/2026. No one had queried them, because the standing report does not include suppressions. No training record anywhere on site covers the geofences. The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] said in interview "I've never had it go off down there", and understood that a quiet system meant an empty ramp. The protected safety report, summarised without quotation, states that the [the other attendee 1] learned only afterwards that the system was switched off there on purpose and believed other operators shared the same assumption. The night shift supervisor knew the system was off at the dig but not that the geofence reached the ramp. Before June the ramp was outside the geofence and the belief was sound, so the change removed the basis for it while nobody was told.

An unsuppressed alert would have sounded at about 02:47:06.5, half a second after the beacon came into view and about a second before the brakes went on. It would therefore have told the [the other attendee 1] only what the [the other attendee 1] was already reacting to, and the system carries no intervention. The [the other attendee 1]'s own remedy was a sign and not the system. The weight of the contribution lies in the expectation and not in the alert. An operator who believed the system would sound for a light vehicle on the ramp had one less reason to think anyone could be there, on a ramp that showed no intersection on the screen and none on the road. That effect is real and cannot be measured. The suppression was virtually certain to have been in place, and it likely contributed through that expectation. It is held at the standard of proof and no higher.

The impact and the injuries

The front left of the heavy vehicle struck the right side of the light vehicle at 24 km/h, 6.2 m into the ramp from the spur mouth, and the accelerometer logged 1.9 g longitudinal. The light vehicle was crossing the descending lane, turning right, with its right side to the heavy vehicle. It was pushed 18.4 m down the ramp and rolled onto its left side against the inner windrow at about 11 m. The roll-over protection structure was deformed inward at the right rear mounting but was not breached, and the seatbelt was fastened with loading marks on the webbing. The driver's fractured left clavicle, three fractured left ribs and small pneumothorax are the injuries of a side impact and roll-over in which the structure held and the restraint was worn. The collision was the mechanism by which the convergence became an injury, and the roll-over was how it produced the injuries that make this a serious occurrence.

What worked

The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1]'s response was quick. The download shows 1.4 seconds from the last steady data point to a service brake pressure rise, inside the 1.5 seconds the plan's own stopping distance calculation allows. Braking was correct and immediate, with the retarder at maximum. Slowing from 37 km/h to 24 km/h removed about 58 per cent of the impact energy, since the square of 24 over 37 is 0.42. The [the other attendee 1] steered 14 degrees right, which took the heavy vehicle away from the open side, where the pit edge dropped more than 100 m. The [the other attendee 1] said the choice was between that and taking the light vehicle over the edge. The [the other attendee 1] then followed dispatch's instruction to stay in the cab until the ramp was stopped, and afterwards stayed with the driver until the emergency response team arrived.

Dispatch stopped all Stage 4 traffic 49 seconds after the collision and paged the emergency response team at 02:48. The night shift supervisor considered the dispatcher's handling to be of the highest standard and would not change anything about it. The roll-over structure and the seatbelt also loaded to the point of showing it and did their work.

Other factors that increased risk

The Stage 4 heavy vehicle roster is 14 nights on and 7 off, at 12 hours 30 minutes a shift, within a fatigue standard that permits up to 14 consecutive night shifts. The collision was on night 10, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift. The roster was virtually certain to exist, and it was unlikely to have contributed to this occurrence. The recorded 1.4 second response was inside the plan's allowance, and the arithmetic shows that braking at 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m with a response time of zero still ends in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24. The 1.4 seconds cost about 7 km/h of impact speed and not the collision. The roster remains a standing exposure, because every [the other attendee 1] on the swing drives a 220 tonne vehicle in the small hours of a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night, where the defence against an unlit hazard at an unlisted intersection is the driver's own detection and response. On that basis it was likely a factor that increased risk.

The night shift is rostered with one qualified mines rescue paramedic at the surface medical facility. The second responder, who must be present before any extrication begins, is called from the accommodation village 11 km from the gatehouse and brings the hydraulic rescue tool in the second response vehicle. The team was paged at 02:48, the first response vehicle reached the scene at 02:54, and the second responder arrived at 03:07. The driver was extricated at 03:14, 27 minutes after the collision. The post-incident review measured a delay of approximately 9 minutes, from the completed primary survey at 02:58 to that arrival, and found no effect on the clinical outcome because the injuries were stable and the driver was conscious throughout. The arrangement was virtually certain to exist. It was unlikely to have influenced this occurrence, because the collision and the injuries were complete before the page. On the tests so far, it was very likely a factor that increased risk, since nothing about the arrangement changes with the severity of the casualty it is called to.

Matters examined and not settled

Fatigue of the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1]

Whether the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] was fatigued to a degree that degraded performance could not be determined either way, and the evidence is evenly balanced. In favour, the collision came on night 10 of 14, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift and inside the window of lowest circadian alertness. The [the other attendee 1]'s voluntary sleep record gave between 5 and 7 hours on each of the preceding ten days, with 6 hours 30 minutes on the last. The [the other attendee 1] described being tired "the way you are on night ten". The task on the approach, detecting a hazard in an unlit pit, is the kind that fatigue degrades first.

Against that, the [the other attendee 1] described the tiredness as ordinary, "Not falling asleep tired", and said they were awake and driving properly. Every limit in the contractor's fatigue standard was met. The recorded 1.4 second response and the correct braking and steering show none of the usual signs. Settling it would need a detailed fatigue assessment, which was not pursued. Even if fatigue had been shown, it would not have contributed, because with zero response time the collision still occurs at about 17 km/h.

Airborne dust on the ramp

Whether airborne dust delayed the heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1]'s sighting of the beacon could not be settled. Dust was likely present on the ramp above the intersection, but whether it affected the sighting is about as likely as not. The water cart's last pass on that section was at 01:10, 1 hour 37 minutes before the collision against a standing 90 minute interval, so a pass was 7 minutes overdue. The fixed dust monitor on the RL 328 bench, 180 m up-ramp of the intersection and above the grade break, logged PM10 at 142 micrograms per cubic metre in the 02:40 to 02:50 interval, against a shift mean of 88. The [the other attendee 1] described dust hanging on the ramp, and the descent was behind the vehicle's own lights in an unlit pit, where airborne dust is worst for a driver.

The case against is strong. The reading was well below the site trigger of 250 and measures particulate at a monitor head and not what could be seen from either cab. The light vehicle driver was firm that the air was clear, that the ramp surface and the windrow could be seen, and that Bindarra's dust is nothing like that at other sites where the driver works. The heavy vehicle [the other attendee 1] declined to say that the dust made a difference. The survey places first sight of the beacon at 53 m, and the download puts the heavy vehicle there at its last steady data point, so a sighting at the geometric limit leaves dust little to explain. A 1.4 second response from that point also leaves no room for a delay to hide in. The matter could not be settled because the first sighting is inferred from that data point and the survey and was not recorded, and nothing at Bindarra measures visibility. The evidence cannot exclude dust costing a fraction of a second at the edge of the lights.

Considered and not found contributing

The road surface was very unlikely to have been wet or slippery. The weather station recorded no precipitation and the last rainfall was 26 days before the occurrence. The investigator found the ramp dry throughout at 06:20, the braked scuff ran continuously for 58 m with no sign of a locked or sliding wheel, and the deceleration achieved matched the unit's dry figure.

The service brakes were very unlikely to have delivered less than their tested deceleration. The tests on 03/08/2026 and 14/08/2026 gave 0.80 and 0.82 m/s2 net, and the occurrence download gave 0.78, which is inside the normal scatter of the two tests. Brake pressure rose from 0 to 620 kPa in 0.4 seconds, the retarder was at maximum, no defect had been raised in the twelve months before, and the pre-start check sheet for 11/08/2026 was complete.
