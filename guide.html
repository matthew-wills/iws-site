The user guide, as the app ships it

This is the same guide you will find under Help inside the application, rendered from the same source, so it matches the version you download. The ideas behind the method are on the Methodology page; this guide is about operating the software.

Overview

The Investigation Workflow Suite maps a safety occurrence as a causal diagram. Every suspected event or condition becomes an object on the canvas, is tested against evidence with the E/I/I framework (Existence, Influence, Importance), and is connected to its consequences with causal arrows.

A finished map reads in two directions at once. Left to right is time: the sequence that led to the occurrence. Bottom to top is organisational depth: from what individuals did, up through the conditions and controls that shaped their actions, to the organisational influences behind those controls. The causal chains converge on the critical event at the bottom right.

The discipline of the method is that nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests. Objects carry their test state visibly, so anyone reading the map can distinguish a proven contributing factor from an untested idea at a glance.

The canvas

The six horizontal lanes are organisational levels. The faint italic text inside each lane suggests the kinds of factors that belong there.

  • External Influences: Influences from outside the organisation: regulators, sector standards, oversight and guidance.
  • Organisational Influences: The organisation's own systems: hazard recognition, management of change, risk evaluation, training requirements, organisational communication, assurance activities.
  • Risk Controls: The defences that should prevent or catch the occurrence: procedures, alerts and warning systems, training, operational supervision, work scheduling, facilities and infrastructure.
  • Local Conditions: The conditions on the day: weather, workload, fatigue, distractions, health, knowledge and experience.
  • Individual Actions: What people did: handling, planning, inspecting, communicating, documenting, monitoring.
  • Occurrence Events: The event sequence itself, converging on the critical event.

The heavier line above Local Conditions separates the organisational levels (above) from the individual and situational levels (below). Drag any lane boundary to resize it; the lanes and objects below move together, so nothing falls out of its lane.

Running an investigation

The order below is the method, not a suggested reading order. Several steps are only correct if they happen before another one: gather before evidence perishes, build the timeline before the map, settle the interview record before drafting. These are the same phases as the Dashboard tab's checklist, so the tool and this page describe one method rather than two.

1Notification and triage

Decide whether there is an investigation, and start the record the moment there is.

  • Create the investigation from File, New investigation, and name it for the occurrence rather than the reportFile menuThe name reaches every export and every draft. An occurrence keeps its name; a report is one of several things you will produce about it.
  • Work down the triage questions and answer each Yes, No, or N/AChecklistAn unanswered question is not the same as N/A, and the header count tells you which is which at a glance.
  • Record the decision to investigate, and the reasoning behind it, as your first log entryLogThe log is contemporaneous. An entry written now is evidence of when you knew something; the same entry written in a fortnight is a recollection.
2Initiation and administration

Set the scope and the clock before the work spreads.

  • Write the scope into the notebook: the questions the investigation must answer, and what it will not examineAcciMap notebookScope creep in a safety investigation is usually invisible until the report will not converge, and the half that gets left out is the half nobody wrote down.
  • Set the standard of proof the investigation works to, and the organisation's acronym glossarySettingsThe evidence form warns when a passed test sits below the standard you set, and the glossary is what lets a draft expand an abbreviation instead of guessing at it.
  • List what has to happen and by when, dragging the list into the order you will work itChecklist, to-do list
3Containment

Deal with continued safe operation before investigating why it happened.

  • Decide whether anything must change now to keep operating safely, and log the decision either wayLogA decision that nothing was needed is a finding, and needs the same record as a decision to act.
  • Refer any risk outside the investigation's scope rather than absorbing itChecklistAbsorbing it is how an investigation stops converging.
4Evidence gathering

Get what perishes first, then build the sequence everything else will be argued against.

  • Work the evidence-gathering list and mark each source obtained, N/A, or still outstandingChecklistCCTV is overwritten, recorded data is overwritten, and testing for impairment has a window that closes. This is the only part of an investigation where a day's delay destroys evidence rather than costing time.
  • File each source as an evidence item with a caption, why it matters, and a referenceEvidenceThe reason you kept something is what you forget, and it is the half a draft most needs. A caption says what a thing is; significance says why it is in the investigation.
  • Mark every text extract as verbatim or as your own descriptionEvidenceThe application holds no recordings, so CCTV and audio arrive as text you typed. A verbatim record may be quoted; your description may not, and marking it is what stops a draft putting your words in someone's mouth.
  • Build the sequence of events, giving each one its source and its time basisTimelineDo this before the map. The map is an argument about what led to what, and you cannot argue about a sequence you have not established. Times in mixed bases with no correction recorded are the usual reason a sequence will not hold together.
5Interviews

Get each person's own account, early, and turn it into something the investigation can use.

  • Review the recorded data and the timeline before the interview, and draft your topics as open questionsTimeline, InterviewsAn interview is the one source you cannot re-run cleanly. Asking what the data already answers spends the time you needed for what only this person knows.
  • Work the preparation checklist before each interview: notice, fitness to attend, a support person, somewhere private, and enough timeInterviewsAn account given by someone fatigued, rushed or blindsided is worth less, and interviewing them again does not undo the first attempt.
  • Read the opening protocol at the start, and confirm the interviewee has understood it before you beginInterviewsWhat an interviewee believes the interview is for shapes what they tell you, so leaving it unsaid does not leave it unanswered.
  • Settle the record while it is fresh - the transcript where it was recorded, your notes where it was not - correct it against what was said, and finalise itEvidence, InterviewsReport drafting is told whether an interview's account is settled or still a working draft, and weighs it accordingly. Leave it unfinalised and the drafting reads it as shorthand, which is why an event overview can read thin.
  • File what the interview added as evidence, and add any new events to the timelineEvidence, TimelineAn interview that stays in the Interviews tab argues nothing on the map.
6Analysis

Test hypotheses rather than select them.

  • Place the critical event, then add an object for every event or condition that might have played a partAcciMapDo not filter at this stage. The method works by testing, and a hypothesis you later exclude still strengthens the record.
  • Test each object for Existence, then Influence, then Importance, recording the confidence behind each resultAcciMapThe category comes out of the testing. Choosing the category first and finding evidence for it is the failure this whole method exists to prevent.
  • Where the evidence genuinely cuts both ways, switch the test to Argument mode and record both sidesAcciMapBoth sides then reach the report, so it can resolve the contrary evidence instead of quietly dropping it.
  • Mark a test Inconclusive when it was examined and could not be settled, and say why in the commentAcciMapInconclusive is not the same as untested, and it is not a finding. It must still be discussed in the report, and your comment is what the draft discusses.
  • Connect the causal chains back from the critical eventAcciMapEvery arrow is a claim that one thing led to another, and the map is only as strong as those claims.
  • Finalise each object once its evidence is settledAcciMapThe green dot says the evidence would withstand review. Exports warn about anything left unfinalised.
7Reporting and review

Draft from the investigation, not from memory.

  • Check what each section will be drafted from before generating anythingHelp, What feeds whatA section receives what its style declares and nothing else. If a section is coming out thin, the usual cause is that what it needs has not been recorded yet, not that the model is weak.
  • Generate section by section, read each against the source material, correct it, then finalise itDraft reportA generated section is a proposal. Finalising is where you take ownership of the words, and it is the only signature the tool records.
  • Regenerate any section marked stale rather than keeping itDraft reportA section drafted from material that has since changed is quietly out of date, which is worse than being obviously wrong because it still looks finished.
  • Export the full set and file it with the investigationExportThe map image, the Evidence and Argument Tables and the collation together are the working record behind the report, and they are what a reviewer will ask for.
  • Assign findings to owners with timeframes, and close the investigation on the checklistChecklistA finding with no owner is an observation.

Building the map

The Analysis phase above, in detail. This is the part of an investigation the map exists for.

  1. 1Place the critical event

    Use + Critical event and put it in Occurrence Events, conventionally at the bottom right. Open it and record what happened and the proof of its significance. Everything else on the map exists to explain this object.

  2. 2Brainstorm hypotheses

    Add an object (+ Object) for every event or condition that might have played a part, placed in the lane where it belongs and roughly where it sits in time. Do not filter at this stage: the method works by testing, not by intuition, and a hypothesis that is later excluded still strengthens the investigation record.

  3. 3Gather evidence and test Existence

    Double-click an object, record the evidence, and answer the first question: did this actually exist or occur? A pass turns the border solid. A fail excludes the hypothesis, struck through but kept on the map.

  4. 4Answer the risk questions

    For objects that exist, decide whether the event or condition increased risk or decreased risk. The two are mutually exclusive. Increased-risk objects take sharp corners; decreased-risk objects become green positive events.

  5. 5Test Influence

    Did this factor actually influence the occurrence? A pass makes it a blue contributing safety factor and turns its outgoing arrows solid green: a proven causal path. A fail leads to one final question. A small Reason button sits beside the field (the single box in conclusive mode, each of the for and against boxes in argument mode) and opens a panel of common reasons under six headings: the mechanism, timing and sequence, place and exposure, alternative explanations, consistency with other occasions, and the strength of the evidence. Choose a heading on the left and each reason shows with a line on when it applies, so you can read before choosing; in argument mode each box offers only its own side's reasons. Choosing one inserts its starting sentence, which you then complete: at the start of an empty box, or on a new line if you have already written something. The arrow keys move through the panel, Enter inserts and Escape closes it. The list is editable in Settings.

  6. 6Test Importance

    A factor that did not influence this occurrence may still be worth addressing. A pass makes it an orange other risk factor, a finding in its own right. Failing both Influence and Importance excludes the object.

  7. 7Connect the causal chains

    Hover an object, drag from one of the ports on its edge, and drop on the object it led to. Build the chains back from the critical event: every arrow is a claim that one thing led to another, and the map is only as strong as those claims.

  8. 8Attach the supporting material

    The evidence form's right-hand panel holds working notes and the material behind them: click the capture zone and paste a screenshot or a manual extract, or drag a file from Explorer and drop it on the zone (PDFs included; the zone lights up as the file comes over it). The Evidence tab's own capture box takes a drop the same way. Each capture takes a short note on why it matters. The app is a tool, not a repository: everything captured is a copy embedded in the .sfm (files to 2 MB, images to 5 MB), so nothing breaks if originals move - and whole documents stay in your organisation's investigation folder, which remains the system of record. Capture the relevant page or passage, not the manual. The form opens at 90 per cent of the window, with that right-hand column taking about a third of it; drag the divider between the two to give whichever side you are working on the room, resize the form from its corner, and both are remembered for the next time you open one. Every field grows as you type. The Captured material sub-tab shows everything held across the investigation in one place, grouped by where it lives, by lane, or by kind, with a jump to wherever each item lives; drag the tabs themselves into whatever order suits how you work.

  9. 9Finalise and review

    When an object's evidence is settled, tick Finalised in its evidence form; the corner dot turns green. Then stand back: does every contributing factor have a proven path to the critical event? Are the excluded hypotheses genuinely closed? Exports will warn about anything left unfinalised.

From record to report

This chapter follows an investigation from what you record to the report a reader receives, and shows what each AI access setting changes along the way. The map, the evidence and the timeline have their own sections; this one is about how what you have recorded turns into words.

The record is the source

Everything a report says comes from the record: the objects on the map and their tests, the evidence behind them, the timeline, the interviews and the safety actions. A paragraph or a section of prose is a reading of that record at one moment. When you finalise one, the app keeps a fingerprint of the record it was finalised against, so it can tell you later when the record has moved on and the words have not. A model, where you use one, drafts from the record and is checked against it. It never adds to the record, and nothing it writes reaches a report until you have read it and finalised it.

The object analysis paragraph

The unit a report is built from is one object's analysis paragraph: the few sentences that argue what the record shows about that object. It is written on the Analysis panel of the object's form (labelled Outcome and consequences on the critical event). On the left of the panel, Analysis: the record shows the object's record read-only, exactly as the Record tab renders it; on the right is the paragraph, with a status badge (Not written yet, Draft, not finalised, Finalised, or Out of date) and the buttons Finalise, Revert to drafted and Clear.

A report's analysis section is these paragraphs, arranged by your analysis groups (or one group per lane until you set any), with a linking paragraph for each group written from Groups on the Record tab. The findings come from the test results themselves, so a paragraph argues a finding but never decides one.

With AI access off, the paragraph is optional. Where none is written, the record's own lines (the evidence, the test results and the causal links) stand in for it, and the record checks do not ask for one. With Chat or Endpoint on, every object is expected to have one, and the record checks list any that are missing.

Finalising a paragraph and finalising an object

An object's form has two separate Finalise controls, and they answer different questions.

  • The Finalised tick at the foot of the form says the object's evidence and tests are settled. The completion dot on the map turns from amber to green, and the Export dialog stops counting the object as unfinished. It says nothing about any wording.
  • The Finalise button on the Analysis panel accepts the paragraph's wording for the report. Only a finalised paragraph reaches the Record tab and the material a drafting pass is given. A paragraph that is written but not finalised is left out as though it had not been written, because nobody has accepted it yet.

Neither one sets the other. The usual order is to settle the evidence and tests, tick Finalised, then write the paragraph and finalise it. Finalising the paragraph first and then recording the test results in the same sitting is fine too: when you save the form, a paragraph finalised while it was open is kept current against the tests you recorded after it.

Once the record behind a finalised paragraph changes later (a test result, a piece of evidence, a causal link), the paragraph is marked Out of date. The badge says so, a note above the text says why with a Re-finalise against the current record button beside it, and the Finalise button reads Finalise against the current record. Read the paragraph against the record on the left, correct it if it needs it, and finalise it again.

The map shows where every paragraph stands. A small triangle in an object's bottom-right corner, opposite the completion dot, is green when the paragraph is finalised and current, and amber when it is written but not finalised or has gone out of date; hover over the triangle to see which of the two it is. An object with no paragraph carries no triangle.

What the Record tab shows, and when

The Record tab shows the record and nothing else. It is assembled again every time anything in the investigation changes, so there is nothing to refresh, and it never shows drafted text. Under the controls row it shows, in order:

  • the line Assembled in the (style) layout, and the sections that layout produces, in order
  • a count of what is outstanding: factors without an analysis paragraph, events without a narrative, and group and section narratives not yet written
  • every section of the layout, with each object from the map as a card under its own title
    • IF the object's paragraph is finalised and current
      • THEN the card shows the paragraph
    • ELSE IF it was finalised and has since gone out of date
      • THEN the card still shows it, under a note that it was finalised against an earlier version of the record, followed by the outline block it has fallen behind
      • and an Out of date button under the section opens the object
    • ELSE
      • the card shows the outline block: the evidence, the test results and the causal links a paragraph would argue from
      • and a Not yet written button under the section opens the object at its Analysis panel
  • Record checks down the right, under Errors, Warnings and Notes, each naming the object it is about
  • Also exported at the foot: the Evidence and Argument Tables, one collapsible table per object under its lane, with Export Evidence and Argument Tables as Word

Export the record as Word, in the controls row, writes the record as it stands, gaps included. At the No AI tier this is the report: your paragraphs where you have written them and the record's own lines where you have not.

How a section draft is assembled

The Draft report tab writes the report in the chosen style, one section at a time. When a section is drafted, the model is given the assembled record for that section: your finalised paragraphs and narratives, marked to be kept as you wrote them; the outline block for any object that has none; and an out-of-date paragraph as guidance only, never as wording to keep, since nobody has confirmed it still fits the record. That is From the assembled record (default), under Advanced beside the length choice. The model is also given the sections already finalised before this one, so it follows on from your approved text instead of repeating it.

An argued section such as the analysis is drafted a few factors at a time, by your analysis groups or by lane, and the note under the section's buttons says how many passes that is. The more paragraphs you have finalised, the less the model has to write for itself: a section drafted from a fully finalised record is mostly your own words, arranged and joined.

What comes back is a draft. Read it in Your report, correct it there by clicking into the text, and press Finalise. Editing a finalised section withdraws the finalisation, and a finalised section goes out of date when the record changes, the same as a paragraph, with Finalise reading Finalise against the current record until you have read it again.

Where the drafted report lives

Drafted text lives only on the Draft report tab, in Your report, the zone under the Drafting rows.

  • Your report shows the sections in the style's order as one document across the full page, exactly as Export report as Word writes them, with a marker on any section not drafted, not finalised or out of date. Each section is edited where it stands: click into it and a toolbar for undo, headings, bold, italic, underline, lists and a quote appears. A Clear on each section's row, or Clear all, takes a section back to not started after a confirmation, with an Undo straight afterwards. The section is still stored as markdown, which the checks, the audit and the export read, and only a real edit writes to it; the section's row then reads Hand-edited. Until a section is drafted it says so in one line, with how many object analysis paragraphs are finalised; those are read on the Record tab.
  • Whole report holds the report written as one piece, either by Draft whole report (Endpoint tier) or by Smooth the whole report, which edits your finalised sections together. It has its own Finalise, Revert to drafted and Clear, and Export whole report (Export smoothed report, for a smoothed one) writes it as a Word file of its own.
  • Check and export, with the report, checks the drafted text against the record and writes it out. Check the record needs no model; Audit against the record, Repair against the audit and Polish the prose join it at the Endpoint tier; Export report as Word writes the assembled sections, with Appendices on the export beside it.

The three AI access tiers

Settings, under AI drafting, holds one choice called AI access, and it decides what every Draft control in the app does: No AI (you write every paragraph against the record), Chat (the app writes the prompt and you carry it to a chat tool and back) or Endpoint (the app sends the prompt to the model set up under Models). It is the same setting for an object's paragraph, a group's linking paragraph, a section and the whole report. The pane says the same under each choice, with what leaves this computer at each.

Your licence level caps the choice, and the edition can cap it further; the lower of the two applies. Free, which is also what applies with no licence, allows No AI only; Professional and above allow all three. A line at the top of the pane says what your licence allows and to when, with Enter a licence or start a trial, or Licence details, beside it, and a choice above the cap says so on its own line. Your choice is kept: while a licence holds it lower, the pane says which level is in use for now. Licence and levels, below, covers applying one.

Chat

The app builds the prompt and you carry it to a chat tool your organisation has approved, and the reply back. The app makes no network call of its own. The copied prompt asks the model to draft the text, audit that draft against the record it was given (every claim found in the record, every probability word from the scale the prompt sets out, no test notation in the prose), correct it, and return only the corrected text. So there is one paste each way, and no separate audit to copy out.

  1. Open the object's Analysis panel and press Copy prompt. Under the button, a line gives the prompt's size in characters and estimated tokens, with a warning when it is longer than the chat paste limit (100,000 characters unless changed under Chat in Settings, AI access), since a chat window may quietly take only part of a long paste. Save prompt as file writes it out as a text file to attach instead. Where Settings, AI access sets the chat to two pastes, Copy prompt is replaced by 1. Copy instructions and 2. Copy record, pasted in that order.
  2. Paste it into the chat tool and wait for the reply.
  3. Paste the reply into the box under Paste the reply here and press Use this text (Cancel goes back without it). It lands as a draft, badged Pasted from chat.
  4. Read it against the record on the left. A pasted reply gets no automatic check, because you are the one who brought it back and signs it. Correct it, then press Finalise.

A section works the same way on the Draft report tab.

  1. The row marked Next in the Drafting zone is open when you arrive; click any other row to open it instead.
  2. Press Copy prompt and carry it to the chat tool, then paste the reply into the box that opens in the same row and press Use this text. On an argued section the row shows Pass 1 of 3 (or however many) with Copy prompt for this pass: paste each reply back with Use this text and the next pass appears, until the section is assembled with one heading per pass.
  3. Read the section in Your report, correct it there and press Finalise. Its row closes with a tick and the next one opens.
  4. If you want the wording improved, Copy polish prompt for this section copies a prompt that asks for the same meaning in better sentences; paste the result under Paste the polished section here. Nothing checks it for you at this tier, so read it against the section it replaces.
  5. Under Check and export, Check the record runs the mechanical checks over everything drafted, and Export report as Word writes the report.
Endpoint

The app sends the prompt to the model in use under Settings, Models, and the reply streams into place. This is the only tier at which the app sends investigation content anywhere by itself; Connecting a model, below, covers where that can be.

  1. On the object's Analysis panel, press Draft. It reads Drafting on (model) while it runs, with Cancel. The paragraph lands as a draft badged Drafted by (model), and mechanical checks run on it at once, listing anything that needs a look under the text.
  2. Under Audit, press Audit to have the checking model check the paragraph against this object's record. The verdicts show in place and are not saved.
  3. Read, correct and Finalise the paragraph.

On the Draft report tab:

  1. Either press Draft whole report at the head of the Drafting zone to draft every section in one run (best on a model with the context for the whole investigation), or press Draft this section in the Next row to draft one at a time. Length and Advanced sit on the same line and govern both.
  2. Read each section in Your report, correct it there, and press Finalise. Audit this section and Polish this section, in the section's open row, work on that section alone.
  3. Under Check and export, press Audit against the record once sections are finalised, then Repair against the audit for anything it flags, and Polish the prose last if you want the wording improved. Each repair or polish is shown beside the text it would replace, and nothing lands until you land it.
  4. Export report as Word.
Whatever the tier, a draft is a head start built from the record, and the investigator signs the report. Read every drafted paragraph and section against the record before you finalise it.

The workspaces

An open investigation shows a row of tabs, each one workspace of the suite. Everything they hold travels in the one .sfm file. Drag the tabs into whatever order suits how you work - the order is remembered on this machine.

The tabs, in the order the suite ships with, are Dashboard, Log, Evidence, Timeline, the map tab (named for the edition's own methodology, AcciMap in this build), Record, and Draft report. Each is its own section below.

Dashboard

Where the investigation stands, on the tab you open first. A band of tiles across the top counts what has been gathered and what has been settled: objects on the map and how many are finalised, evidence items, interviews, timeline events, outstanding to-dos, checklist questions answered, report sections drafted and finalised in the style you have chosen, and the errors and warnings the record checks are currently reporting. Every figure is read live from the file, so none of it has to be kept up to date by hand. Click a tile to go to the tab where that work is done.

Under the tiles sit the two lists the tab has always carried: the checklist, and the to-do list, which is the one running list of what still needs doing, fed from the checklist, from objects on the map and from anything you type here, with Completed beneath it.

The checklist is a sequential question flow for the whole investigation, answered Yes, No, or N/A (click an answer again to clear it). Guidance notes sit under the questions where a step needs prompting - including an evidence-gathering list (CCTV, recorded data, audio, records of recent change, impairment and fatigue assessment and so on) to help you think of sources before they are overwritten or lost. The header counts how many items have been addressed, and each section collapses to its header (click the title or chevron) so completed sections tuck away. The checklist seeds from the template in Settings when the investigation is created.

The to-do list beside it is the other half of the same idea. The checklist asks what an investigation of this kind should cover and is the same every time; a to-do is whatever this one needs next, in your own words, and no template could anticipate most of it: ring someone back on a number, chase the CCTV before it is overwritten, ask the roster office for the prior fortnight. Items stay in the order you added them. Ticking an item writes a line to the Log as a record of what was done and moves the item out of the list above into a collapsed Completed section beneath it, which you can expand to check or untick; unticking moves it straight back. Dropping an item with the cross removes it without logging anything, because it was never done. The list is yours as a working tool; nothing on it reaches a report.

A to-do can also be raised from inside an object's form on the acci-map, just under its notepad, for whatever you need to chase up before you can finish that object. It is the same list as this one, not a separate one: it shows here too, with a from: <object> line you can click to jump straight to that object on the acci-map, which is brought into view and flashed so you can see exactly which one it is (double-click the line to open its form instead), so what still needs doing is visible at any point, whether you are working the to-do list or the map, and it is easy to hand a job to another investigator.

Below it sits Distribution: who this investigation's outputs go to, as a Title and Name table. Add, remove, and reorder rows freely - it is appended automatically to the foot of every exported report, so it is never left off by hand. Copy as table puts the same rows on the clipboard as a table for pasting into a document the app did not produce, falling back to tab-separated text where a rich clipboard paste is not available. A new investigation starts with whatever rows the edition ships as defaults, if any; existing investigations are never changed.

Evidence counts finalised evidence items out of the total and jumps to Evidence. Interviews counts finalised interviews out of the total and also jumps to Evidence. The checklist panel

The panel is titled Checklist, with a count reading "{n} of {n} addressed" beside it. + Section adds a new section and asks for its title. Each section carries a chevron that collapses it to its header (clicking the title does the same), a count of how many of its items are answered, and a × that deletes the whole section. Within a section, each item offers Yes, No and N/A as its answer - click the one already chosen to clear it again - and a × that removes the item. The to-do panel

Titled To do, with a count reading either "nothing yet" or "{n} of {n} left". Type into Add something to do… and click Add to raise one. Each row carries its own tick, its text, a × that drops it, and, where it was raised from an object on the map rather than typed here, a from: <object> line naming that object. Ticking a row moves it down into a collapsed Completed section beneath the list, which expands so you can check or untick what is done.

The distribution panel

Titled Distribution, with a Title column and a Name column. Each row carries Move up, Move down and Remove this row, and + Row adds another beneath them. Copy as table puts the whole list on the clipboard, ready to paste into a document the app did not produce.

Log

The running sheet: a timestamped record of investigation activity, displayed as message bubbles grouped by day. Type a line, press Enter, and it is on the record. Entries cannot be edited afterwards (one added in error can be removed), which is what makes the log worth citing. The logbook is included in exports and in the drafting collation as the contemporaneous record.

The application writes to the same sheet as you work. A checklist question answered, a to-do added or ticked, an object created, renamed or removed, a test result recorded, evidence captured or finalised, an interview finalised, a draft landed, an audit run, a report exported, a shared-file conflict resolved: each one appears as its own entry, marked "auto" and shown in a muted bubble. Typing in a text box is never logged, only the change it settles. Use the All / Manual / Automatic filter at the top of the tab to read one kind at a time. An automatic entry cannot be edited or removed, which is the point of it, and both kinds go into the drafting collation and the report's investigation activity appendix, so a reader can follow the investigation's own timeline.

Reading an entry

Choose All, Manual or Automatic at the top of the tab to narrow the list to one kind. An entry that names an object on the map carries a → <object> link that opens it. A manual entry carries a ✕ for removing it; an automatic entry carries neither link nor cross, since there is nothing to remove and nothing of its own to open.

Adding an entry

The composer sits at the foot of the tab: a box reading Log an action, request, receipt, or decision… (Enter to add), with an Add entry button beside it for the same thing. Either way the line lands on today's date, in its own bubble, timestamped to the minute.

Evidence

Everything the investigation has gathered, under one tab with three sub-tabs: Safety reports, Interviews, and Captured material.

Safety reports

Reports submitted by the people involved: air safety reports, written statements, self-reports. Paste each one in as it was written, with the submitter's role, the date, and the report reference so it stays traceable. Do not tidy the wording; how someone described what happened is itself evidence. These are first-person accounts written close to the event, often before anyone has been interviewed, which makes them some of the best narrative material an investigation has, and they feed report drafting the same way interviews do. They need no writing-up pass of their own: a submitted report is already prose, in the submitter's own words.

Each report is marked as submitted under a confidential or just-culture scheme by default. That flag travels with it into drafting, where the model is told it may use the account to understand what happened and attribute it by role, but must not quote it verbatim or reproduce identifying detail from it. Those protections are the reason people report at all, and a drafted report is a document that gets distributed. Untick it only for a statement given without them.

+ Report adds a new sub-tab, titled by the report once you give it one. A report submitted under a confidential or just-culture scheme carries a small ● lock mark on its sub-tab, so the protected ones are visible at a glance without opening each in turn.

The safety report form

Report details carries the fields that place the report: Title, Submitted by (role), Submitted, and Reference. A Link to the original field and its Open original button sit beside them, for a report that is also filed somewhere else - a safety database, a shared drive - so you can get back to the original without hunting for it. The confidentiality tick is below them, as described above.

Report text is the single box the account goes into, pasted in as it was written. Supporting material is the same capture panel an object carries on the map, for anything that came with the report itself. Delete report, at the foot, asks before it removes the report.

Interviews

One record per interview. Each starts with a preparation checklist and an opening protocol seeded from the templates in Settings - answered Yes, No, or N/A and collapsible once complete, the same way as the investigator's checklist - then background fields and the boxes you write the interview up in.

+ Interview adds a new sub-tab. Double-click a sub-tab to rename it to the interviewee's name once you have it; Delete interview, at the foot, removes the interview after asking.

The record section opens with a badge reading Finalised or Not finalised, so an interview's status is visible without opening it. Below the badge sits Attendees - Lead interviewer, Assisting interviewer, Interviewee and Support person - then the collapsible checklists Preparation and Opening, answered Yes, No or N/A the same way as the investigator's checklist, and Background information for whatever sets the scene before the account itself.

An interview here is a record, and the app asks one question about it first: was it recorded?

Recorded - the transcript is the record. Import transcript… brings in the Teams .docx or .vtt download, or paste it straight in. It is what was actually said rather than what you caught and remembered. Correct it where the transcription is wrong, because Teams mishears names, readbacks and technical words, and a record nobody may fix is worse than one they may. Your own notes sit alongside it as your observations.

What you imported is kept exactly as it arrived, under Original transcript above the box you type in. You are correcting a working copy; the source stays where it is, so a correction can always be checked against what the recording actually produced. The card says whether the working copy has moved since the import, and Show original transcript opens the text itself. Nothing edits the original, and only the corrected copy is finalised, exported or read when a report is drafted - a mishearing you have already fixed must not come back as evidence. Remove transcript takes both away, since a source with nothing left to compare against is only clutter.

Not recorded - your notes are the record. Write them up while the interview is fresh. If it turns out there was a recording after all, importing a transcript switches the interview to Recorded.

You write the interview up in three windows. Free recall is what the interviewee told you in their own words, before you asked anything, in the order they told it. Additional questions is what you asked once the free recall was finished, and what they answered. Follow-up questions is what is still open: what to put to this person again, or to check with someone else. Under them is your own notes box, for what you observed rather than what was said. Where the interview was recorded the transcript is the record and the three windows wait behind Add your own notes, for your own additions to it.

Give every speaker in a transcript a role. The names stay in that box: the record and the report see only the roles you set. A speaker you leave unmapped still reaches the record, but as an unidentified speaker rather than by name - dropping their turns would lose evidence, and keeping the name would put it in the report. The mapping is deliberately yours to make: a model that guesses which speaker was the captain can attribute a remark to the wrong person, and nothing downstream would catch it.

Where the interview was run by another investigator, add their notes with Add notes from another investigator rather than pasting them into your own box, so whose account is whose stays clear in the record and in drafting.

Investigator's notes, at the foot of the record, has its own small toolbar - B, I and • List - for notes written in your own words rather than in the structured windows above, formatted enough to stay readable without being another document to maintain.

Finalising, and reading the record back

Finalise interview when the record says what was said. Report drafting is told the account is settled rather than still moving, and the page stops being a form: the boxes give way to the record itself, read as one document - who was interviewed and when, the background you noted, then the corrected transcript or the three sections in order, then your own notes, with anything you left empty simply not there.

From then on, clicking the interview opens that document straight away rather than the form it was filled in on. A recorded interview carries a long transcript and a page of notes, so a small control above it chooses which to read: Transcript, Notes or Both, which is where it opens. The original transcript stays reachable under the document for comparison. The speaker's name at the start of each turn is shown in bold, here and in the transcript box while it is being edited, so the finished interview is easy to follow. The bold is only how it is shown: the transcript itself is stored as plain text. An interview that was not recorded has only its notes, so no control is offered.

Reopen puts the boxes back, and is the only way to correct a finalised record: the lock is there so an account cannot drift underneath a draft that already quotes it.

Captured material

Evidence pasted into an object's form, or captured from the timeline or directly, is held once: it shows here too, as a grid of tiles that fills the width of the window, so you see what rests on what without leaving the map. The grid runs newest capture first, within each group, so the file you just dropped in is the one you see.

Each tile carries its caption as its title and the material itself below it: an image as a picture, a text extract as its first few lines, and a file as an icon for its type (PDF, document, spreadsheet, audio, video) with its name. A text capture renders as formatted text rather than raw markdown, so a pasted quote reads as an indented extract. Rest the pointer on a tile, or move to it with Tab, and the rest of the record comes up over the material: category, significance, reference, when it was captured, whether it is finalised, and what rests on it. Each object listed there is a jump: clicking it takes you to the object on the acci-map, brought into view and flashed. View shows the capture at full size, and Delete removes the item from the register after showing you everything that rests on it.

Click a tile, or press Enter on it, to open the item's own form, where the capture is shown in full above the caption and significance (see The evidence item form, below). The capture is never editable, because it is what was captured. A file is not shown in the app at all: its form and View give its name and size and an Open button, which writes a copy into the investigation folder's evidence subfolder, beside exports, and opens it in whatever this computer uses for that kind of file. Your own PDF reader beats anything the app could show you. A file kept beside the investigation rather than inside it (see Settings, Evidence, Attached files) says so under its name, with its size, and opens where it already sits; if it is not there - an .sfm passed on without its folder - the tile says "attachment not found" rather than failing when you click Open. Finalise, in the form, saves it and settles the item: its tile carries a Final badge and its form opens locked, to be read, with Edit the only way back in. Unlink, in the form, takes the item off the object you opened it from and leaves it here, in this view. Captured material also files by category, shown as sub-tabs. Only categories something is filed under appear; "Show all" reveals the rest greyed out, where they work as a prompt - nothing under CCTV yet, on an occurrence that happened in view of a camera.

The category list itself is edited in Settings - Evidence - Categories: add, rename, reorder, or remove one, or reset back to the built-in list. An item always keeps its category as its own text, so renaming or removing one here only changes what the picker offers for the next item filed, never material already captured, and typing a category that is not on the list still works - it is kept with that item and shown for it.

An image or a text extract can also go into the report. Its form carries one tick, Include in the report as a figure, off until you set it, and the card then shows a Figure badge on its tile, so you can see at a glance which captures are headed for the document. A dropped file gets no tick, and its form says why: there is nothing in a file that can be put on a page, so capture the page you want as an image instead. A ticked capture is numbered in the order the report argues the objects the captures belong to, so Figure 1 is the first one a reader meets, whichever kind it is, and its caption and reference are what print under it. An image prints inside a thin frame with the caption below; an extract prints as the words themselves in a frame of the same weight, quoted lines set in behind a rule, with the reference as a source line under them. Write the caption as you want it read, because that is the wording that goes into the report.

The application holds no recordings. Audio, CCTV and recorded data arrive as text somebody typed, so a CCTV item is a time and what happened at it, with a screenshot if it helps. That makes one distinction worth stating on every text extract, and each one carries it: Verbatim for the source's own words - a transcript, a quotation from a manual, the text of a message - and My description for your account of what you watched or heard. A report may quote the first. The second is already your interpretation, so a draft will summarise it and never put it in quotation marks or attribute it as anyone's speech. Anything you have not marked is treated as a description, because putting your words in someone else's mouth is the mistake you cannot defend and a reader cannot detect.

Group by, above the grid, chooses how it is split: Nothing (one grid), Where it lives, Category, Lane, or Kind, each group headed with its count. Size beside it sets the tiles at Compact, Standard or Large; the grid fits as many across as the window allows, and Full size cards brings back the original cards with every detail on show. This computer remembers the choice. Evidence attached to an object, a timeline event or a safety report appears as the same small tile in its own form. Category sub-tabs run across the top, starting with All, each carrying a count of what is filed under it; a category nothing is filed under yet sits greyed out behind Show all.

Add to the evidence register is the capture zone itself, sitting above the grid on All; opened from a category sub-tab it reads Add to <category> instead, and anything that lands there is filed under that category from the moment it is captured. It takes a pasted image, a pasted block of text, or a file dropped in from outside the app, the same as the capture panel on an object's own form.

The evidence item form

The form is four headed sections in one scroll. The first is the capture itself, headed with its type (Image, Text extract, PDF and so on), exactly as it was captured and never editable here. An image shows at a readable size with its shape kept; click it, or View full size, to see it whole. A text extract grows with its content up to about half the window and then scrolls, fading at the foot while there is more to read; Read in full opens it in the viewer and Copy text puts it on the clipboard. A file is one row with its type, name and size, and Open hands it to whatever this computer uses for that kind of file.

About this evidence holds Caption and Significance, which start small and grow as you write. Category offers the list set up in Settings and also takes a word that is not on it, which is kept with the item either way. Reference is wherever this item is filed, cited, or numbered outside the app.

In the report holds the tick Include in the report as a figure for an image or a text extract, as described above; a file's form says why it has none. On a text extract, choose My description or Verbatim to say whose words these are, and use Summary for the report for the wording a draft will actually use for this extract, with the draft and prompt buttons on the section's heading.

Used by lists everything that rests on the item, each a jump to it. Opened from an object, that object's row carries Unlink from this object, which takes the item off that object without removing it from the register.

Delete item sits at the left of the foot, and asks first, showing everything that rests on the item. At the right, Cancel closes the form without saving, Finalise saves and settles the item, and Save writes your changes and closes it. Ctrl+Enter saves as well. A finalised item opens locked, its fields shown as plain text, with Edit and Close at the right. Edit unlocks it in place without asking; Cancel locks it again with nothing changed, and Save writes your changes and reopens the item.

Timeline

The investigation's sequence of events: when each thing happened, where that time came from, and how firmly it is held. It is both a working tool and a deliverable. On screen it is a drawn timeline you arrange, with a table under it you edit like a spreadsheet, and the two are views of one record, so a correction in either moves both. At the end it is the sequence-of-events table in the report, and the chronology the drafting model is given.

Entering a time

Type a time the way you would write it. The app reads 18:05, 1805, 18:05:30, 180530, about 1805 (or ~1805, c. 1805), an optional leading date like 28/08 or 28/08/1998, and a date on its own. What you type also says what you know: typing 18:05 records a time known to the minute, and the report writes it as 18:05 rather than inventing a 00 on the end. Typing 18:05:30 records a recorded stamp, and about 1805 records an approximation, which reads as "about 18:05" wherever it appears. The Known to picker on the event card sets the same thing by hand, and adds Day only for an event you can place on a date but not in a clock. An investigation that has never touched this reads to the second everywhere, exactly as it always did.

Sources rarely agree, so the card asks what the recorded time is expressed in: UTC (the default, no correction needed), Local, or Other for a source clock or a device time. Local and Other then ask two things. Which clock names it (AWST, UTC+09:30, ship's clock, FDR clock), and the correction to UTC is the signed adjustment, like -8:00 for a zone or -0:02:14 for a clock error. The card shows the calculated time as you work. The corrected time drives the ordering and every output, while the recorded time and its correction survive into the report, so the trail can be audited: "06:45:30 (recorded 06:47:30 ARFF watch room clock, correction -0:02)". Corrected events are starred on the axis and chipped in the table. The whole investigation can also be worked in local time rather than UTC with the clock toggle, which changes the labelling everywhere including the exports; per-event corrections work either way.

What an event records

The event itself is the one line that appears on the bubble and in the sequence; detail is what happened more fully, including anything uncertain about the time. Entity is who or what the event belongs to, and gives the bubble its colour. Duration turns a point in time into a span, and an event can carry several, each measured from its start: take-off to aircraft stopped, and take-off to V1. Location is where it happened: a position, a berth, a chainage, a runway.

Two fields say how much weight the entry carries. Kind of source is what sort of source it is (recorded data, voice recording, a traffic service record, surveillance, CCTV, physical evidence, a maintenance or operator record, an interview or account, a document), offered as a list you can type over, and source of information is the specific reference that lets a reader go and check it. They print together: "[Source: Recorded data: FDR channel 12]". Status says how firmly the entry is held: established for something supported by a recorded or physical source, reported for an account that has not been independently confirmed, and estimated for a time you derived yourself. It is blank until you set it, and a blank one claims nothing.

Report narrative is the event in report voice, one or two sentences. The occurrence section of the report is built from these; an event left blank there renders as its plain timeline line instead, and the Record page shows it as a gap to fill.

The event card

+ Event opens a blank card; Open on a table row or a double-click on a bubble opens that event's own. The card runs in four headed sections, in the order you would fill them in. Event comes first: the Event line itself, then Date (DD/MM/YYYY), Time (UTC), which reads Time as recorded once the basis is anything but UTC, and Known to. A compact row below holds Time is expressed in with its three choices, Which clock and Correction to UTC where the basis is Local or Other, Effective time (UTC), which is worked out from all of that and cannot be typed into. Duration sits under that row as a short list, one entry per span: What it spans (optional, such as Take-off to V1), Length or or end time, which is the other way into the same length, then a colour (Entity colour, the default, or one of six duration colours chosen to stay apart from the entity colours and to read for colour-blind viewers) and where it is drawn, On the axis or Below. The arrows reorder the list and × removes an entry; + Add duration adds another. An event with no duration shows one blank entry, so a first span is just a length typed in. The first entry with a length is the event's main span: it is the end the report's chronology prints, and the one an older copy of the app reads. Location, Phase and Entity close the section, followed by This investigation's own columns where the investigation has added any.

Evidence holds Kind of source and Status together, then Source of information, Detail and Supporting material. Report narrative carries the narrative box, with the prompt actions (Copy AI prompt and Save prompt as file, at the chat tier) on the heading's line and the prompt's size estimate in small type beneath them. Analysis links ends the card with Linked analysis objects: Link an object… adds one and a × beside each takes it off. The foot stays in view however far the card scrolls, with Delete event on the left, which removes the whole entry, and Done on the right, which closes the card.

Phases, and the order events are read in

Phase is a free label for the part of the sequence an event belongs to: pre-departure, the transit, the occurrence, the response. Set it on the card or in the table, and the chronology in the report, the exports and the drafting collation breaks into subheadings wherever it changes. Phases already in use are offered as you type, so one phase does not end up with four spellings. A phase that comes round again gets its heading again: the sequence is never reordered to suit the grouping, and an investigation that uses no phases prints with no headings at all.

Order follows the times, always. Correct a time and the sequence re-sorts; drag a bubble and only its layout moves, with the stalk still pointing at the true tick. Where two events share the same corrected time, and only there, the table offers arrows to put them in the order they actually happened.

Linking to the map and to the evidence

An event can carry supporting material the same way a map object does: paste or drop it in, and it is filed once in the evidence register and linked here. Bubbles with material show a paperclip, and binary captures are written out beside the report when you export.

An event can also be linked to the objects on the map it bears on, under Linked analysis objects, which is you saying that this is the moment that factor acted. Two things follow. The chronology names the factors beside the event, so the sequence and the analysis cannot be read apart, and the record check that looks for a causal arrow pointing backwards in time reads the linked event first, ahead of the weaker readings it otherwise has to fall back on (a shared document, a time written into the evidence text, or where the box sits on the canvas).

Your own columns

Every organisation keeps one or two things on a timeline that no application can guess: an occurrence reference, a flight phase code, a crew position. Columns… on the toolbar adds them to this investigation. A column has a name and a kind, where text takes anything, number refuses what is not a number, and choice offers a list you can still type over. They appear as columns in the table, as fields on the event card, and in the chronology as "[Occurrence reference: 2026/0412]". They belong to the one investigation, so adding one needs no new version of the app, and deleting one says how many events carry a value before it takes them with it.

The Columns dialog

Columns… on the toolbar opens it, headed Timeline columns. Each column already in the investigation lists its name, its kind (Text, Number or Choice), the choices it offers where the kind is Choice, and Delete, which first says how many events already carry a value in that column before it takes them with it. Below the list sit a new column's own name and kind, beside Add column, and Done closes the dialog.

Working the timeline

The toolbar carries the controls you reach for most, in two groups. The view group decides how the timeline is shown: a spacing choice of Sequence or Proportional, a working-clock choice of UTC or Local, and the zoom control. The editing group changes the record: Undo (Ctrl+Z) and Redo (Ctrl+Y), + Event, + Entity, Import… and Columns…. Under it sits a quick-add box, reading "Quick add: 14:42:05 Aircraft departs the seal - or 28/08 06:10 Wreckage exam begins (Enter, times UTC)", the inline way to log an event as it is called out rather than opening the card. Below that, the entity legend shows one chip per entity in use; clicking a chip opens its own small editor, with the name, the colour swatches, a count of how many events use it, Delete entity and Done.

Add an event with + Event for the full card, with the quick-add line (21:43:52 Aircraft departs the sealed surface then Enter), or by typing in the table. A new event inherits the last one's phase, since a sequence is usually entered a phase at a time. Create entities with + Entity; each takes a colour, its events' bubbles match, and clicking a legend entry renames, recolours or deletes it (deleting warns how many events use it and leaves those events in place with no entity). Bubbles resize from their corner handle, and every timeline edit is undoable.

The spacing toggle switches between Sequence (even spacing, readable, not to scale) and Proportional (true time gaps). The canvas pans and zooms like the map: drag empty canvas to pan, Ctrl+wheel to zoom at the cursor, wheel to travel along the axis, and Fit to frame everything. Timelines can span days, weeks or months, and day changes are marked on the axis with dated dividers. A duration placed on the axis is drawn as a translucent bar laid over the axis in its colour, starting at its event's anchor dot (its corrected start) and ending with a small tick and its label, such as Take-off to V1, 27 s. Bars that overlap in time sit on thin tracks hugging the axis, alternately just above and just below it, which is how concurrent activity becomes visible, and an approximate start fades in rather than starting sharp. A duration placed below goes on a track in a band beneath the lowest cards, starting at its event's position, with a dotted guide up to the anchor dot and a small dot repeating it, so a second or third span does not crowd the axis; overlapping bars there take a track each. Where a label would run into the next bar or another label, it is shortened to the length alone, or left off. Hover a bar for its name, start, end and length, and its event card lights up with it (hovering a card lights every one of its bars); click a bar to open the event. In Proportional spacing a bar's length is to scale; in Sequence spacing it runs to the events it overlaps, because there the position is the event's order, not its time, and a bar that outlasts every event ends in a chevron. A card with supporting material shows the paperclip count on its own line at the bottom, clear of the title. The exported timeline image draws the same bars.

The zoom control in the toolbar's view group gives the same reach with your hands off the mouse: − and + step the zoom, the reading between them shows where it sits, and Fit returns to the whole timeline. A bubble carrying supporting material still shows its paperclip here, and nothing about reading the canvas changes once you are this close to it. The bar between the drawn timeline and the table resizes the two: drag it, or click it and use the up and down arrow keys, and the height is remembered on this machine.

The table underneath carries the same events as plain rows: Date, Time (UTC), whose heading follows whichever clock the investigation is working in, Phase, Entity, Event and Status, then any columns this investigation has added of its own, then the row's own actions. Move earlier and Move later appear only where two events share a time, for the ordering arrows already described above; Open takes you to that event's card, and a × at the end deletes the row's event outright.

A chronology already held in a spreadsheet comes in through Import…: choose a .csv or .xlsx, confirm what each column holds (the app guesses from the headings, including source kind, status, location and phase), and see exactly what will land, including any row it cannot read, before committing. A cell holding 18:05 imports as a time known to the minute, the same as typing it. Only the rows are taken; the file itself is not kept.

Where the timeline goes

Into the report as the sequence of events, under phase subheadings where you use them, with the correction trail, the status, the location, the source and your own columns on each line. Into the exports as a PNG of the drawn timeline, and into the report's appendix as that same image. Into the drafting collation as the same chronology, with each event's linked extracts beneath it, so a model writing the occurrence section is working from the record rather than from a summary of it. And into the summary, which takes the occurrence date from the timeline.

A worked example

A ferry grounding. The investigation works in UTC, and the vessel's clock was local time, ten hours ahead. For the plotter freezing you would enter the recorded time 18:07:06, set the basis to Local, name the clock AEST, enter the correction -10:00, and leave the precision at the second, because it came from the plotter's own event log. Kind of source is Recorded data, the source is "Plotter internal event log, cause code 0x51", the status is established, the phase is "The transit", and the event is linked to the map object for the unreported plotter defect. The deckhand's account that he was sent to collect fares is a different kind of entry: enter it as about 1804, kind of source Interview or account, status reported. Draft report

The report is built here, one section at a time, and it lives in the investigation file rather than only in an exported document. The page runs in the order the work does: Style at the top, then two zones. Drafting is getting the words: one row per section, in the order they are written rather than the order they print, each showing where it stands (not started, drafted, audited, finalised, or out of date because the record has changed, with a Hand-edited tag once you have changed the drafted wording). Your report, below it, is the result: the whole report assembled from its sections in printed order, which you read and correct where it stands, with Check and export beside it. Pick a style, then work through the rows: draft a section, read it in the report, correct it there, and Finalise it.

The model's draft is a first version built from the record, not the report: the investigator works from that head start, reads it against the record, corrects what is wrong or overstated, and finalises it before anything is published under their own name. The Audit action below is an extra check on top of that reading, not a replacement for it.

The Style band

What this style produces, an expandable line under the picker, open until the first section is drafted, is the longer version: every section of the chosen style with an explanation of what goes in it and which parts of the record it is drafted from. Both it and the section rows are read out of the style itself, so an organisation's own style describes itself here the same way the shipped ones do.

Under the picker itself is the short version: what the chosen style is for, in the style's own words, and a line counting its layout, the number of sections and what they add up to in words, followed by the section names in printed order. Where the app ships a worked example drafted in that style, an Example (PDF) link sits on the same line and opens it in your PDF reader. A style with no example simply has no link.

The style decides the sections, their order, and what each one may be drafted from. These are the styles this edition ships with:

  • Standard investigation. The full investigation report, and the longest of these styles: executive summary, event overview, PEEPO analysis, findings summary and appendices. Written for the safety department and the people who have to act on it.
  • Preliminary investigation update. About three pages for executives while the investigation is still running: what is known, what is still being looked at, and what is already being done. Carries no findings.
  • ATSB-style final report. A full-length public report in the ATSB shape: investigation summary, the occurrence, context, safety analysis, findings, then safety issues and the action taken against them. Written for an industry readership. Modelled on published ATSB reports (CC BY 4.0).
  • NTSB-style probable cause report. A full-length public report in the NTSB shape: factual information, analysis, then conclusions carrying the findings and a single probable cause statement with its contributing factors, followed by recommendations. Modelled on published NTSB reports (US government work, public domain).
  • AAIB-style causal and contributory factors report. A full-length public report in the AAIB shape: synopsis, factual information, analysis, then conclusions listing the findings and splitting causal from contributory factors, followed by safety action and recommendations. Modelled on published AAIB reports (Crown copyright, Open Government Licence v3.0).
  • Executive brief. Three to four pages of plain language for the people who decide: what happened, why it happened, what was found, and what is being done. No factual detail beyond what carries the argument.
The Drafting zone

Sections are drafted individually for two reasons. You can redraft one section without rerunning the whole report, which on a local model is the difference between a minute and twenty. And nothing regenerates over the top of your edits: a section you have approved is never rewritten. Instead, each section is given the sections already finalised before it, so it follows on from your approved text rather than repeating it. Working in the style's own order therefore gives the best result.

The actions on the whole report sit where they belong in the order of work. Draft whole report heads the Drafting zone, with the length target and where drafting starts from on the same line. Everything done to the report once it is drafted is in Check and export, with the report in Your report: Check, Repair once an audit has run, and Export. Each carries a line saying what it does, and the one action that is next is the only one drawn as the primary button, wherever on the page it sits, so the page always points at the same step the rows mark Next.

The Drafting zone's own header carries a count, such as "3 of 5 sections done", so you can see at a glance how much of the style is finalised against its total. With AI access at Chat or Endpoint, a row under it carries Length and Advanced, and at the Endpoint tier it also carries Draft whole report, which reads Redraft whole report once a whole-report draft already exists, with Cancel the run beside it while one is under way.

Each row shows the section's name, a status line and its state as a plain label: Not started, Drafted, Audited, Finalised or Out of date, with Hand-edited beside it once you have changed the drafted wording. A finalised row shows a tick in place of its number. Click a row to open it; only one is open at a time, and opening another closes the first. The open row says in one sentence what the section is for and holds its controls. The row marked Next is the first section still to draft, and it is the one open when you arrive; while it is closed it carries Draft this section, or Start this section where AI access is set to Chat. Once you finalise a section its row closes and the next one opens.

With an endpoint that has the context for the whole investigation, Draft whole report, at the head of the section list, does the opposite: the entire record goes to the model in one run (an outline, then every section against the full collation, a cohesion edit, and an adversarial grounding audit that checks every claim in the draft against the source material), so the report is written as one argument rather than assembled from sections drafted apart, fitted to the chosen length target without dropping established facts. The finished report lands in the Whole report area, and each of its sections also lands in Your report as a draft, so you still read, correct and finalise it one section at a time. A section you have already finalised is never overwritten; the run log names any it kept. The audit appears in the audit panel and the record check runs as it does for a section draft, and any claim the audit marks unsupported or contradicted is the model's invention and must be fixed or cut.

An open row

An open row holds what can be done to that section at your AI access tier. At the Endpoint tier, Draft this section starts it, reading Redraft this section once there is already a draft to replace, with Cancel beside it while it runs. At the Chat tier the same place holds Copy prompt (Copy prompt to redraft once there is a draft) and Save prompt as file, with the prompt's size under them; paste the reply into the box that opens and press Use this text. Finalise accepts it, reading Finalise against the current record when the record has moved since the draft was made, so finalising is a deliberate choice against what currently stands rather than against what the model saw. Revert to drafted throws your edits away and goes back to the last model draft, and, at the Endpoint tier, Audit this section runs the model audit over this one section rather than the whole report. Read it in the report scrolls to the section in Your report, where its text is. With AI access off, the row says so and points to the Record tab, which assembles the record into one document in the style's outline.

Your report

Your report shows every section in the style's printed order as one document, exactly as Export report as Word writes it, across the full width of the page. A section not yet drafted shows its heading and a marker saying so; one not finalised, or out of date, carries its marker above it with a Finalise button beside it. While no section is drafted for the style it says so in one line instead, with how many object analysis paragraphs are finalised; those are read in the Record tab's assembled view, which shows the record and never the drafted text.

Click into any section to edit it where it stands, as the document it is: a small toolbar appears with undo and redo, two heading levels, bold, italic, underline, bulleted and numbered lists and a block quote, and paragraphs work as they do in any word processor. There is no markdown to edit. Each section is its own editing area, so an edit can only ever change the section it is in. Behind the page the section is still stored as markdown, because that is what the model writes, what the next section receives as context, and what the checks, the audit and the exporter read; your edit is written back to it, and merely opening or reading the report changes nothing. An edit marks the section Hand-edited in its row and takes its approval back until you finalise it again, and Revert to drafted, in its row, returns it to the drafted wording. Tables and figures show as they will print but are not edited here. A bracketed marker, such as [NOT ESTABLISHED], is part of the text and must be resolved before a draft goes anywhere.

Every formatting option reaches the Word file as the same formatting: underline prints underlined, a block quote prints as an indented quote with a rule beside it, and the two heading levels print as Word headings. Underline has no plain-markdown form, so it is stored as <u>…</u>; the record check and the audit read the words without the tags. Nothing you format changes what the checks find.

To start a section again, press Clear on its row. A confirmation asks first, then the section goes back to not started, exactly as if it had never been drafted: its text, your edits, its approval and its Hand-edited tag are all gone, and the next Draft starts clean. Clear all, at the head of the Drafting zone, does the same to every drafted section of the style on show and to nothing else (other styles, the whole-report document and the review notes stay as they are). Straight afterwards a line under the Drafting heading offers Undo, which puts back what was cleared, and the Log records the clear and any undo. Undo never overwrites a section you have drafted again since.

An argued section such as Safety analysis drafts a few factors at a time rather than as one request - by your approved analysis groups when you have set any, by lane otherwise - and the note under Draft this section says how many passes that is. What the Draft controls do follows the AI access setting in Settings: off, they are hidden and you assemble the record into a document on the Record tab instead; Chat copies a prompt (one per pass, under Pass 1 of 3 and Copy prompt for this pass, for an argued section) that also asks the model to audit and correct its own draft, and takes a pasted reply back; Endpoint drafts straight into the section, in the same passes, and adds the audit and polish buttons.

Length, beside Draft whole report, is a compact choice between no target, Concise, Standard, Extended and a custom word target, with the words and pages of the chosen one shown beneath it. It applies wherever a length target is asked for, and it is remembered on this machine rather than in the investigation.

Drafting starts from the assembled record: your finalised paragraphs and narratives marked to keep exactly as written, and the record's own outline blocks where nothing is written yet, built the same way the Record tab assembles it. That is the behaviour, marked From the assembled record (default) under Advanced. The other choice there, From the collated evidence only, starts from the collated evidence alone instead, the way drafting worked before the record view existed, which is worth a look only when comparing the two. A drafted section gets a mechanical Record check for free - each factor's category and confidence word checked against what its own test result actually says, and, for a Findings section, its categories checked against the derived classification. It also reads the prose itself: more than a couple of semicolons per thousand words is flagged as a sign the text has not been edited into full sentences, and a Findings bullet that negates its subject ("No assessment was likely recorded") is flagged too, because the term then qualifies what did not happen rather than the absence itself and the finding reads as its opposite - it belongs in front of the whole statement instead ("it is likely that no assessment was recorded"). It runs automatically once an endpoint draft lands (never on a pasted chat reply, which is yours to read before it lands), and Check the record, in the Check group, runs it on demand over everything currently drafted. It is mechanical, not a model opinion, and sits separately from the model audit above it.

A Findings section whose list contains a finding graded at a band that may be stated with no hedge at all (by default, virtually certain) opens with one sentence saying so: "A finding stated without a likelihood term was established as fact." It is there for the reader, so a plain finding among hedged ones reads as a deliberate statement of fact rather than a missing word, and the Record tab, the Word export and the drafted report all carry it in the same place. It is fixed wording: a drafting pass is told to reproduce it exactly, and the record check and the model audit both leave it alone rather than reading it as one of the findings. Where every finding carries a term, the sentence is left out, since there is nothing to explain.

Check and export

Under the report, Check and export groups its controls by what they do. The Check group holds Check the record, joined by Audit against the record at the Endpoint tier. Repair, offered at that tier once an audit has run, holds Repair against the audit. Polish, also Endpoint only, holds Polish the prose. Export holds Export report as Word. Each one carries a line saying what it does, and whichever is next in the order of work is drawn as the page's primary button, wherever it sits.

Audit against the record, beside it in the Check group at the Endpoint tier, is the model pass over the assembled sections rather than one of them: it checks the report against the whole investigation, which is the only way to catch two sections that are each defensible on their own and together say different things. The Chat tier has no whole-report audit; its drafting prompts audit themselves instead. What the audit flags is what Repair against the audit acts on, described under LLM drafting below, and Polish the prose is the step after it: the same meaning in better sentences, landed only once the checks and a fresh audit show nothing moved.

Export report as Word assembles the sections in the style's order into a Word document and a markdown copy. A section that is missing, unapproved, or out of date is still included, but carries a marker saying so, and the cover page records that the report is incomplete. Silently dropping unapproved work, or letting it leave looking finished, would both be worse than a marker you have to delete.

Appendices on the export, beside the export it changes, decides what goes out behind the report itself: an image of the accimap, an image of the timeline, and the Evidence and Argument Tables. Each ticked one is appended after the report body under its own heading, in the Word document and therefore in any PDF made from it. The two images each take a landscape page of their own. The ticks are yours rather than the investigation's, so they stay put from one investigation to the next, and they apply to every report export, this tab's and the Record tab's alike, which is why the same ticks appear on both pages. An appendix with nothing to show, a timeline with no events on it, is left out rather than printed empty, and so is one the style already prints itself: where a style's own body carries the Evidence and Argument Tables, that tick is disabled and says "already in this style", rather than the report going out with the same tables twice under two lettering schemes. Beside the Evidence and Argument Tables tick, an Evidence and Argument Tables document tick reading List the evidence under each table adds, under each object's table, a line per attached item naming where it came from rather than the material itself, the same option the Record tab's Also exported card carries.

Figures travel with the report. An image you ticked on the Evidence tab goes in after the paragraph that first says "Figure 3", and where the prose never mentions it, at the end of the section that argues the object it belongs to; a capture no object claims closes the document in a Figures appendix. It prints at the text width inside a thin black border, with "Figure 3: your caption" under it. The drafting model is told the figure exists and what it shows, so it can refer to it by number, but whether the argument needs it is left to the draft and to you. In Word the number is a real caption field and every "Figure 3" in the text is a cross reference to it, so Insert Caption, Insert Cross-reference and a table of figures all work on what the app wrote, and adding a figure by hand renumbers the rest. The Record tab's assembled view and Your report show the figures in the same places, so what you read on screen is what the Word file carries.

A drafted section says "Figure 3" and nothing more, and what Figure 3 is gets settled again when the report is exported. So if you tick another image onto a factor the report argues earlier, every number below it shifts and sentences written about one picture now point at another. The record checks say so: a section drafted against a different numbering is reported as out of step, naming what each number now points at, and you redraft the section or correct the numbers yourself. Nothing is silently rewritten, because only you can tell which the sentence meant.

The whole report

Whole report, at the foot of Your report, holds the report written as one piece. Draft whole report fills it at the Endpoint tier, as above; Smooth the whole report instead edits your finalised sections together into one document: shared terms and tense, transitions between sections, repetition removed. It is edited in place the same way as the sections. It never adds, removes, or reweighs a finding, and it never replaces the section texts above it - the same Draft, Finalise, Revert and Clear controls apply, and it exports as its own file so it is never confused with the section-by-section report. It goes out of date the moment a finalised section changes underneath it, the same way any other drafted text does here.

The smoothing pass itself runs from Smooth the whole report. Once the smoothed text exists, Finalise accepts it, Revert to drafted throws your edits away and goes back to the last smoothed pass, and Clear drops the whole-report document altogether and returns to the section-by-section report alone. Export whole report (Export smoothed report for a smoothed one) writes it out as its own Word file, separate from Export report as Word above it.

Causal arrows

Arrows carry the causal argument, and their appearance reflects its strength. An arrow stays grey and dashed while its source object's Influence test is unresolved; the moment Influence passes, every arrow leaving that object turns solid green. A map whose chains are green from organisational influences down to the critical event is a completed causal argument. These arrows also decide reading order: wherever a report or the Record lists factors - Analysis, Findings, Issues - it lists each cause before the effects it feeds, following the arrows rather than where a factor first happened to appear on an earlier chain.

Arrows are claims. If you cannot say in one sentence how the source led to the target, the arrow is not ready to draw.

Evidence discipline

Three habits keep an investigation defensible. First, keep excluded hypotheses on the map: what was ruled out, and on what evidence, is as much a part of the record as what was confirmed. Second, record positive events: the defences and decisions that limited the outcome deserve reinforcement, and identifying them is part of the investigation's value. Third, finalise deliberately: the green dot is a statement that the evidence for that object is settled and would withstand review.

Each test also takes a confidence slider, with the percentage translated to a verbal probability ("likely", "virtually certain") so reports use calibrated language rather than bare gut feel. Beside the slider the form states what the position you have chosen reads as: the term, the whole band of percentages that term covers, and whether the value passes the standard of proof, fails it or settles nothing either way. The wording comes from the same table Settings edits, so a band edge or a term changed there shows here too. The standard of proof the investigation works to is set in Settings, in steps of 5 from 50 to 95 per cent: 50 is the civil balance of probabilities, 60 the figure some just-culture frameworks set, and 70, the default, the standard transport safety investigators commonly work to. Each step is named by the standard it is, and says which band of the probability scale it opens or sits inside, read off the scale as it stands, so the standard and the language of the report cannot drift apart even if you move a band edge. The form warns when a passed test sits below it. The threshold works both ways, so it also settles what counts as a fail, at its mirror, and leaves the band between the two inconclusive rather than forcing a choice the evidence does not support. Where the evidence genuinely cuts both ways, switch a test from Conclusive to Argument mode: it then records evidence for and evidence against side by side, and both appear in the outputs, so the report can present and resolve the contrary evidence instead of hiding it.

What is never sent

  • The to-do list. It is your working tool. What is outstanding on it says nothing about whether a line of enquiry is settled, and half of it is logistics.
  • Deleted map objects in the recycle bin. Deciding something is not part of the investigation should remove it from the reasoning, not leave it where a model can still see it.

Where you take ownership

Material does not flow onward on its own. At each of these points you decide, and until you do the application falls back to something weaker and says that it has.

  • Marking an extract verbatim or a description. A verbatim extract may be quoted. A description is your interpretation, so a draft summarises it and never quotes it or attributes it as speech. Unmarked material counts as a description.
  • Testing an object. Categories come from the E/I/I results, never from assumption, and the confidence you recorded travels with each result so a draft cannot state a matter more firmly than you did.
  • Declaring what a report section is drafted from. A section's Needs line names the parts it receives, and it receives nothing else. A section that declares nothing receives everything, so the declaration is the control.
  • Finalising a report section. A generated section is a draft until you finalise it, and a section whose collation has since changed is marked stale rather than silently kept.

LLM drafting

AI access, in Settings, decides how every Draft action in the app reaches a model - the same setting whether the object is a factor's analysis paragraph or the whole report. No AI writes from the outline with nothing leaving the machine at all. Chat copies a complete prompt (the record, the guard rails, the instruction to return only the paragraph) to the clipboard and opens a box to paste the reply back into. The prompt also tells the model to audit its own draft against the record it was given and correct it before replying, so one paste each way does the work of a draft and an audit. Under the copy button the app gives the prompt's size in characters and estimated tokens, warns when it is past the chat paste limit, and offers Save prompt as file for a chat tool that takes an attachment. A pasted reply lands as written, with no automatic check, because the investigator reads and signs whatever they bring back. Endpoint sends the same prompt, without the self-audit instruction, straight to the configured model and drafts in place, with cancel. A factor's Analysis panel shows the matching Draft control under its buttons; the whole-report pipelines below use the same setting. From record to report, above, walks through each tier step by step.

At the Endpoint tier, each factor's Analysis panel also has an Audit action: it checks the paragraph against that factor's own record - never the whole investigation - and reports verdicts in the same Checked / Supported / Unsupported / Contradicted / Distorted format as the whole-report audit. It runs on the checking model, set in Settings, Models as a model's Checking model (ideally a different, smaller model from the one that drafted, since a checker from the same family tends to wave through its own confident mistakes). The Chat tier has no separate audit to copy out, because its drafting prompt already carries one. The result is shown inline and is never saved with the investigation - it is a second opinion, checked again by hand, not a certificate.

A paragraph drafted on Endpoint is also swept by a set of mechanical checks the moment it lands: a factor whose Influence test did not pass being written as having contributed, a probability word other than the one recorded against the test, the investigation's own E/I/I notation leaking into report prose, and American spelling. These run with no model at all, so they are instant and always available; a Chat paste skips them, on the view that the investigator reads and signs whatever they bring back themselves. The warnings sit under the paragraph and clear the moment you edit the text or finalise it - they never block Finalise.

An Endpoint is usually a local LLM server (LM Studio, Ollama, or anything else speaking the OpenAI-compatible protocol) configured in Settings, in which case nothing leaves your network. The server needs CORS enabled and a capable model loaded with generous context - 32k tokens at minimum, more for a large investigation. It can run on this machine or on another one on the same private network - Settings has an "Add" preset for LM Studio or Ollama either way. Reaching LM Studio on another machine needs "Serve on local network" and CORS turned on in its Developer tab; reaching Ollama on another machine needs OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0 and OLLAMA_ORIGINS=* set on that machine. Once either is reachable, pressing Refresh list (or Test) in Settings, Models, fetches its model list and shows which model is loaded and at what context, so there is nothing to type from memory. The page never contacts a server on its own when it opens.

An endpoint can also be hosted: OpenAI's or Anthropic's public API, or an organisation's own gateway in front of one of them. Settings has an Add button for each, and for an Azure or gateway endpoint, so the protocol and address are filled in for you; all you supply is the key. You create the account and generate the key on the provider's own site and pay them directly for what you use. Settings takes the protocol, the header the key travels in, and the key itself - stored on this machine only, the same as the rest of an endpoint's settings, never written into the .sfm and never carried into an export or a run log. The difference that matters is where the prompt goes: whatever an endpoint drafts or audits, the record it is built from - the factor, the section, the whole investigation - is sent to that provider over the internet, the same way it would be typed into a chat window. The Settings pane says so in those words above the buttons. Point AI access at a hosted endpoint only once your organisation has approved investigation content leaving the machine; a local server keeps that content on your network regardless.

Draft whole report runs the same drafting and audit pipeline over the whole investigation in one pass, described under Draft report in The workspaces above.

Once there is an audit, Repair against the audit sends back only the sections something was flagged in, with only the flagged claims to correct, re-audits each one, and shows the result beside the text it would replace. A Findings or summary bullet is judged against the narrower seven-term scale those two sections are held to, not the full ten, so a bullet correctly narrowed to one of those terms is no longer flagged as unsupported, and a summary may also state a finding plainly, with no probability term at all, since the Findings section already carries it. Nothing is written until you land it, section by section: the model proposes and you accept, the same as everywhere else here. A section the pass could not improve is shown too, with the reason, because "the re-audit was no better" is worth reading.

Polish the prose comes after that, and Polish this section, in a section's open row beside its Draft and Audit buttons, does one section at a time. It asks for the same meaning in better sentences, and only that: no fact, figure, name, date, probability term or finding may change, nor the order the findings are stated in, and the headings, the figure mentions and the Findings preamble come back word for word. Then it proves it. The polished section has to point at every figure the drafted one pointed at, record the same terms in the same findings, and raise nothing new in the record check. Then a second reading sets the two texts side by side and lists anything the original says and the polished wording no longer says: a fact, a figure, a name, a date, a quantity, a probability term or a qualification. The list has to come back empty. Last, a fresh audit has to leave no more claims unsupported than before, and contradict no more. Anything less and the drafted wording stays, with the reason on screen, naming what went missing. What passes is shown beside the text it would replace and landed by you, section by section. The Polish group sits in the check and export group whenever AI access is at Endpoint, even before anything is drafted - disabled, with "Draft a section first" as its reason, rather than missing outright. A model also has Polish granularity under Advanced in Settings > Models, used only for a server on this machine: set to one paragraph at a time, an experiment for small local models, the pass rewrites a paragraph at a time instead of a section, puts a paragraph that lost something back as drafted and tells you which, and still holds the whole section to every test above before it may land.

Settings > AI access has its own tick under Endpoint, Polish each draft as soon as it lands, off by default and shown only at the endpoint tier. Turned on, a draft that lands - one section from its row, or the whole report from Draft whole report - goes straight into the same polish pass, over exactly what was just drafted, and is offered the same way the Polish button offers it: nothing lands unseen, and the repair pass is never chained onto this. The chat tier pastes every reply back by hand, so there is no automatic pass for it to run, and the tick does not show there.

Every drafted text carries where it came from and whether it still matches the record. A badge reads "Drafted by" and the model's name, or "Pasted from chat", and a document brought in from elsewhere says that instead. When the record underneath it changes - a test result, a factor's paragraph, the interview it rested on - the text is marked Out of date and its Finalise button reads "Finalise against the current record" until you have read it again. Only a finalised, current text feeds a later drafting pass, so neither an unreviewed generation nor one the record has moved past can quietly become the input to the next.

Drafting retries automatically on transient server errors, and the dialog shows a live log of each pass. Interviews have no drafting pass of their own: the record is yours to write and finalise, and drafting reads it as it stands.

Files and safety net

The app opens on a start screen: New investigation…, Open existing investigation…, Reopen last where there is one, and underneath them or explore the worked example investigation. The example is a complete investigation - a rejected take-off and runway overrun at Tarlton Springs - with its map tested, a timeline, interviews, evidence and a drafted report, and it is the quickest way to see what a finished one looks like. Everything in it is invented: the operator, the aerodrome, the aircraft and the people were written for teaching and none of it describes a real occurrence. The documents it produced ship with the app too. With the example open, File, Open exports folder shows the standard report, the preliminary update, the executive summary, the Evidence and Argument Tables and the collation, in Word and PDF, and anything you export from the example lands there beside them.

Start your own with File, New investigation: name it and choose where it lives, and the app creates a folder there holding the investigation file and an exports subfolder that exports and report drafts write into. The whole investigation - map, checklist, timeline, interviews, evidence captures, logbook - lives in the single .sfm file, compatible in both directions with the original application, so the folder can sit beside your other investigation material (OneDrive included).

Save with Ctrl+S; a dot beside the project name marks unsaved changes, and autosave writes every five minutes once the file has a home. If a save ever fails the app says so plainly - a red notice, not silence. Every edit is undoable with Ctrl+Z, deleted objects are recoverable from the recycle bin, and File, Reopen last restores the previous investigation at the start of the next session; in a browser this takes one permission click.

Investigations on a shared folder

A .sfm on OneDrive or a network drive can be open on two machines at once, and every save writes the whole file, so the last writer would otherwise win without either person noticing. Two guards sit under that. The app notes the file's size and modification time each time it reads or writes it, and if the copy on disk has changed since, the save is held and you choose what happens: take theirs and reload, save yours alongside as a copy, or write over theirs. There is no merge, because a whole investigation file cannot be merged safely, so the choice is deliberate and it is yours. Beside the file the app also keeps a <name>.sfm.lock while the investigation is open, refreshed about every minute and removed on close, so whoever opens it next is told you have it. The lock only warns: a synced folder can delay it by minutes, and a stale one from a crash or a sleeping laptop is ignored, so the size-and-time check is the guard that actually holds.

Connecting a model

The investigation itself runs with no model involved. Drafting is the only part of the application that wants one, and how it reaches one is a single setting you control. This section says what that setting does, what leaves your machine when it is switched on, and which kinds of model are suitable for investigation material.

The three levels of AI access

Settings, under AI drafting, carries one choice called AI access, and it decides how every Draft action in the app behaves, from a single factor's analysis paragraph to the whole report.

No AI means no model is involved anywhere. Reports are assembled from the record under the chosen style's headings, the Draft controls are not shown, and nothing about the investigation leaves the machine. This is the level the app starts at, and it is the level it falls back to without a licence.

Chat means the app builds the prompt and copies it out for you. You paste it into a chat tool your organisation has approved for investigation material, not a personal account, and paste the reply back. The prompt asks the model to audit its own draft against the record and correct it before it replies, so there is one paste each way; the reply lands as a draft for you to read and finalise like any other. The application makes no network call of its own at this level; you are the transport, and you see exactly what is being sent because you are the one pasting it.

Endpoint means the app sends the prompt itself, over the network, to the model set up under Settings, Models. This is the only level at which the application sends investigation content anywhere by itself.

Your licence level caps this choice. Free allows No AI only, and Professional and above allow all three. An edition can cap it further, and the stricter of the two applies. A line at the top of the pane says what your licence allows and to when, and a level above it is marked on its own line (Not included in your licence, Available with Professional, or Not available in this edition). Your own choice is kept as you made it: if a licence lapses, the pane says which level is in use for now, and renewing the licence brings the choice back without your setting it again.

What an endpoint is, in this app's terms

An endpoint is a named profile describing one model server; the Models pane lists them. Each one shows where it sends, worked out from its address (this computer, your network, or the internet with the host named), and when it last passed a test. The one marked In use is the one every Draft goes to. Add a model offers the presets grouped by where the data goes: LM Studio or Ollama on this computer, the same on another computer on your network, and your organisation's gateway or the OpenAI or Anthropic public API, which leave your network.

Each model asks for a Name, an Address, an API key where the service needs one, a Drafting model and a Checking model (picked from the list Refresh list fetches, or typed in), and the Factor detail to send, either the full argument or the conclusions only. The Checking model runs the audit and the checks after repair and polish; a smaller, cheaper model is fine there, and with none set the drafting model runs them. Protocol, Auth header, the Anthropic API version and the experimental polish by paragraph sit under Advanced, since a preset fills them. Done checks the model before it is kept: an address that is missing or still holds a placeholder, a public service with no key, or no drafting model is shown beside its field, and a plain http:// address outside your network is flagged.

Test sends a short test prompt ("Reply with OK.") to the drafting model and, where it differs, the checking model, after asking the server for its model list. It carries no investigation content but costs a few tokens on a paid API, which the button says. It reports on the line beside it whether both models answered, or what failed (a refused key, a model that did not answer), and the result is kept with the time, so the AI access pane can show it.

Protocol is the part worth understanding, because it is what decides whether a given server will work at all. OpenAI-compatible posts each call to /chat/completions under the base URL, which is the shape LM Studio, Ollama and most corporate gateways expose. Anthropic Messages API posts to /messages instead and sends a version header with every call. Either way the app asks the server's /models path for the model list, and the key, if there is one, goes in the header you name, with Authorization and a Bearer prefix assumed when you leave it blank. A server that speaks neither of those two shapes needs a gateway in front of it that does.

A key is a credential and is treated as one. It is stored on that machine alone, never written into the .sfm, never included in an export, and removed from any error message the server echoes back, so it cannot travel with an investigation you share or turn up in a screenshot.

What is sent, and where it goes

A drafting run sends the whole collated record: every object with its tests, confidences and reasoning, the timeline, the finalised interviews, the evidence, and the safety reports. People appear by role rather than by name throughout, which is done when an interview or a transcript is brought in rather than at the point of drafting, so the name never reaches the record, the collated export or any model. Where a local model cannot hold all of it, set that endpoint to conclusions only and it is sent the result of each test rather than the whole argument.

It goes to the endpoint you configured and nowhere else. The application has no service of its own, phones nothing home, and sends no telemetry. If you have configured no endpoint and left AI access at No AI, nothing about the investigation leaves the machine at all.

Not a consumer chat service

Investigation material is sensitive, and a consumer chat service such as ChatGPT, Claude.ai or Gemini is the wrong place for it. Those accounts sit outside your organisation's data agreements, the content can be retained or reviewed, and you have no way to say afterwards where it went. The application is not designed to be pointed at one: the endpoint route expects an API you or your organisation controls, not a consumer chat window. Use the alternatives below instead.

A local model on your own machine

LM Studio or Ollama runs a model on the investigator's own computer, and the app reaches it at an address like http://localhost:1234/v1. On data, this is the strongest option available: nothing leaves the computer, so there is no agreement to negotiate and no provider to trust. On cost, there is none beyond the hardware you already have. On quality, it is the weakest of the three, because a model that fits on a laptop is far smaller than a hosted one, holds less of the record at once and writes less well, which is why the conclusions-only setting exists. There is nothing to ask IT for unless the machine is locked down, in which case you need permission to install the runner, and the firewall left alone if the model is to run on a second machine on the same network.

Your organisation's private cloud endpoint

Most organisations that allow model use at all do it this way: AWS Bedrock, Azure OpenAI, Google Vertex AI, or an internal gateway sitting in front of one of them. On data, the material goes to the cloud but stays inside the organisation's own tenancy and its own agreement with the provider, which ordinarily means no training on your content and a stated retention period. On cost, it is metered per call and paid by the organisation, and a whole-report draft is a handful of large calls rather than thousands of small ones. On quality, it is the best of the three, because these are the frontier models and they can hold the entire record. Ask IT for the base URL, whether it speaks the OpenAI-compatible or Anthropic shape, which header the key goes in, the deployment or model names for the drafting and audit roles, a key, and the retention terms in force so you can record them.

Your organisation's own hosted model

Some organisations host an open-weight model on their own servers and expose it as an internal API. On data, this is as contained as a local model while still being a proper service, since the material never leaves the organisation's own infrastructure. On cost, there is no per-call charge, but the organisation carries the hardware and the people to run it, so it only exists where the demand already justified it. On quality, it sits between the other two and depends entirely on which model they chose and how much context it is configured to accept. Ask IT the same questions as for a cloud endpoint, and one more: how much context the deployment accepts, because a record that does not fit is the usual reason drafting fails on an internal server.

When there is no endpoint at all

Chat is for the common case of an investigator who is allowed to use an approved chat tool but has no API to point the app at. The app builds the same prompt it would have sent, plus the instruction to audit and correct its own draft, you move it across by hand, and the reply comes back into the same provenance and finalising as any other draft. What it does not get is the Endpoint tier's separate audit model and automatic checks, so reading it against the record is your job. The same caution applies to which chat tool you paste into: it should be one your organisation has approved for material of this sensitivity, not a personal account.

What the published case studies used

The case studies on the website were drafted with a Claude-based pipeline, reached through the suite's own sealed command-line transport rather than an API key, standing in for the kind of private cloud endpoint an organisation would use in practice. The pipeline, the prompts, the audit and the checks are the ones the application ships.

Analysis

Standard of proof

Required confidence for a finding offers a list of standards in steps of 5 from 50 to 95 per cent, each named by the standard it is and saying which band of the probability scale it opens or sits inside. Different organisations set different standards: transport safety investigators commonly work to likely or probable, which is 70 per cent; some Just Culture frameworks use 60 per cent; the civil balance of probabilities is 50 per cent.

Probability wording

Probability wording holds the band edges and the words a report may use for each term on the probability scale. The ten terms themselves are fixed, so findings stay comparable between investigations; what you can change is how each one may be said. "Almost certainly" means the same band as "extremely likely", "probably" the same band as "likely", and a drafting model may reach for any phrase you list. Each row starts at the wording in force, so you edit the list in front of you rather than retyping it, and clearing a row hands it back to the built-in. A phrase already accepted under another term is refused, since a phrase meaning two bands would make every check that reads it ambiguous. The report checks read every phrase in use back as its term, so a report can read like English without a single claim changing strength. The confidence slider on an object's tests stops at 5 and 95 per cent as well as the round tens, so "very unlikely" and "extremely likely" can be recorded exactly rather than only reached by typing.

The pane is a table, one row per band, with three columns: From, Band and Term. The From figure is the lowest percentage the band covers, and you can change it: the app ships with the ATSB's own table from AR-2007-053, and an organisation working to different numbers edits them here. An edge is shared with the band below, so moving one number moves one boundary and the bands can never gap, overlap or swap order; a number outside what its neighbours allow is pulled back to the nearest value that works. The Band column reads the band back to you and names the slider steps that can record it, since two bands at the bottom of the scale, 1 to 4 per cent and under 1, have no slider step at all. Reset every band puts the standard table back. A moved edge follows through everywhere at once: the word the object form shows beside a slider, the standard of proof list, the findings scaffold and every check that reads a term from a percentage.

The Term column holds the phrases, each with a tick that takes it out of use without losing it, so an organisation that does not want "ruled out" read as a probability can switch it off and switch it back on later; a phrase out of use is offered to no drafting pass and read back by no check. The term's own word has no tick box, because a band with no word for itself leaves a check nothing to read. Type in the field itself to add a phrase or take one off the list altogether.

Each term also has a tick reading May be stated plainly, with no hedge for a statement with no hedge at all. It is on for "virtually certain" only, and should stay that way: where the investigation recorded 100 per cent, writing "the pump was unserviceable" says exactly what the record says, while the same flat sentence against a recorded "likely" claims more than the evidence supports. A drafted paragraph that drops the hedge that way is flagged after the draft lands, alongside the check for a term from the wrong band.

Influence reasons

The headings and reasons the Influence test's Reason panel offers. Each heading opens and closes, showing how many reasons it holds while closed. Each reason is one row with its side (for or against), its label, a line on when it applies and the starting sentence it inserts, all edited in place; move and remove appear when you point at the row. Add reason sits at the foot of each heading and Add heading at the foot of the page. Restore defaults, once the list differs from the built-in one, asks first and then replaces the whole list. Renaming or removing a reason changes only what the panel offers next; a test that already used it keeps its own text. Like most pages, nothing is kept until Save, and Discard puts it back; a blank or repeated reason name holds Save until it is fixed.

Evidence

Categories

The list an evidence item files under, each row with its name, Move up, Move down and Remove. Add a category sits below it with its own box and an Add button, and Reset to built-in appears once the list differs from the built-in one. An item always keeps its category as its own text, so renaming or removing one here only changes what the picker offers for the next item filed, never material already captured, and typing a category that is not on the list still works.

Attached files

Decides where a dropped file goes. An investigation is normally one self-contained file: everything captured goes inside the .sfm, which is why a dropped file is held to 2 MB, or 5 MB for an image. Keep dropped files beside the investigation turns that off: a dropped file is kept in an attachments folder beside the .sfm instead, with the evidence item recording its name, its size and where it went; the limit rises to 100 MB, which takes a set of scanned reports. The trade is that the investigation becomes a folder to send rather than a file, so leave it off if your document system takes only one file. Pasted screenshots and pasted text stay inside the .sfm either way, nothing already captured moves, and the setting lives on your machine rather than in the investigation. Like most pages it waits for Save changes. The investigation has to have been saved before a file can be kept beside it; drop one into an unsaved investigation and you are asked to save it first.

Templates

One page holds the default templates as plain, editable text, with a row of tabs across the top to pick which: Checklist, Interview preparation, Interview opening and Interview background. Each is one editable text box with Reset to built-in above it, and a tab whose text has been changed carries a small dot, as does Templates in the list.

Templates seed new content only. Creating an investigation copies the checklist template into its file; adding an interview copies the preparation, opening, and background templates into that interview. From then on the copy belongs to the investigation - it travels in the .sfm, is edited in place, and is never rewritten by later template changes. Template overrides are stored on the machine, not in the investigation, and Reset to built-in returns any template to the edition's default.

Checklist

Seeds the investigator's checklist in a new investigation. Each uses a simple line format - # Section starts a checklist section, - item adds a question or checkbox, > text adds a guidance note or a statement to read aloud, and two leading spaces nest an item; background fields are one entry per line.

Interview preparation

Seeds the preparation checklist copied into every new interview: the scheduling, conduct and follow-up checks to work through before and around it.

Interview opening

Seeds the checkable protocol at the top of every new interview, including the statements to read aloud before it starts.

Interview background

Seeds the background details recorded for each interviewee, one field label per line.

AI drafting

AI access

One choice decides how every Draft action in the app reaches a model, from a factor's analysis paragraph to the whole report. Where the edition has licensing, a line at the top says what your licence allows and to when, with Licence details, Enter a licence or Start the 90-day trial beside it, each of which opens Licence. A tier your level does not include is shown in full but greyed out, with its level as a tag and Available with Professional under it. Under each of No AI, Chat and Endpoint the pane says how that level changes the work, what leaves this computer and where it goes, and what the licence has to allow, the same account as The three AI access tiers in From record to report, which Open the guide at the foot of the pane opens.

Each level's own settings show under it only where they apply. Under Chat, in every edition: Paste the prompt as One paste or Two pastes: instructions, then record, for a chat tool that drops a long first message, and Warn when a prompt is longer than a number of characters (100,000 unless changed). Under Endpoint: the model in use, where it sends, its last test with Test and Change beside it, and the tick Polish each draft as soon as it lands, off by default, which runs Polish the prose on every section or whole report that lands; you still see each change beside the text it replaces before it is used. Like everything else in Settings, these wait for Save changes.

Models

Where Endpoint drafting sends its prompts. The page is there at every level, so a model can be set up and tested before the licence allows Endpoint; below Endpoint a line at the top says nothing on it is in use. Opening it contacts no server. One card per model shows In use, its name, Sends to (this computer, your network, or the internet with the host, worked out from the address rather than the name), its last test, and Test (sends a short test prompt, no investigation content), Edit and Remove, which asks first. The last model cannot be removed, since the Chat tier's prompts read its settings.

Add a model… offers the presets grouped by where the data goes: Stays on this computer (LM Studio, Ollama), Stays on your network (LM Studio or Ollama on another computer) and Leaves your network (your organisation's gateway, the OpenAI public API, the Anthropic public API), plus Something else to set every field yourself. Edit opens the model's form: Name, Address with its Sends to line, API key with Show and Hide, Drafting model with Refresh list, Checking model (each a list of what the server reports, with Other… (type a model id) for one it does not list), Factor detail as Full argument or Conclusions only, and under Advanced the Protocol, Auth header, Anthropic API version and Polish granularity. Done checks the model and shows what is missing beside its field; Cancel puts it back as it was.

Documents

Finding terms

Finding terms applies at every AI access level, the Record tab's export included, not only to drafting.

Finding terms holds the words a Findings section uses: the three group headings, the safety issue tag, and the lane at which a safety factor becomes a safety issue. The pane opens with the rule those words are attached to, so an organisation can see what it is customising. An object that fails Existence, or passes it and then fails both Influence and Importance, is an excluded hypothesis and is reported under the other-findings heading. One that passes Existence and Influence and increased risk is a contributing safety factor. One that passes Existence, fails Influence and passes Importance is an other factor that increased risk. Everything else - a factor that reduced risk, a neutral event or condition, a factor whose Influence test was examined and left unsettled - goes under other findings. The lane then decides the tag: either kind of safety factor sitting in Risk Controls, or in a lane above it, is also a safety issue. It keeps its own heading, carries the tag, and is listed again in the report's Safety issues section; below that lane it is a safety factor and nothing more. The threshold lane is a setting like the words are, so an organisation using its own lane names can move it. Three layers decide each word, and the nearest one wins. The app has a built-in; a report style can declare its own instead, which most do, so different bodies' formats read as they should; and anything you type here overrides both, for every style on this machine. Leave a field empty to let the style decide it, and Reset hands it back. The word you set is used by the findings scaffold, by the report check that reads a drafted Findings section, and in the instruction a drafting model is given, so the three can never disagree.

Contributing factors heading, Increased-risk factors heading, Other findings heading, Safety issue tag and Safety issues start at this lane are each marked overridden and carry a Reset once you set them.

Word template

Current template shows what is set, or says none is. A file picker takes a .docx or .dotx file - your organisation's report template, or any document with the styles you want - and the app reads its heading and body styles only, so generated documents take its fonts and colours. Each export still keeps a plain cover (title, subtitle, drafted date) and its own page-number footer; the reference document's own header, footer, logo and cover wording are not carried through yet. Clear removes the template and returns to the app's built-in formatting. Your own template is a Professional feature: at Free the page carries the Professional tag, the picker is disabled, and a template already stored is kept but not applied.

To use your organisation's template:

  1. Open Settings, then Word template under Documents, and choose your .docx or .dotx file. The pane says Loaded styles from the file's name, and Current template shows it.
  2. Export or draft as usual. The template takes effect on the next export; documents already written are not changed.
  3. Open the result and, if Word asks to update fields, say Yes, so the contents page fills in.

Only the template's styles are read. The ones that matter are Heading 1, Heading 2 and Heading 3 (every heading; the cover title, cover subtitle and the Contents title take their look from Heading 1 and 2 through styles the app adds, Cover Title, Cover Subtitle and TOC Heading, unless your template defines TOC Heading itself, so they never appear in the contents), the contents styles TOC 1 and TOC 2 (the contents page), Caption (figure captions), List Paragraph (bulleted lists) and the document defaults in Normal (the body font and spacing). A style the template does not define is not an error: Word falls back to its own plain version of it, so a template with no Heading 1 gives light blue Calibri headings and an unstyled contents page. Define each style once, because a template that defines the same style twice may be read either way. The template's own cover page, header, footer and logo are left out, and each export prints its own plain cover and its Page X of Y footer instead. An investigation marked as a fictional case still carries its label in the header and the footer.

Abbreviations

Abbreviations holds your organisation's glossary. Paste it straight in, one term per line, in the box under One per line, short form first. A term on the list may be written out at first use in a drafted report, using exactly the meaning you gave it; anything not on the list is reproduced as the source wrote it and never guessed at, so an unfamiliar code stays as the investigator recorded it rather than becoming a confident mistake. Every drafted report ends with an abbreviations table covering the terms it actually used, built by matching the finished text rather than asked of the model, so it cannot list a term the document never used or invent a meaning. Codes the glossary does not cover are named at the end of the grounding audit, for you to add or write out. Reset to edition list, Import…, Merge… and Export… sit below the box, for sharing the glossary as a file between machines or starting again from the edition's own list.

Fictional label

The words a fictional case's exports carry in their header and footer, for an investigation marked with the Fictional case tick on the Record tab. The built-in label reads "Fictional case study for training. Not a real investigation."; change it in the Label box, or put it back with Reset to the built-in label. Start new investigations as fictional cases is for an installation used for teaching: with it ticked, File, New investigation starts every investigation already marked, and the mark can still be taken off on the Record tab. Both settings belong to this machine, and neither changes an investigation that already exists.

Exporting

Export in the header writes a timestamped folder containing whichever outputs you tick, plus a snapshot of the .sfm file for the record. When the investigation was created with File, New investigation, exports land in its exports subfolder automatically; otherwise the app asks where (or downloads the files in a browser). The outputs come in three groups:

  • Word documents - the investigation report and a summary with the map image embedded, ready for distribution or further editing. The Evidence and Argument Tables are also available as a Word document: every object's test results and supporting text, grouped by lane, one test at a time down the page, after a page on how to read the tables. Your own notes on an object stay on the map and in the object form, and are not printed in the tables.
  • Markdown and text - the same report content as plain files, and the report-drafting collation: everything the investigation holds (tested map, evidence, notes, interviews, captured material) structured for a local LLM to draft the report from.
  • Image - the full map as a PNG, rendered with every object in frame regardless of the current zoom, and the timeline as arranged on its tab, with entity colours and legend.

The Export dialog

Every output is a tick, and nothing is written until you press the button at the foot. The ticks are remembered, so the second export of an investigation asks nothing the first one did not.

Under Word documents sit Investigation report (.docx), Summary with map (.docx) and Evidence and Argument Tables (.docx). Under Markdown and text sit Investigation report (.md), Investigation report (.txt), Evidence and Argument Tables (.md) and Report-drafting collation (.md). Under Image sit Investigation map image (.png) and Timeline image (.png). Each tick carries a line saying what that file holds.

Beside the title, a count of the objects that are not finalised yet, so an export sent early is a decision rather than an oversight. Export selected writes the lot and reads Exporting… while it works, then names the folder it wrote to. Close leaves without writing anything.

Binary supporting material - object attachments, timeline captures, evidence files - is unpacked into an attachments subfolder of the export, so every file reference in the reports resolves to a real file beside them. Exports warn when objects are not finalised, and every report marks untested material clearly, so a draft sent early cannot be mistaken for a finding. Report drafting with a local LLM is covered in its own section above.

A drafted report comes out as a document meant to be read on paper: a cover page, a contents page after it, and every major section starting on a new page. Word builds the contents itself from the headings, which is why it asks once, as the document opens, whether to update the fields in it. Say yes and the entries and page numbers fill in. That question is Word doing its job, not a fault in the file. Word words it as "This document contains fields that may refer to other files", but it is only filling in the contents and the figure numbers, and Yes is the answer. The outline export has no contents page and does not ask.

An investigation marked as a fictional case (the Fictional case tick on the Record tab) says so on every page it exports. Each Word document, report, summary, Evidence and Argument Tables and every appendix alike, carries the fictional-case label as a centred line of its own in light teal, in the header and again in the footer, under the built-in look or a Word template set in Settings. The markdown and text exports open with it, the report-drafting collation carries it on its title line, the map and timeline images show it under the render date in their title bar, and the slide deck prints it across the foot of each slide.

The example reports and PDFs that ship with the app came out of this same path, run against the worked example investigation, so what you see there is what the app writes rather than something tidied up by hand afterwards.

Licence and levels

A licence decides a level, and the level decides which features are available. Licensing controls features, never your access to your own investigation data.

  • Free conducts and structures an investigation: the map, evidence, the timeline, the checklist, interviews, the log and the to-dos, and exports of the Evidence and Argument Tables, the map and timeline images, the report-drafting collation and the project snapshot.
  • Professional completes it: AI drafting (an endpoint you set up), Chat AI (copying a prompt out and pasting the reply back), finished Word reports and slides, and your own Word template. The AI is yours to choose; Professional allows every AI access setting you configure.
  • Organisation is Professional adapted to an organisation's own framework, terminology and reporting.

With no licence, this Generic edition build is at the Free level, and what it exports goes out clean, with no watermark. A control that needs Professional stays in view, disabled, with the line "Available with Professional." beside it.

Nothing is ever locked. When a licence lapses or a trial ends, the app drops to Free and only the Professional actions wait: text drafted while you were licensed stays in the file, readable and editable, and goes out in the project snapshot and the collation as before. Nothing is deleted or hidden, and the app writes nothing to your investigation because the level changed. A stored Word template is kept, unapplied, until a licence allows it again.

A free 90-day Professional trial is offered the first time the app runs, once the build can reach the licence server, on one quiet line under the header; Not now puts it away, and Settings, Licence offers it again. Give a name and email and the app asks the licence server for a signed trial licence for this email and this installation, once. Only the name, the email and a random number identifying the installation are sent; no investigation data. Offline, keep working in Free and start the trial later. During the trial the header chip reads "Trial" with the days left.

A licence arrives as a block of text beginning IWS1.. Open Settings, choose Licence, paste the whole thing into the box (line breaks and all, they do no harm) and press Apply. The pane then shows the level in force, who the licence names, and when it runs out. It is checked on this machine against a key built into the app; nothing is sent anywhere, and it works with no network at all. If a paste is refused, the pane says which of the problems it is: not a licence, not issued for this application, issued for a different edition, or expired. The app header carries a chip reading Free, Professional, Organisation or the trial's days left; hover it for who the licence names and its expiry date, and click it to open this pane.

A paid licence keeps working for 14 days after its expiry date, so a renewal that arrives late by email does not interrupt an investigation; the pane says when one is in that period. A trial has no such period. One thing to know about the clock: if the machine's date is set earlier than the last date the app saw, the licence is treated as expired until the date is put right.

Getting the app

On your own machine, use the installed desktop application. On a machine without administrator rights (a corporate laptop), try these in order:

  1. Standalone exe. Copy investigation-workflow-suite.exe anywhere and run it - nothing is installed. It needs only the WebView2 runtime, standard on Windows 10 and 11. Full native file dialogs and in-place saving.
  2. Per-user installer. The ...x64-setup.exe installer puts the app in your user profile with no admin prompt and adds a Start-menu entry.
  3. Portable bundle. Copy the portable folder to the machine and double-click Start IWS.bat. Python's built-in web server hosts the app and your browser opens it - nothing is installed and the bundle contains no executable, so application-control policies have nothing to block. Open and Save work on real .sfm files in Edge or Chrome; exports arrive as downloads.
The exe options are unsigned, so expect a SmartScreen warning ("More info", then "Run anyway") where policy allows them at all. The portable bundle works wherever Python exists - the same prerequisite the original Tkinter application relied on.
