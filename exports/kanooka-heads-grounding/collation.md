Free recall:
Took the vessel over at about 13:05 at the town wharf. The outgoing master told him the plotter had frozen twice that morning and come good on its own both times. He replied that it had done the same to him the month before. Neither of them wrote it down.

The afternoon was normal, two round trips. TF18 loaded 38 at Muntari and sailed on time at 17:35. Clear, light westerly, no sea in the entrance. He described the conditions as the best you could ask for.

Picked up the No. 1 beacon and went into the channel on the line. Happy with his position at No. 2. About ninety seconds later the plotter froze: picture on the screen, vessel symbol stationary, no response to touch. Tried it twice.

Went looking for the No. 4 beacon out of the window, because that is the transit and he has done it four thousand times. Could not find it. Opened the wheelhouse window in case it was the glass. Still could not find it.

When he did pick it up it was dead ahead instead of on the starboard bow, and he knew immediately he was on the bank side. The alarm went about a minute later. Both throttles astern straight away; she went on anyway.

Stopped the engines and did not touch them again. Called Port Control, got lifejackets out, got the void sounded, waited for the workboat.

Additional questions:
What exactly was said about the plotter at the handover: it froze twice on the morning runs and came back by itself both times, probably the heat in the wheelhouse. Neither man was worried about it.

Why it was not entered in the defect book: 'it's not a defect in your head, is it. It's a thing that happened and stopped happening.' The book is a foot from where he was standing and he says the entry would have taken eleven seconds. He raised this himself before being asked.

Whether that is normal practice: yes, and he says everyone does it and nobody has ever told them not to. If it is broken you write it, if it came good you say it.

Whether he considered stopping when the plotter froze: no, and he called that the honest answer and not a good one. He went straight to the visual transit because in his mind the visual transit is the backup. 'There's nothing that tells you to stop. There's nothing that tells you anything. We haven't got a night plan for that channel.'

The ninety seconds looking for the beacon: 'you're looking into a wall of light', the town plus the new car park floodlights, all one glow from out there. A 0.4 second green flash in front of that is 'a nothing'. It used to be a mains light.

What he did without deciding to: eased the vessel to starboard five degrees while searching. 'I never made that decision.'

What he has done about the beacon over two years: told other masters, told the tearoom, told the workboat crew once. Never wrote it down, never told the office, never told the port. 'There's nowhere to put it. There's no form for the beacon looks dim. It's not our beacon. And I don't know who'd read it.'

What came with the timetable change on 01/06: a new roster sheet, and nothing else. No brief, no plan, no conversation.

The twenty six previous darkness transits: all fine, which he says is the part that bothers him. 'It just hadn't bitten anybody yet.'

His duty state: tired, six and a quarter hours the night before, normal for a split. Not fighting to stay awake and not slow. Asked that the roster not carry the finding.

The decision not to refloat: falling tide, unknown bottom, 38 people aboard, nine seconds of grinding. 'On the bank she can't sink.'

Follow-up questions:
Nothing outstanding with this witness. He was asked at the end what the investigation should look at and answered 'the beacon and the night plan, in that order', and that his own part he would wear and had already written down.

To put to the operator rather than to him: whether any master on the route has ever been given a night passage plan for any channel, and whether the company reporting system's 'navigation hazard' category has ever been used on this route. Checked 20/07: it exists, it has not been used on this route in three years, and no master interviewed knew it was there.

TF18 Muntari to Kanooka Heads, 7 July, last service. Thirty eight passengers

beacon now with the new car park up, and a two and a half second green flash

nowhere near where I had it in my head, and I understood straight away I was
well to the west. I had already put her over five degrees looking for it
without really deciding to.

and she hit anyway. I stopped the engines. I did not try to back her off. The
tide was falling and I did not know what I had under me, and I would rather
have a boat sitting on a bank with everyone on it than a boat in deep water
with a hole in her. I got jackets on everyone, got the void sounded, told
Port Control, and the workboat had them off inside an hour.

Two things I want on the record.

book, and I did not stop when it went. Nobody made me do either of those
things.

The second is not mine. That beacon has been getting harder to see for two
years and every one of us has said so to each other and none of us has ever
written it down anywhere, because there is nowhere to write it down. We do
not have a night plan for that channel. We were never given one. The
timetable moved into the dark in June and nothing else about the run changed
at all. I would like somebody to look at that, because the next bloke will be
me on a worse night.
