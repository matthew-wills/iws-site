1 The occurrence as first notified

A rejected take-off at night, and an aircraft off the end of the runway

This is what was known on the evening of 18/06/2026 and the morning after, before anybody had read a recorder or opened a manual. It is the brief the investigation started from.

SB512 was the last scheduled sector of the day out of Tarlton Springs, a certified regional aerodrome in the remote far west, about 380 nm inland of the operator's home base at Port Carrick. The crew had picked the evening rotation up that morning from a crew who reported unfit, and they left on schedule. The aerodrome was in full darkness, with no town lights anywhere around it. Tarlton Springs has no control tower: crews make their own broadcasts on the common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF) and switch the runway lights on themselves through the pilot-activated lighting.

The take-off roll on runway 09 was normal to V1. Two seconds after the V1 call the first officer saw two large kangaroos already on the sealed surface ahead, crossing from the northern side, and the captain rejected the take-off at 118 kt. About a second later the aircraft struck both animals. The No. 2 propeller was damaged and its reverse did not respond for the rest of the stop. The aircraft left the end of runway 09 at about 21 kt and came to rest 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

There was no fire and no evacuation. The 44 passengers walked off by the forward airstair a few minutes later. One cabin crew member later reported bruising, and the aircraft was substantially damaged.

The captain's own report, submitted the next morning under the company's just culture scheme, put two things on the record before anyone asked. He had stopped above V1, and everything he had ever been trained on for a stop or go decision had been an engine problem below V1. And the kangaroos at Tarlton Springs were no surprise to anyone who went there.

2 What came in first

The first material, filed before anyone had a theory

In the days after the occurrence the notification, the captain's report and the first records arrive. They go into the file as they are, and the checklist starts the work that follows from them.

The first material

Take the safety report in as it was written

ASR-2026-1184 was submitted by the pilot in command at 02:40 UTC the next morning. It goes in as written, with the submitter recorded by role, the time, and the reference in the reporting system. No name goes into the file.

One tick says the report came in under the company's just culture scheme. From then on report drafting is told the account may inform the investigation and must not be quoted into a distributed document, and the audit, the application's model check against the record, looks for any place it was.

File the first evidence items

The notification, the flight data readout, the CTAF and CVR extract and the weather go into Captured material, each with a caption, its significance, a category and a reference. The recorders came off the aircraft the same day, so the readout arrived early and settled the speeds before anyone argued about them.

Each item is linked to whatever rests on it. When a source later turns out to be unreliable, what has to be revisited is already listed.

What the app recordedThe material itself inside the investigation file, why each item matters, and every object, interview or finding that depends on it.

3 The evidence pack

Nine documents, and what each one settled

By the end of the gathering phase the investigation held nine source documents. They are published here as one pack, so everything below can be read against the same material the investigator had.

Occurrence notification

The first account: what happened, the damage, the injuries, the risk rating and the actions taken that night. It opened the file and seeded the first timeline events.

Flight data summary

The recorder readout. It fixed V1 at 112 kt and the reject at 118 kt, timed the strike a second later, and showed the deceleration achieved falling short of the scheduled accelerate-stop performance. It also closed the engine failure hypothesis: both engines were making power right up to the reject.

CTAF recording and CVR extract

The crew's own broadcasts on the CTAF, the words in the cockpit across the roll, the reject and the stop, and the call to the aerodrome reporting officer afterwards. It is the evidence behind the reject decision, and behind the fact that no runway inspection was ever asked for.

Weather, light and moon

The observations and forecast, the end of civil twilight and the state of the moon. It established the roll five minutes after the end of civil twilight with no moon, and it excluded the tailwind hypothesis: every source gives a headwind.

Air safety report, pilot in command

The captain's own account, submitted under the just culture scheme. It is the origin of two lines of enquiry, the training gap behind the reject and the aerodrome's wildlife history, and it is protected, so it informs the investigation without being quoted into anything distributed.

Operator procedures and policy extracts

The aerodrome standard, the aerodrome manual, the operations manual wildlife and rejected take-off sections, the route risk assessment and the change record. Everything in the external, organisational and risk control lanes rests on these.

Maintenance, training and aerodrome records

The aircraft history and the post-occurrence inspection, the crew's duty and training records, the evening rotation's roster pattern, the recurrent training syllabus, and the aerodrome operator's works register and sighting log. The deferred fence, the training gap and the 41 recorded sightings come from here.

Interview transcript, pilot in command

The captain's account in his own words, taken once the records were in hand. It is what turned the training question from a complaint into a factor that could be tested.

Interview transcript, first officer

The first officer was the pilot flying and the one who saw the animals. Her account is the evidence on detection, and on what the runway lighting looked like from the seat.

4 Interviews, and 5 the timeline

The two accounts, and the sequence they have to fit

Both flight crew were interviewed once the records were in hand, so the questions could be about what the records did not say. The sequence of events is then built from everything at once.

The interviews

Import the transcripts, and settle the accounts

Two interviews, each on its own sub-tab: the pilot in command and the first officer. Both were conducted over Teams and both transcripts came in as the .vtt download, imported straight into the record. A .docx download, or a pasted transcript, import the same way.

De-identification is built into the import. Every speaker in a transcript is mapped to a role, and it is the role, never the name, that reaches the record, the collated export or any drafting model. On this file the speakers are the lead investigator, the second investigator, the pilot in command and the first officer, and that is all anyone downstream ever sees.

The investigator corrects what the transcription got wrong, writes notes beside it, and finalises. From then on the account is settled: the transcript and the notes lock to read-only, and drafting treats the account as evidence rather than as a draft.

What the app recordedThe corrected transcript, the role for every speaker, the investigator's notes beside it, and whether the account has been finalised.

The timeline

Build the sequence, and say what each time rests on

The timeline runs from the crew signing on that morning through to the passengers standing on the ground, and it mixes sources: the recorder gives the roll to the second, the CTAF recording gives the crew's broadcasts and the call to the aerodrome reporting officer, the duty records give the shape of the day, and the aerodrome's own log gives the closure.

Each event carries its source and its time basis, so the sequence can be defended when two records disagree about when something happened. Here they did not: the CTAF recorder's clock agreed with the cockpit voice recorder's to within a second, and that is recorded too.

What the app recordedThe ordered events, the evidence behind each one, the time basis it rests on, and which event is the critical one.

6 Building the map

The analysis, built one stage at a time

The map is the analysis. It starts with the occurrence itself, fills with everything that might have played a part, and is then cut down by testing rather than by opinion. These are three real states of this investigation's own file.

Three states of the same map

Place the critical event, and nothing else

One object goes down first: Runway overrun onto the runway end safety area, in the Occurrence Events lane at the bottom right, with what happened and the proof of its significance recorded against it.

Everything else on the map exists to explain this one object. Putting it down first is what stops the analysis drifting into everything that was ever wrong with the operation.

What the app recordedThe critical event as an object, in its lane, at its point in time.

Put every hypothesis up, including the ones you expect to fail

Nineteen more objects go up, scattered roughly where they belong in lane and in time. The occurrence sequence: two kangaroos crossed the strip onto the sealed runway, the No. 2 propeller and right main gear struck two kangaroos, the deceleration fell below the scheduled accelerate-stop performance. The crew's actions: no runway inspection requested before departing after last light, the take-off rejected 6 kt above V1, maximum braking established within 2 seconds of the reject decision.

The conditions the crew worked in: the roll in darkness, 5 minutes after last light with no moon, the departure inside the aerodrome's logged kangaroo peak, four northern edge lights above nominal intensity, and a crew who might have been fatigued on the fifth sector, 11 hours into their duty. Above them the controls and the organisation: the inspection after last light required only where a wildlife strike was reported in the previous 24 months, no training or guidance for a runway obstruction during the take-off roll, floor escape path lighting checked only every 24 months and not pre-flight, the failed northern fence left unrepaired through three budget rounds, the evening service approved without reopening the daylight-only risk assessment, the evening rotation rostered to close five-sector duties within 45 minutes of the limit, and the standard's size threshold that left the aerodrome with no documented wildlife plan.

Two of them are up there to be disproved: No. 2 engine power loss before the reject decision and Tailwind component during the take-off roll and stop. Nothing is filtered at this point. The method works by testing, not by intuition, and a hypothesis that is later excluded still strengthens the record.

What the app recordedEach hypothesis as an untested object, drawn with a dashed white border until someone has tested it.

Test as the evidence arrives, and let the arrows follow

Nine objects have been tested here. The occurrence sequence is joined up, animals to strike to deceleration shortfall to the overrun, and the wildlife chain has started to form above it, from the standard's size threshold through the failed fence to the animals. The inspection criterion has passed its tests too, but its arrow waits on the inspection nobody requested, which is still untested. Both hypotheses raised to be disproved have failed Existence and are struck through on the map, with the reason on the record.

An investigation does not arrive in order. A transcript changes what a factor means, a record closes a line of enquiry, a new hypothesis turns up in week three. Objects are moved, tested, connected and reconnected as that happens, and the map is never more than the current state of the evidence.

Every arrow is a claim that one thing led to another. It stays grey and dashed until the object it comes from has passed its tests, and turns green when it has, so the picture builds itself as the work is done.

What the app recordedEvery object and every connection, with the state of each test behind them.

7 Testing a factor

Nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests

Three questions are asked of every object on the map, in order, and a confidence is recorded for each answer. The test results determine how the object is classified, while the investigator remains responsible for the judgements behind those results.

Existence

Did this event or condition actually exist?

If it did, the border turns solid and the next question is asked. If it did not, the hypothesis is excluded: it stays on the map, struck through, with the reason recorded.

PassFailInconclusive
Influence

Did it influence anything else, and if so in what way?

If it did, it is a contributing safety factor and its outgoing arrows become proven causal paths. Where the evidence cuts both ways, the test is argued with evidence for and against.

PassFailArgued
Importance

If it did not influence anything, is it still important enough to raise in the report?

A factor that played no part in this occurrence may still be a risk worth addressing. If so, it is a finding in its own right, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows.

PassFail

The three questions, on one object

Ask them of the operator's own defence

The object is Inspection after last light required only where a wildlife strike was reported in 24 months. Part A 8.14.2 requires the pilot in command to request a runway inspection before the first departure after last light at any aerodrome in Appendix 8A, and 8.14.3 requires nothing at all anywhere else. An aerodrome is listed only where a wildlife strike involving a company aircraft has been reported there in the preceding 24 months. Tarlton Springs never has been on it.

Did it exist? Yes, at 100 per cent: the manual text and the appendix listing are documentary. Did it influence the occurrence? Yes, at 90 per cent. Had the requirement applied, an inspection would have been requested and a pass required before the roll, and the aerodrome's own staff disperse animals when they find them. The missing 10 per cent allows that an inspection finished some minutes before departure does not guarantee a clear runway at the roll.

The corner dot says how far the object's own investigation has got: amber while it is still being worked, green when the investigator has finished with it. Eighteen of the twenty objects on this map are green, and two were deliberately left amber.

What the app recordedEach verdict with its confidence and its evidence, the investigator's reasoning, and the standard of proof the investigation set. The form warns when a pass falls short of it.

Argue the one the evidence cuts both ways on

The object at the top of the same chain is Standard's threshold left the aerodrome with no documented wildlife plan. The aerodrome is not in breach of anything, which is the point of putting it in the External Influences lane: its heaviest scheduled aeroplane and its passenger numbers both fall under the standard's limbs, so it needs no documented plan, and it held none.

For: with no plan, nothing owned the aerodrome's wildlife hazard as a hazard. The failed fence sat in the works register as an ordinary maintenance item competing for council money, deferred three times with no hazard assessment behind it. Under a documented plan it is a recorded wildlife control whose failure has to be answered for. Against: the aerodrome operator was still obliged to take reasonable measures, a plan is not the only way to repair a fence or pass a sighting log to an airline, and the deferrals were budget decisions. A plan is paper; the fence is money.

Influence was accepted at 70 per cent, argued. The investigation's standard of proof is the application's default of 70 per cent, so this meets it exactly and no more, and it is the weakest of the influence findings on the map. The report carries that as its confidence word rather than presenting the finding as settled.

What the app recordedBoth sides of the argument as separate evidence, the confidence the investigator settled on after weighing them, and the reasoning for that number.

8 The finished map

The whole investigation in one image

Twenty objects and fifteen arrows. Left to right is time, bottom to top is organisational depth, and the critical event sits at the bottom right where every chain arrives.

What the map says

The chains converge on the animals on the runway and on the reject above V1. The wildlife chain runs from the standard's threshold through the failed fence to the animals, joined by the inspection criterion through the inspection nobody requested, and by the logged kangaroo peak. A change-management chain runs from the evening service's approval to the roll in darkness, and the darkness and the missing obstruction training both lead to the reject. The overrun is then reached by two routes: the strike through the deceleration shortfall, and the late reject directly, because a late reject shortens the runway left rather than lowering the deceleration. A green arrow also runs from the positive event, maximum braking established within 2 seconds of the reject decision, because that changed the outcome as well.

The two orange objects carry no arrows at all. They failed Influence, so there is no proven causal path to draw and an arrow would be a claim the evidence does not support. They stay on the map because they are findings in their own right. The fatigue object carries none either: its Existence was argued and left inconclusive at 50 per cent, so there is nothing established to draw an arrow from, and it stays on the map as tested and set aside.

The one unproven arrow

One arrow is grey and dashed: the claim that four northern edge lights above nominal intensity delayed the crew's detection of the animals, and so the reject. A photometric check seven days later measured them at 118 to 131 per cent of nominal in the section the animals crossed. The lighting is pilot-activated at one fixed setting that nobody changed in between, so Existence passed at 80 per cent. The captain described a corridor of light with everything outside it black; the first officer, who saw the animals, called the lighting normal and saw them clearly once they were on the seal. That shows a deviation. It does not show an effect on detection.

Influence was argued and left inconclusive at 45 per cent, so the arrow cannot be drawn as proven. The application draws it grey and dashed rather than dropping it, because an open line of enquiry is part of the record and the report has to say that it is open.

  • Blue, solid border: a contributing safety factor. It existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange: an other risk factor. It existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through, dashed: an excluded hypothesis. It did not exist, and the reason is on the record.
  • Green, rounded: a positive event, something that reduced the risk or limited the outcome.
  • Red, rounded: the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White, dashed: not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • Green arrow: a proven causal path. The object it comes from has passed Influence.
  • Grey dashed arrow: a causal path still under testing.
  • Green dot: the object's investigation is complete and its evidence finalised.
  • Amber dot: still being investigated.

10 Drafting, audit and repair

The report is written from the record, then argued with

Drafting is not the end of the work. Every draft is audited against the record that produced it, and what the audit finds is acted on before anything is finalised.

The pipeline

Draft

A style is chosen and its sections are drafted from one collated copy of the whole record: an outline pass, a call per section, then a cohesion edit across the lot. The model drafts from the investigation record under instructions not to go beyond what the tests support, with the findings, the categories and the confidence words taken from that record. The checks that follow compare the draft back against the record before the investigator reviews it. Each section is corrected and finalised by hand, and nothing regenerates over a finalised one. This investigation was drafted through a model reached from the investigator's own machine; the endpoint is a setting, so the same pipeline runs against a local model, a hosted API with your own key, or an organisation's gateway. With AI access off the tab says so and the report is assembled from the record instead. The drafts published here were produced by a Claude-based pipeline run through the suite's own sealed command-line transport, standing in for the kind of private cloud endpoint an organisation would use.

What the app recordedEach section with the model and the version of the record that produced it, so a draft goes visibly stale when the record moves on.

Audit, repair, then finalise

The audit, a second model pass, then reads the finished draft back against the whole record and gives a verdict on the claims it identifies, naming what it could not find and what it found contradicted. It is the application's own model check rather than an independent review, and like any model output it can miss things. A mechanical check with no model in it runs beside it, comparing each factor's category and confidence word in the text against its recorded test, and the report's findings against the derived classification. The repair pass takes only what those two flagged: it sends the section carrying a flagged item, with the record and the cited excerpt, under one rule, change what is flagged and nothing else, then re-audits the reply and keeps it only if the re-audit is clean or strictly better. Nothing lands on its own. A repaired section is shown beside the text it would replace and landed by hand, and it carries "repaired against the audit" in its provenance. The audit and the check are published with the reports below, because what the audit caught is the reason a draft is never final until an investigator has read it.

What the app recordedThe audit and the record check beside the draft, the review notes the model made about the record itself, which section each repair touched, and the investigator's decision to finalise.
