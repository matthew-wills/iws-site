1 The occurrence as first notified

A rejected take-off at night, and an aircraft off the end of the runway

This is what was known on the evening of 18/06/2026 and the morning after, before anybody had read a recorder or opened a manual. It is the brief the investigation started from.

SB512 was the last scheduled sector of the day out of Tarlton Springs, a certified regional aerodrome in far western New South Wales. The crew had picked the sector up when another crew went sick, and the doors closed about 40 minutes behind schedule. The aerodrome was in full darkness, with no town lights anywhere around it.

The take-off roll on runway 09 was normal to V1. Immediately after the V1 call the first officer saw two large kangaroos already on the sealed surface ahead, crossing from the northern side, and the captain rejected the take-off at 118 kt. About a second later the aircraft struck both animals. The No. 2 propeller was damaged and its reverse did not respond for the rest of the stop. The aircraft left the end of runway 09 at about 21 kt and came to rest 62 m into the 90 m runway end safety area.

There was no fire and no evacuation. The 44 passengers walked off by the forward airstair a few minutes later. One cabin crew member later reported bruising, and the aircraft was substantially damaged.

The captain's own report, submitted the next morning under the company's just culture scheme, put two things on the record before anyone asked. He had stopped above V1, and everything he had ever been trained on for a stop or go decision had been an engine problem below V1. And the kangaroos at Tarlton Springs were no surprise to anyone who went there.

2 What came in first

The first material, filed before anyone had a theory

In the days after the occurrence the notification, the captain's report and the first records arrive. They go into the file as they are, and the checklist starts the work that follows from them.

The first material

Take the safety report in as it was written

ASR-2026-1184 was submitted by the pilot in command at 02:40 UTC the next morning. It goes in as written, with the submitter recorded by role, the time, and the reference in the reporting system. No name goes into the file.

One tick says the report came in under the company's just culture scheme. From then on report drafting is told the account may inform the investigation and must not be quoted into a distributed document, and the audit checks that it was not.

What the app recordedThe account verbatim, its reference and time, the submitter's role, and the protection it was given.

File the first evidence items

The notification, the flight data readout, the tower and CVR extract and the weather go into Captured material, each with a caption, its significance, a category and a reference. The recorders came off the aircraft the same day, so the readout arrived early and settled the speeds before anyone argued about them.

Each item is linked to whatever rests on it. When a source later turns out to be unreliable, what has to be revisited is already listed.

What the app recordedThe material itself inside the investigation file, why each item matters, and every object, interview or finding that depends on it.

Open the checklist, and raise the first to-dos

File > New investigation creates a folder with the investigation file inside it. The first checklist section covers notification, triage and the terms of reference, and the list runs on through evidence gathering, interviews, analysis and reporting. Each item is answered yes, no or not applicable, and the tab shows how much is still open.

The first to-dos came straight off it: interview both flight crew, get the aerodrome operator's works register and sighting log, get the operations manual wildlife section, get the crew's training records. A job raised inside an object's form lands on the same list with a link back to that object, so there is one list to read and one list to hand to a colleague.

3 The evidence pack

Nine documents, and what each one settled

By the end of the gathering phase the investigation held nine source documents. They are published here as one pack, so everything below can be read against the same material the investigator had.

Occurrence notification

The first account: what happened, the damage, the injuries, the risk rating and the actions taken that night. It opened the file and seeded the first timeline events.

Flight data summary

The recorder readout. It fixed V1 at 112 kt and the reject at 118 kt, timed the strike a second later, and showed the deceleration achieved falling short of the scheduled accelerate-stop performance. It also closed the engine failure hypothesis: both engines were making power right up to the strike.

Tower audio and CVR extract

The words in the cockpit across the roll, the reject and the stop, and the exchange with the tower afterwards. It is the evidence behind the reject decision, and behind the fact that no runway inspection was ever asked for.

Weather, light and moon

The observations and forecast, the end of civil twilight and the state of the moon. It established the departure in the hour after last light, and it excluded the tailwind hypothesis: every source gives a headwind.

Air safety report, pilot in command

The captain's own account, submitted under the just culture scheme. It is the origin of two lines of enquiry, the training gap behind the reject and the aerodrome's wildlife history, and it is protected, so it informs the investigation without being quoted into anything distributed.

Operator procedures and policy extracts

The aerodrome standard, the aerodrome manual, the operations manual wildlife and rejected take-off sections, the route risk assessment and the change record. Everything in the external, organisational and risk control lanes rests on these.

Maintenance, training and aerodrome records

The aircraft history and the post-occurrence inspection, the crew's duty and training records, the recurrent training syllabus, and the aerodrome operator's works register and sighting log. The deferred fence, the training gap and the 41 recorded sightings come from here.

Interview transcript, pilot in command

The captain's account in his own words, taken once the records were in hand. It is what turned the training question from a complaint into a factor that could be tested.

Interview transcript, first officer

The first officer was the pilot flying and the one who saw the animals. Her account is the evidence on detection, and on what the runway lighting looked like from the seat.

4 Interviews, and 5 the timeline

The two accounts, and the sequence they have to fit

Both flight crew were interviewed once the records were in hand, so the questions could be about what the records did not say. The sequence of events is then built from everything at once.

The interviews

Import the transcripts, and settle the accounts

Two interviews, each on its own sub-tab: the pilot in command and the first officer. Both were conducted over Teams and both transcripts came in as the .vtt download, imported straight into the record. A .docx download, or a pasted transcript, import the same way.

De-identification is built into the import. Every speaker in a transcript is mapped to a role, and it is the role, never the name, that reaches the record, the collated export or any drafting model. On this file the speakers are lead investigator, captain and first officer, and that is all anyone downstream ever sees.

The investigator corrects what the transcription got wrong, writes notes beside it, and finalises. From then on the account is settled: the transcript and the notes lock to read-only, and drafting treats the account as evidence rather than as a draft.

What the app recordedThe corrected transcript, the role for every speaker, the investigator's notes beside it, and whether the account has been finalised.

The timeline

Build the sequence, and say what each time rests on

The timeline runs from the crew signing on that morning through to the passengers standing on the ground, and it mixes sources: the recorder gives the roll to the second, the tower log gives the calls, the duty records give the shape of the day, and the aerodrome's own log gives the closure.

Each event carries its source and its time basis, so the sequence can be defended when two records disagree about when something happened. Here they did not: the recorder and the tower log agreed to within a second at the take-off clearance, and that is recorded too.

What the app recordedThe ordered events, the evidence behind each one, the time basis it rests on, and which event is the critical one.

6 Building the map

The analysis, built one stage at a time

The map is the analysis. It starts with the occurrence itself, fills with everything that might have played a part, and is then cut down by testing rather than by opinion. These are three real states of this investigation's own file.

Three states of the same map

Place the critical event, and nothing else

One object goes down first: Runway overrun onto the runway end safety area, in the Occurrence Events lane at the bottom right, with what happened and the proof of its significance recorded against it.

Everything else on the map exists to explain this one object. Putting it down first is what stops the analysis drifting into everything that was ever wrong with the operation.

What the app recordedThe critical event as an object, in its lane, at its point in time.

Put every hypothesis up, including the ones you expect to fail

Seventeen more objects go up, scattered roughly where they belong in lane and in time. The occurrence sequence: two kangaroos entered the runway strip and moved onto the sealed surface, the No. 2 propeller and right main gear struck two kangaroos, the deceleration achieved fell below the scheduled accelerate-stop performance. The crew's actions: no runway inspection requested before the first departure after last light, the take-off rejected 6 kt above V1, maximum braking and No. 1 reverse applied without delay.

The conditions the crew worked in: the departure in the hour after last light, the fifth sector 11 hours into duty and 40 minutes behind schedule, and the runway edge lighting intensity. Above them the controls and the organisation: the runway inspection required only at listed aerodromes, the rejected take-off training that covers system failures below V1 only, the partially unserviceable cabin floor proximity lighting, the perimeter fence repair deferred for three budget cycles, the evening service approved as a schedule change, and the documented wildlife plan the standard requires only above a size threshold this aerodrome does not meet.

Two of them are up there to be disproved: No. 2 engine power loss during the take-off roll and Take-off commenced with a tailwind above limits. Nothing is filtered at this point. The method works by testing, not by intuition, and a hypothesis that is later excluded still strengthens the record.

What the app recordedEach hypothesis as an untested object, drawn with a dashed white border until someone has tested it.

Test as the evidence arrives, and let the arrows follow

Nine objects have been tested here. The occurrence sequence is joined up, animals to strike to deceleration shortfall to the overrun, and the wildlife chain has started to form above it, from the standard's size threshold down to the deferred fence and the listing criterion. Both hypotheses raised to be disproved have failed Existence and are struck through on the map, with the reason on the record.

An investigation does not arrive in order. A transcript changes what a factor means, a record closes a line of enquiry, a new hypothesis turns up in week three. Objects are moved, tested, connected and reconnected as that happens, and the map is never more than the current state of the evidence.

Every arrow is a claim that one thing led to another. It stays grey and dashed until the object it comes from has passed its tests, and turns green when it has, so the picture builds itself as the work is done.

What the app recordedEvery object and every connection, with the state of each test behind them.

7 Testing a factor

Nothing is a finding until it has passed its tests

Three questions are asked of every object on the map, in order, and a confidence is recorded for each answer. What the object becomes follows from the answers, not from the investigator's view of it.

Existence

Did this event or condition actually exist?

If it did, the border turns solid and the next question is asked. If it did not, the hypothesis is excluded: it stays on the map, struck through, with the reason recorded.

PassFailInconclusive
Influence

Did it influence anything else, and if so in what way?

If it did, it is a contributing safety factor and its outgoing arrows become proven causal paths. Where the evidence cuts both ways, the test is argued with evidence for and against.

PassFailArgued
Importance

If it did not influence anything, is it still important enough to raise in the report?

A factor that played no part in this occurrence may still be a risk worth addressing. If so, it is a finding in its own right, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows.

PassFail

The three questions, on one object

Ask them of the operator's own defence

The object is Runway inspection required only at listed aerodromes, and the listing criterion is a strike. Part A 8.14.2 requires the pilot in command to request a runway inspection before the first departure after last light at any aerodrome in Appendix 8A, and 8.14.3 requires nothing at all anywhere else. An aerodrome joins the appendix when a company aircraft reports a wildlife strike there. Tarlton Springs never has been on it.

Did it exist? Yes, at 100 per cent: the manual text and the appendix listing are documentary. Did it influence the occurrence? Yes, at 90 per cent. Had the requirement applied, an inspection would have been requested and a result required before the roll, and the aerodrome's own staff disperse animals when they find them. The missing 10 per cent allows that an inspection finished some minutes before departure does not guarantee a clear runway at the roll.

The corner dot says how far the object's own investigation has got: amber while it is still being worked, green when the investigator has finished with it. Sixteen of the eighteen objects on this map are green, and two were deliberately left amber.

What the app recordedEach verdict with its confidence and its evidence, the investigator's reasoning, and the standard of proof the investigation set. The form warns when a pass falls short of it.

Argue the one the evidence cuts both ways on

The object above it is Documented wildlife plan required only above a size threshold the aerodrome does not meet. The aerodrome is not in breach of anything, which is the point of putting it in the External Influences lane: its heaviest scheduled aeroplane and its passenger numbers both fall under the standard's limbs, so it needs no documented plan, and it held none.

For: the absent plan is the absent mechanism. It is what would have connected the aerodrome's own sighting log to the airlines using the aerodrome, and what would have put the failed fence on a funded programme instead of an unfunded works register. Against: the operator was still obliged to take reasonable measures, a plan is not the only way to repair a fence or pass on a log, and the deferrals were budget decisions. A plan is paper; the fence is money.

Influence was accepted at 70 per cent, argued. The investigation's standard of proof is the application's default of 66 per cent, so this clears it, but only just, and it is the weakest of the influence findings on the map. The report carries that as its confidence word rather than presenting the finding as settled.

What the app recordedBoth sides of the argument as separate evidence, the confidence the investigator settled on after weighing them, and the reasoning for that number.

8 The finished map

The whole investigation in one image

Eighteen objects and fifteen arrows. Left to right is time, bottom to top is organisational depth, and the critical event sits at the bottom right where every chain arrives.

What the map says

Two chains converge on the deceleration shortfall and through it on the overrun: an organisational one running from the standard's size threshold through the deferred fence and the unarmed inspection requirement to the animals on the runway, and a shorter one from the training syllabus to the reject above V1. A green arrow also runs from the positive event, maximum braking and No. 1 reverse applied without delay, because that changed the outcome as well.

The two orange objects carry no arrows at all. They failed Influence, so there is no proven causal path to draw and an arrow would be a claim the evidence does not support. They stay on the map because they are findings in their own right.

The one unproven arrow

One arrow is grey and dashed: the claim that the runway edge lighting intensity reduced the crew's ability to detect the animals, and so delayed the reject. A photometric check measured four northern edge lights at 118 to 131 per cent of nominal in the section the animals crossed, and the captain described a corridor of light with everything outside it black. That shows a deviation. It does not show an effect on detection.

Existence was left inconclusive at 45 per cent, so the object never reached Influence and its arrow cannot be drawn as proven. The application draws it grey and dashed rather than dropping it, because an open line of enquiry is part of the record and the report has to say that it is open.

  • Blue, solid border: a contributing safety factor. It existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange: an other risk factor. It existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through, dashed: an excluded hypothesis. It did not exist, and the reason is on the record.
  • Green, rounded: a positive event, something that reduced the risk or limited the outcome.
  • Red, rounded: the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White, dashed: not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • Green arrow: a proven causal path. The object it comes from has passed Influence.
  • Grey dashed arrow: a causal path still under testing.
  • Green dot: the object's investigation is complete and its evidence finalised.
  • Amber dot: still being investigated.

10 Drafting, audit and repair

The report is written from the record, then argued with

Drafting is not the end of the work. Every draft is audited against the record that produced it, and what the audit finds is acted on before anything is finalised.

The pipeline

Draft

A style is chosen and its sections are drafted from one collated copy of the whole record: an outline pass, a call per section, then a cohesion edit across the lot. The model is given the record and nothing else, and the findings, the categories and the confidence words all come from the record, so a section cannot claim more than the tests behind it support. Each section is corrected and finalised by hand, and nothing regenerates over a finalised one. This investigation was drafted through a model reached from the investigator's own machine; the endpoint is a setting, so the same pipeline runs against a local model, a hosted API with your own key, or an organisation's gateway. With AI access off the tab says so and the report is assembled from the record instead. The drafts published here were produced by a Claude-based pipeline run through the suite's own sealed command-line transport, standing in for the kind of private cloud endpoint an organisation would use.

What the app recordedEach section with the model and the version of the record that produced it, so a draft goes visibly stale when the record moves on.

Audit, repair, then finalise

An adversarial pass then reads the finished draft back against the whole record and verdicts every claim in it, naming what it could not find and what it found contradicted. A mechanical check with no model in it runs beside it, comparing each factor's category and confidence word in the text against its recorded test, and the report's findings against the derived classification. The repair pass takes only what those two flagged: it sends the section carrying a flagged item, with the record and the cited excerpt, under one rule, change what is flagged and nothing else, then re-audits the reply and keeps it only if the re-audit is clean or strictly better. Nothing lands on its own. A repaired section is shown beside the text it would replace and landed by hand, and it carries "repaired against the audit" in its provenance. The audit and the check are published with the reports below, because what the audit caught is the reason a draft is never final until an investigator has read it.

What the app recordedThe audit and the record check beside the draft, the review notes the model made about the record itself, which section each repair touched, and the investigator's decision to finalise.
