Maritime case study

Kanooka Heads: a passenger ferry grounding at night

A passenger ferry's chart plotter freezes in a dredged channel in full darkness. The vessel eases out of the channel while the master looks for a beacon he cannot see, and grounds on a sandbank with 38 people aboard. This page follows the investigation from the master's own report to the documents it produced: the evidence it gathered, the map it tested, what it established, and what IWS generated from that one record.

Everything in this case study is fictional: the operator, the port, the vessel, the waterway, the people and every record. The screenshots are real screens from the investigation file, and the documents are published exactly as the application produced them, faults and all.

1 What happened

A ferry off the side of a dredged channel, in the dark, on a falling tide

This is what was known on the evening of 07/07/2026 and the morning after, before anybody had pulled a track or opened a manual.

Service TF18 was the last of the day, running from Muntari Island to the town wharf at Kanooka Heads with 38 passengers aboard. It was the first winter of a timetable that had moved that service from 16:15 to 17:35, so the transit of the Merrandi Channel was made in full darkness, 35 minutes after the end of civil twilight, with no moon.

The channel is dredged to 3.2 m and is 40 m wide. The vessel entered it on the charted centreline and held it. Ninety seconds past the No. 2 beacon the bridge chart plotter froze: the chart picture stayed on the screen, the vessel symbol stopped moving, and the display would not take a touch. The master did not stop. He went looking out of the window for the No. 4 starboard-hand beacon, which is the secondary means of position fixing named in the passage plan.

In the same second the heading began to ease five degrees to starboard, most of it while he was tapping at the frozen screen, and on his own account he never decided to make the turn. It took him 91 seconds to find the beacon, and the vessel crossed the channel's western edge 114 seconds after the freeze. When he saw the light it was dead ahead. Thinking he was just outside the edge, when he was nearly 40 m out, he eased her back to port rather than stop. The echo sounder's shallow alarm gave 16 seconds' warning, the master had both engines astern four seconds after it, and the vessel struck Boolarong Bank at 6.8 kt, 54 m outside the channel.

Nobody was seriously hurt. The starboard hull was holed and the vessel took water in one void. The master stopped the engines, refused to back a holed vessel off a bank on a falling tide, got lifejackets onto everyone and had all 38 passengers ashore by workboat within 74 minutes.

His own report, written from the vessel forty minutes later under the company's just culture scheme, was in the safety system before the operator's own notification was raised, and it is what opened this investigation. It put two things on the record before anyone asked. He had been told about the plotter at the handover and had not written it down. And the No. 4 beacon had been getting harder to see for two years, the masters had all said so to each other, and none of them had ever written it down.

2 How it was investigated

The evidence first, then every hypothesis on the map, each one tested

Two safety reports, seventeen items of captured material, three interviews and a timeline went into the one record over the following fortnight, filed as they arrived and before anyone had a theory. The master, the deckhand and the master who had the vessel that morning were interviewed once the records were in hand, so the questions could be about what the records did not say.

What each source settled

  • Safety reports, master and deckhand. The master's (SR-2026-0288, submitted at 18:52 from the vessel while the workboat was still running passengers ashore) opened the investigation. The deckhand's (SR-2026-0291, three days later) was not about the grounding at all: she had to squeeze behind the kiosk counter to reach the forward saloon lifejacket locker. Nobody had asked her, and it became a finding. Both are protected, so they inform the investigation without being quoted into anything distributed.
  • Occurrence notification. The operator's own account: what happened, where, the damage, the injuries, the risk rating and the actions taken that night. It fixed the agreed facts and seeded the first timeline events.
  • Voyage data, AIS track and equipment logs. The shore AIS track second by second, the engine and steering recorder, and the plotter's and echo sounder's own logs. It fixed the moment the vessel left the centreline, showed the set west as a held heading change rather than a current, and closed the steering failure hypothesis: every parameter was normal until the impact.
  • VHF and bridge audio extract. Both VHF channels and the bridge camera audio, from the departure call to the last passenger ashore. It is the evidence behind the plotter freeze, behind the 91 seconds between "Where's four" and "There's four", and behind the decision not to refloat.
  • Tide, weather and light. The tide predictions and the port's own gauge, the weather and the light and moon data. It established the transit in full darkness, and it excluded the tide hypothesis: the gauge was within 0.03 m of prediction at the moment of the grounding.
  • Operator and port procedure extracts. The aids to navigation code, the port services agreement, the passage planning and defect reporting manuals, the route risk assessment and the change record. The external, organisational and risk control lanes rest on these seven pages.
  • Vessel, port and crewing records. The defect book, the post-grounding survey, the lifejacket stowage survey, the crew and roster records, and the Port Authority's aids register with its range and visibility check and the foreshore lighting survey. The screened beacon, the halved range and the three deferred works rounds come from here.
  • Interview transcript, master. Taken once the records were in hand. It turned the beacon from a complaint in a tearoom into a factor that could be tested, and it is where he explains a five degree alteration he says he never decided to make.
  • Interview notes, deckhand and outgoing master. The deckhand's gave the lifejacket finding and the question of who keeps a lookout; the outgoing master's gave the other half of the handover.

All nine documents are in the source pack, so the analysis can be read back against the same material the investigator had.

The map, at three points in the work

The investigator began with the critical event, worked backwards to identify twenty possible events and conditions, then tested each against the available evidence. Two hypotheses, a steering or propulsion failure before the vessel left the centreline and the tide setting down below prediction over Boolarong Bank, were subsequently excluded.

One object, from the map to its table

Each AcciMap object carries its own evidence and assessment. As the investigator tests an event or condition, the reasoning is recorded against that object and carried directly into the Evidence and Argument Tables.

Beacon's range halved by solar conversion with no notice to channel users is the finding everybody reaches for first, and the evidence will not carry it as a cause: it existed, it did not influence the grounding, and it is still important enough to raise. Here it is in the three places it lives.

  1. 1On the map

    Selected on the finished map, in the Organisational Influences lane. It is orange, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows, while the deferred sightline clearing beside it runs straight down to the screened beacon. Its corner dot is green: the investigator has finished with it.

  2. 2Its assessment form

    Existence passed at 100 per cent. Eight months before the occurrence the No. 4 beacon was converted from mains to a solar lantern and its nominal range cut from 6 nautical miles to 3. No local notice went out, no operator was told, and the company's passage plan still gave the beacon at 6 M on the night. Influence was argued and failed at 20 per cent. Importance passed at 85 per cent.

  3. 3Its table

    Page 6 of the Evidence and Argument Tables: the same three tests, each with its argument for, its argument against and its reasoning, generated from the form. Nothing is typed twice.

For Influence: the master called the beacon "a nothing" against the town and said it used to be a proper light, and for at least the last 37 seconds of his search it was clear of the islet and not yet seen, which is when a weaker light against a bright background is harder to pick up. Against: over the half mile in which the vessel left the channel the beacon was screened by the canopy, and a screened light is not made visible by being brighter. The master already knew the light was weaker, so a notice would have told him nothing he did not know, and the search ran from 0.47 to 0.25 nm, well inside even the measured range of 2.6 nm.

Weighed, Influence failed at 20 per cent, well short of the investigation's standard of proof, the application's default of 70 per cent. The one mechanism left, the light's conspicuity against the floodlights once it cleared the islet, belongs to the floodlight object and stays open there. The conversion is still a finding: a change to an aid on a 96-passenger route reached no user of the channel, so the report carries it as an other risk factor rather than a cause.

3 What it established

Ten contributing safety factors, in three converging chains

Twenty-one objects and fourteen arrows: ten contributing safety factors, five other risk factors, two excluded hypotheses and one positive event, with one factor whose Influence is unresolved and one object still open on Existence.

What the map says

Three chains converge. A beacon chain runs from the deferred sightline clearing to a beacon screened by mangroves over most of the stretch where the master was looking for it, and from there to a heading that eased five degrees to starboard without a decision to turn. A defect chain runs from a procedure that never requires a fault that cleared itself to be recorded to a plotter that froze with a known fix nobody had applied, and from the plotter both to the heading change and to the transit continuing at eight knots. A short timetable chain runs from a change record approved one level too low to a night transit with no plan for it, and on to the same continued transit. Through the vessel leaving the channel and the late alarm, all three arrive at the grounding.

The freeze itself is on three independent records (the unit's own log, the bridge audio to the second and the master's account), so its Existence passed at 100 per cent and its Influence at 95. A 46 hour bench test afterwards found no defect and could not make it happen again; the contractor identified the cause code as a known fault with a firmware fix issued in 11/2024, never applied to this unit.

A green arrow also runs from the positive event, the engines stopped with no attempt made to refloat, because that changed the outcome as well. The five orange objects carry no arrows: the aids code, the solar conversion, the omission at the handover, the roster that put a darkness transit at the end of a split duty, and the blocked lifejacket locker. They failed Influence, so an arrow would be a claim the evidence does not support, and they stay on the map because they are findings in their own right.

What stays open

One arrow is grey and dashed: the claim that the unshielded boat ramp floodlights behind the beacon are part of why the held heading went unnoticed. Fourteen unshielded floodlights went in ten days before the winter timetable began, and from half a mile north they sit behind the beacon within four degrees of the same bearing. Both masters interviewed described the same thing in almost the same words, and for at least the last 37 seconds before the master called the beacon it was clear of the islet and not seen. That shows a condition, and a window in which it could have mattered. It does not show an effect on detection.

Over most of the search the beacon was screened and would not have been visible against a black sky either, and the heading change began at the plotter, before the search, so the floodlights cannot have caused the turn. Existence passed at 100 per cent, because the floodlights were measured, but Influence was argued and left inconclusive at 45 per cent, and the report has to say the question is open.

The master's fatigue carries no arrow either. Its Existence was argued and left inconclusive at 50 per cent, and a factor that may not have existed points at nothing.

Where the file is up to

Nineteen of the twenty-one objects are finished (green corner dot) and two were deliberately left amber: the floodlights and the lifejacket locker.

The to-do list holds seven items, five still open: the firmware fix has not been proved to be the fix, nobody has said what it would take to fund the sightline work, the operator has not answered whether a second person belongs on the bridge at night, only two of the five masters on the route have been asked whether the plotter has ever frozen on them, and the master has not yet been asked whether he identified the No. 4 beacon before passing No. 1, as the passage planning manual requires. Two of the seven were raised from an object's own form and link straight back to it.

Twelve checklist items are still answered no when the final investigation report is drafted, because they are honestly no: two objects are not finalised, the preliminary investigation report has not gone out, nobody outside the investigation has read the draft, and the investigation cannot be closed. The deckhand's interview record is deliberately not finalised, because her question about who keeps a lookout is still out with the operator; drafting treats it as a working draft rather than a settled account.

What the colours and shapes mean
  • Blue with a solid border is a contributing safety factor: it existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange is an other risk factor: it existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through and dashed is an excluded hypothesis, with the reason on the record.
  • Green and rounded is a positive event, something that limited the outcome.
  • Red and rounded is the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White and dashed is not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • A green arrow is a proven causal path.
  • A grey dashed arrow is a causal path still under testing.
  • A green dot means the object's investigation is complete; amber means it is still being worked.

How the reports were drafted

Each report was drafted section by section from the record, then audited against it. What the audit flagged went to a repair pass, and a repaired section was landed by hand, never on its own. The model also wrote review notes as it drafted, on contradictions and gaps in the record itself; on this investigation they caught things worth catching, and they are published with the audits. These drafts came from a Claude-based pipeline run through the suite's own sealed command-line transport, standing in for the private endpoint an organisation would use. With AI access off, the report is assembled from the record instead.
