Mining case study

Bindarra: a haul truck and light vehicle collision

A 220 tonne haul truck and a light vehicle meet at a new intersection in an open pit, on the tenth night of a fourteen night swing, and the light vehicle's driver is seriously injured. This page follows the investigation from the hazard report nobody acted on to the documents it produced: the evidence it gathered, the map it tested, what it established, and what Synoptic generated from that one record.

Nothing in the workflow changes between an aerodrome and a gold mine. The lanes are the same, the three tests are the same, and the report comes out of the same record. What changes is the evidence: a fleet management download instead of a flight recorder, a survey pickup instead of a photometric check, a pit channel instead of a tower frequency.

Everything in this case study is fictional: the mine, the operator, the contractors, the regulator, the people and every record. The screenshots are real screens from the investigation file, and the documents are published exactly as the application produced them, faults and all.

1 What happened

Two vehicles at a blind intersection, at ten to three in the morning

This is what was known by dawn on 12/08/2026, before anybody had pulled a download or opened a procedure.

A haul truck was descending the main ramp of the Stage 4 open pit, empty, at about 37 km/h. A light vehicle carrying one grade control technician came out of a side road onto the ramp in front of it. The side road had been opened ten days earlier to reach a new dewatering bore.

The side road meets the ramp 34 m beyond a crest. The truck operator first saw the light vehicle's beacon at about 53 m and the vehicle itself at 41 m, six seconds before the impact. He braked and steered right, towards the wall and away from the open edge, which took the impact from 37 km/h down to 24 km/h and kept the light vehicle from being driven over the edge. The truck's front left struck the light vehicle's right side, pushed it 18 m down the ramp and rolled it onto its left side against the inner windrow.

The driver was conscious throughout. His door was against the ground, so he was taken out through the rear of the vehicle 27 minutes later and flown out with a fractured clavicle, three fractured ribs and a small pneumothorax. His seatbelt was fastened and the webbing carried loading marks; the roll-over structure deformed and was not breached.

The truck operator's own report, written the following afternoon under the site's just culture scheme, put three things on the record before anyone asked him. He did not know the road was open. He made no radio call, and nor does anybody else, because the site's plan does not require one there. And the collision awareness system in his cab had never once gone off down in the pit.

2 How it was investigated

The evidence first, then every hypothesis on the map, each one tested

The first thing filed in this investigation was not written after the collision. It was written a week before it, by somebody else, about the same intersection. Twenty items of captured material, two safety reports, three interviews and an eleven-event timeline followed it into the one record. The two vehicles were left where they stopped and the scene was measured before recovery, so the geometry was fixed before anybody had an opinion about it.

What each source settled

  • Safety reports. HAZ-2026-0917 was raised at ten past ten on a Tuesday night by a dewatering technician who came out of the same side road in front of a descending truck sixty metres away and reversed back in. He described the rise in the road, the windrow, the missing sign and his own conclusion: the trucks do not know the road is there. It was closed two days later by a pre-start reminder to take care and give way, and its rating reduced from Medium to Low on closure. ASR-2026-0341, the truck operator's own account, came in under the just culture scheme, so it informs the investigation without being quoted into anything distributed.
  • Occurrence notification. The first account: what happened, the injuries, the damage, the risk rating and the actions taken that night. It opened the file and seeded the first timeline events.
  • Fleet management and collision awareness download. The truck's own data. It fixed the speed at 37 km/h, the response at 1.4 seconds, the impact at 24 km/h, and brakes performing to specification, which closed the brake failure hypothesis. It also showed the detection system logging the light vehicle at 21 m and saying nothing.
  • Pit channel radio extract. Every transmission from the return from crib to two minutes after the collision. The truck called at each listed intersection it passed, nobody made a call about the side road or a light vehicle in the pit, and the emergency response is timed from the collision onwards.
  • Scene examination and survey record. The marks, the impact position and the roll, then the survey department's pickup of the intersection to the eye height of each vehicle. This is where the 53 m and the 41 m come from, and where the wet surface hypothesis was closed.
  • Site procedures and policy extracts. The statutory guideline, the traffic management plan and its appendix of intersections, the management of change procedure, the works order that cut the road, and the contractor management standard. The external, organisational and risk control lanes rest on these.
  • Equipment, roster, training and site records. The brake tests either side of the collision, the roster and the operator's sleep record, both drivers' training files, the plan's review history, a search of two years of hazard reports, twelve months of collision awareness statistics, and the emergency response log.
  • Interview transcript, haul truck operator. Taken once the download was in hand. It turned the alert geofence from a system configuration detail into a factor that could be tested.
  • Interview notes, night shift supervisor. Taken by video call on his days off and not recorded. The most useful account for the organisational picture: he did not know a light vehicle was in his pit, and there is no process by which he could have.
  • Interview notes, light vehicle driver. Not recorded, at his request, and read back to him at the end. He corrected himself twice, including on the point least helpful to him, and his description of the sight line matches the survey to within a few metres.

All ten documents are in the source pack, so the analysis can be read back against the same material the investigator had.

The map, at three points in the work

The investigator began with the critical event, worked backwards to identify nineteen possible events and conditions, then tested each against the available evidence. Two hypotheses, a wet or slippery ramp surface and service brakes delivering less than their tested deceleration, were subsequently excluded.

One object, from the map to its table

Each AcciMap object carries its own evidence and assessment. As the investigator tests an event or condition, the reasoning is recorded against that object and carried directly into the Evidence and Argument Tables.

Haul operators rostered for fourteen consecutive 12.5 hour night shifts is the factor that reads like the cause and is not: it existed, it did not influence the collision, and it is still important enough to raise. Here it is in the three places it lives.

  1. 1On the map

    Selected on the finished map, in the Organisational Influences lane. It is orange, an other risk factor, and it carries no arrows, while the access card object beside it runs down to the light vehicle driving out without stopping. Its corner dot is green: the investigator has finished with it.

  2. 2Its assessment form

    Existence passed at 100 per cent: the roster is 14 nights on and 7 off, 18:00 to 06:30, inside the contractor's fatigue standard, and the operator was on night 10 of 14, 8 hours 47 minutes into the shift. Influence was argued and failed at 25 per cent. Importance passed at 85 per cent.

  3. 3Its table

    Page 8 of the Evidence and Argument Tables: the same three tests, each with its argument for, its argument against and its reasoning, generated from the form. Nothing is typed twice.

Night ten of fourteen, in the ninth hour of a shift, at ten to three in the morning is exactly where a reader expects to find the answer, and the evidence will not carry it. The download gives a 1.4 second response, inside the 1.5 seconds the plan's own stopping distance calculation allows for, and the braking and steering that followed were correct and immediate. The arithmetic settles it: braking at the recorded 0.78 m/s2 from 53 m, a response time of zero still ends in a collision, at about 17 km/h instead of 24.

It goes into the report anyway, because the roster puts every haul truck operator at the wheel on a tenth, twelfth and fourteenth consecutive night on every swing, and at an intersection with a longer sight line than RL 312 a slower response is the difference between stopping and not. The operator's own state on the night is a separate object, Haul truck operator fatigued during the approach to the intersection: its Existence was argued and left inconclusive at 50 per cent, so it carries no arrow either.

3 What it established

Twelve contributing safety factors, one in every lane

Twenty objects and fifteen arrows, with a contributing safety factor in every one of the six lanes: twelve contributing safety factors, two other risk factors, two excluded hypotheses and one positive event, with one factor still under testing and one object left inconclusive on Existence.

What the map says

Every chain converges on the two drivers being unaware of each other, and through that on the injury. A listing chain runs from the guideline's missing review trigger through the call requirement that could not reach the new road to the call nobody made. A works order chain runs from the spur opened with nothing referred to anyone, to the missing stop control and on to the light vehicle driving out without stopping, and to the collision alerts suppressed at the spur. A shorter induction chain runs to the same unstopped entry, and the 41 m sight line runs straight to the convergence. A green arrow also runs from the positive event, the truck operator braking and steering right, because that changed the outcome as well.

The sight line is the measurement the map rests on. The survey puts the spur mouth 34 m below the grade break the crews call the crest: a descending operator first sees the top of a light vehicle's beacon at 53 m and the mouth at 41 m, and a driver at the mouth first sees a descending truck at 38 m. An empty truck at the posted 40 km/h on that grade needs 96 m to stop, and the plan's own rule requires the sight distance to be at least that, and never less than 120 m. Existence passed at 100 per cent, surveyed and described independently by the hazard report seven days earlier. Influence passed at 90 per cent: with twice the sight line, the same response and the same braking stop the truck short of the spur, and the missing 10 per cent allows that a sight line is only fatal once something is in it.

The two orange objects, the night roster and a night shift extrication that waits on a second responder called from the village, carry no arrows. They failed Influence, so an arrow would be a claim the evidence does not support, and they stay on the map because they are findings in their own right.

What stays open

One arrow is grey and dashed: the claim that airborne dust on the ramp delayed the truck operator's sighting of the light vehicle. Both of its tests were argued. For Existence, the water cart's pass was seven minutes overdue and the dust monitor on the bench above the grade break read 142 micrograms per cubic metre against a shift mean of 88. Against, 142 is well below the site trigger of 250, a monitor head is not a cab, and the light vehicle driver was firm that the air was clear. Existence passed at 70 per cent, because the driver's clear air was the spur below the windrow, not the ramp above the crest where the truck was.

That shows a deviation. It does not show an effect on anybody's vision: the beacon was in view from 53 m, the first point at which the survey says it could be seen at all, and the operator described dust hanging and declined to say it made a difference. What stops the test failing outright is that the sighting is inferred from the download's last steady data point and the survey, not recorded. Influence was left inconclusive at 40 per cent, below the 70 per cent standard of proof, and the report has to say the question is open.

Where the file is up to

Eighteen of the twenty objects are finished (green corner dot) and two were deliberately left amber: the dust and the night shift extrication.

Six to-dos came off the work and four are still open, which is how an investigation actually looks at the point this file was captured. Two were raised from inside an object's form and link back to it: who was told the alert geofence had moved, and whether the site induction supplement was ever issued to the injured driver.

Two of the three interview records are finalised. The injured driver's is deliberately left open, because two questions are still outstanding with him and a second conversation is planned once he is further along. On the checklist the camera coverage item is marked not applicable, because the truck's cameras are live view and record nothing.

What the colours and shapes mean
  • Blue with a solid border is a contributing safety factor: it existed and it influenced the occurrence.
  • Orange is an other risk factor: it existed, it did not influence this occurrence, and it is still worth raising.
  • Struck through and dashed is an excluded hypothesis, with the reason on the record.
  • Green and rounded is a positive event, something that limited the outcome.
  • Red and rounded is the critical event, the occurrence itself.
  • White and dashed is not yet tested, or a test left inconclusive.
  • A green arrow is a proven causal path.
  • A grey dashed arrow is a causal path still under testing.
  • A green dot means the object's investigation is complete; amber means it is still being worked.

How the reports were drafted

Each report was drafted section by section from the record, then audited against it. What the audit flagged went to a repair pass, and a repaired section was landed by hand, never on its own. On this investigation the audit and the mechanical record check caught different things. On the final investigation report the audit verdicted 210 claims and flagged three, each a safety action the draft reports that it could not find in the collated record. The record check caught the confidence words: a findings bullet on the guideline factor with no probability term at all, a findings bullet stating the convergence as "very likely" where the analysis argues "virtually certain", and the inconclusive fatigue object with no sentence in the analysis saying it is unresolved.

These drafts came from a Claude-based pipeline run through the suite's own sealed command-line transport, standing in for the private endpoint an organisation would use. With AI access off, the report is assembled from the record instead.
